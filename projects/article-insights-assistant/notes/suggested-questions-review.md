# Entry card questions: the hand review

> Task 013, 2026-09-26. Generated by `scripts/generate-suggested-questions.ts`
> in 24 warm calls for about $0.80, then reviewed line by line. This note
> records what was changed and why, because these are reader-facing sentences
> and a generated sentence nobody read is a sentence nobody wrote.

## How they are produced

Generated once, reviewed, and committed to
`content/insights/suggested-questions.json`. The corpus build reads that file
and attaches the questions to each article, so a reader who opens the console
causes no model call before they have asked anything.

Three shapes, one question each, in this order:

1. **The decision.** A choice the reader faces that the article takes a position
   on, with the circumstance named: no legal operations function, spend data in
   four systems, 200 open matters and no process owner, a senior litigator
   retiring in six months.
2. **The objection.** A push back on the argument, or on the way vendors and
   analysts frame the same thing. A reader who disagrees is the reader worth
   answering.
3. **The rest of the library.** A question that has to cross into another
   article, which is what the whole corpus in context buys.

Summarize is a fourth option, added in code rather than in the data, and it is
never first. An entry card that leads with summarize teaches readers not to read
the article, which would undo the reason the articles are long.

## What the review changed

**Fifteen of the seventy-two questions were rewritten.** The generator's first
pass was stronger than expected on specificity and weaker on variety.

**One false premise.** The ontology article's third question asked how the
ontology relates to "the report cards described in the $20B Blind Spot article".
That article contains no report cards; the phrase belongs to three others. A
question carrying a false premise invites an answer that either corrects the
reader or, worse, plays along. Replaced with a question about whether curated
context needs the ontology first, which the knowledge management article does
argue.

**One grammatical error.** "just a excuse" in the mandate article.

**Template monotony in the objection slot.** Twenty of twenty-four objections
opened with "Isn't X just Y", and four of those used "dressed up as". Each
article shows only its own three questions, so no reader sees the repetition on
one page, but it is still one shape doing all the work. Ten were rewritten into
other forms of push back: what happens if we move off Microsoft, what our
security team still has to review, what changes the general counsel's mind, why
guardrails are not enough. Twelve "Isn't" questions remain, kept where the
objection genuinely is a claim of rebranding.

**Two cross-article questions leaned on position rather than substance.** "the
next article" and "the later series article" break as soon as the publication
order changes. Both now name the subject instead.

**One hyphen.** "third party IdP" to "third-party IdP", matching the article.

## What was verified rather than trusted

Every technical term and cross-reference the questions assert was checked
against the corpus text: Foundry IQ, Exhibit 1, CLOC Core 12, the five modules,
"skip to inference", the front door, operational memory, the Agent Framework,
SharePoint Embedded, Power Automate, third-party identity provider federation.
All present except the report cards described above.

`scripts/check-insights-prompt.ts --offline` now checks the committed file on
every run: three questions per article, the copy rules, no question repeated
across articles, and summarize last. A regeneration that produces weak copy
fails there rather than reaching a reader.

## For the owner

Two things are worth a decision, and neither of them blocks the work.

**Two published titles contain em dashes**, the feature reference for the
platform and the article on legal AI not being deterministic. House voice bans
the mark and both titles predate the rule. The questions avoid quoting either
title, but the assistant will reproduce a title whenever it cites the article,
so the mark will appear in front of readers until the titles change.

**The questions name competitor products in the reader's voice**, specifically
ChatGPT and Copilot, both of which the articles themselves name. The assistant
is told to make no claim about a named competitor beyond what an article states,
so the question invites an answer the prompt already constrains. Worth reading
one of those answers before launch.
