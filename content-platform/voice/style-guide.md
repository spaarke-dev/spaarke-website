# Style guide

Spaarke writes for general counsel, legal operations directors, and CIOs who have heard the AI pitch a hundred times. They read McKinsey Quarterly and Harvard Business Review, and they measure vendor writing against that standard. They discount any sentence that could have come from a sales deck, and they trust prose that earns its claims.

This is the rulebook for how every piece sounds. Read it at the start of every writing session, together with its four companion files:

- `examples/consulting-register.md` is the positive model. It describes the register, quotes the publications we are measured against, and gives model passages.
- `examples/ai-tells.md` is the removal guide. It lists the constructions that readers identify as machine-written, with a before and an after for each.
- `stance.md` is the posture. It says where the writer stands: toward the evidence, the causes, the reader, and the other parties. On sentence mechanics this guide governs; on posture that file governs.
- `examples/house-exemplar.md` is the exemplar. It names the one published article the writer has approved as house voice and annotates the eight traits that carry it. The rules in this guide are stated as removals, and a draft can satisfy every one of them and still have no speaker in it; read the exemplar to know what to reproduce.

**Why this guide was revised.** In September 2026 readers told us that the published articles sounded as though an AI had written them. A local audit traced the cause to this guide and its example files, which had endorsed em dashes, negation followed by correction, verbless fragments, short symmetrical closers, one-sentence paragraphs, and bold lead-in bullets. This revision reverses those instructions. Where an older voice document, a template, or a published article conflicts with this guide, this guide governs.

## 1. Voice: what we sound like

The target register is strategy-consulting long-form: the register of McKinsey Quarterly and Harvard Business Review, and of the better work from BCG, Bain, Deloitte Insights, and MIT Sloan Management Review. The writer is an informed colleague who explains a considered position to a peer. The stance remains operator to operator, which means that we write as people who build and run systems, for people who run legal departments. The writer is neither a vendor making a pitch nor an analyst delivering a lecture.

The register is business-like without being stiff. It uses complete sentences, states a clear claim, gives the evidence, and explains the reasoning. It differs from marketing copy because every claim is sized by its evidence. It differs from academic prose because the conclusion comes first and the reader is told what to do with it. Six qualities define the voice, and each is shown here with a sentence we would write and a sentence we would not.

> **Confident.** We take a position and state it before the support. Confidence comes from the evidence that follows the claim, and adjectives cannot supply it.
> We say: "At companies with revenue above $20 billion, median total legal spend rose from $50.8 million to $80 million in a single year (ACC and Major, Lindsey & Africa, Law Department Management Benchmarking Report, 2024). An increase of 57% in one year is large enough to draw the attention of any CFO."
> We do not say: "AI is completely transforming legal operations as we know it!"

> **Measured.** We say how far the evidence goes in the same sentence that says what it supports. A limitation is a subordinate clause hinged on "but", and the main clause carries the assertion. A claim takes at most one hedge, placed on the verb ("suggests", "tends to"), and the strongest objection is conceded once and then answered.
> We say: "Among in-house lawyers who report high stress, 24% say they plan to leave within the next year (ACC, The State of Stress Among In-house Legal Professionals, December 2025). That measures intent rather than departures, but a quarter of a stressed population planning to leave is a staffing risk a general counsel can act on now."
> We do not say: "The figure measures intent, so it signals turnover risk and should not be read as a departure rate." The sentence ends on what the number cannot do, and the reader is left holding the limitation instead of the finding.

> **Specific.** Names, dates, counts, durations, and before-and-after figures do the work that adjectives cannot do, and each one has to change something for the reader. A date earns its place when it changes how a finding should be weighed: a survey's field period, a product release, the fiscal year attached to a market size. A revision date on a reference document does not. Where the point is that a document is out of date, a verb says it better than arithmetic: "The CLOC Core 12 and the ACC Legal Operations Maturity Model, the profession's shared frameworks for the function, predate the impact AI is having on legal operating models." A count of how many times a word appears inside a source document is not specificity; state the qualitative finding instead ("AI itself appears rarely, and only at the most advanced stage of maturity").
> We say: "When she retired, her replacement inherited the case files but not the playbook. Settlements that she would have pushed harder on were accepted too early."
> We do not say: "Knowledge loss has a major impact on operational outcomes."

> **Plainspoken.** We use plain verbs, concrete nouns, and the field's own terms, and we keep one term for one thing.
> We say: "A signed contract records the final terms. The negotiation history (what was conceded, which arguments worked, and what the fallback position was) usually stays with the negotiator, and the next negotiator starts without it."
> We do not say: "Our solution leverages enterprise-grade memory architecture to drive transformative collaboration outcomes."

> **Collegial.** The writer stands beside the reader and looks at the problem with them. We credit other thinkers by name and build on the frameworks the field already uses.
> We say: "CLOC's Core 12 already defines the functions a mature department performs. We build on that framework here and examine which of those functions depend on the same matter data."
> We do not say: "The future of legal is here, and it is more powerful than ever before."

> **Direct about the reader's own function.** We say the uncomfortable thing about the reader's department in short plain words, in its own clause, before we describe the remedy. A claim of this kind is a judgment from experience and it takes no citation (section 5, rule 12; `stance.md`, rule 4).
> We say: "Opening the model also addresses a harder acknowledgment: legal can become an impediment."
> We do not say: "The purpose of the guardrails, the thresholds, the playbooks, the automated and AI-assisted workflows, and the escalation paths is to take legal out of the bottleneck wherever technology can manage the risk."

Pronouns follow from the stance, and the house uses "we" in two ways.

The first is the citing "we", the one we use to point at our own earlier articles, and it is the one that appears most often. When a piece links to another Spaarke article, Spaarke is the actor and the sentence says what we did there: "In our earlier article, [The $20B Blind Spot](/why-spaarke/the-20b-blind-spot), we described what a department gives up when it cannot answer the second of those questions." The frame is "In our earlier article" or "In our previous article", and the verbs are "we discussed", "we explained", "we described", and "we set out". Most links to a Spaarke article carry that construction, and a long-form article in a series normally runs five or six of them.

The purpose of the preference is that a reader can tell our body of work from the field's research, so the test is whether the sentence marks the library as ours. Three further forms pass that test and are house form. The possessive, with the article as the grammatical subject: "Our earlier article, [From Reactive to Predictive](/why-spaarke/loi-maturity-model), maps the frameworks against the maturity question in detail." Spaarke in the third person, where the sentence is about the company's position rather than about a piece of writing: "Spaarke set out the category-level argument that the series continues in [What Is Legal Operations Intelligence?](/why-spaarke/what-is-legal-operations-intelligence)." And a bare link, where the subject is the problem rather than our treatment of it: "an old problem, described in [Institutional Knowledge Is Walking Out the Door](/why-spaarke/institutional-knowledge)." What fails the test is "[title] describes X" or "X is described in an earlier article" used as the standing form for every cross-link, which makes our own library read like third-party citation and leaves the reader unable to tell which sources are ours and which are the field's.

The second is the arguing "we", which states a position as ours: "we recommend", "we found", "in our view", and "in our experience" where the experience is real. These are discussion pieces, so stating a view in the first person is appropriate wherever we are giving a judgment rather than reporting a finding. It does the opposite job from the citing "we" and is not a substitute for it.

Describe readers by role in analysis ("general counsel", "legal operations directors"). Use "you" for direct advice, checklists, and diagnostic questions. The announcement register remains out of bounds (section 5, rule 8).

If a sentence could appear on a CMO's slide, it probably does not belong in our writing.

## 2. Sentences

Write complete sentences. Each sentence has a subject and a finite verb, makes one point, and connects logically to its neighbors.

Aim for an average of roughly 15 to 25 words across a piece, and let length follow content. A definition or a finding may need eight words; a qualified claim may need 35. A sentence that runs past about 40 words usually holds two ideas and should be divided.

Short declarative sentences are welcome when they carry content, such as a topic sentence, a finding, or a definition. They are never used for drama. If a short sentence exists only to land the sentence before it, merge the two or delete it. Earlier versions of this guide praised "a long claim, a short landing" and quoted two closers from the library as models. That advice is withdrawn, because readers identify the pattern as machine-written.

Verbless fragments are not used. `Not storage.`, `Faster.`, and `No audit trail.` each need a subject and a verb, or they belong inside the sentence they comment on.

Claims are stated positively. Say what a thing is and why, and do not introduce a view that nobody holds in order to correct it (section 5, rule 14).

Subordinate clauses, semicolons, and conjunctions that name the logic ("because", "although", "whereas", "so") are the normal equipment of business prose, and one well-built compound sentence is preferable to three clipped ones. The earlier instructions to split any sentence that needs "scaffolding" and to cut whatever cannot be read aloud in one breath are withdrawn.

Prefer the active voice. The test is whether the sentence has an actor who matters. Where a person, a team, or a system does the thing, name them and make them the subject. "The department decides which work the business may complete on its own" is better than "A decision is made about which work the business may complete on its own", because the second hides the party the sentence is about. Naming an actor is not the same as assigning authority, and the two errors have opposite costs. Before writing "owns" or "decides", check the authority boundary in `stance.md`, section 2: a legal risk decision belongs to the department or the general counsel, and legal operations takes the enabling verb. Active constructions also tend to be shorter and to force a claim into the open: a writer who has to name the actor finds out whether they know who it is.

The passive is correct, and sometimes better, in three cases, and none of them needs rewriting. The actor is unknown or beside the point ("The report was published in March 2026"). The object is the topic of the passage, and promoting the actor would displace it ("Privilege travels with the entity, and it applies wherever the document has been copied"). Or the sentence reports a state of affairs rather than an act ("An outcome records what was decided and what followed from it").

An agentless passive is not acceptable in a sentence about responsibility. Our subject matter is who decides, who owns, and who is accountable, so a sentence saying that work "is prioritized", that a standard "was agreed", or that a duty "has been assigned", without saying by whom, withholds the content the reader came for. Where the actor is genuinely unsettled, write that ("no framework names an owner for the practice") instead of concealing it in the grammar. The converse error is to satisfy this rule by giving the sentence the nearest available actor. A sentence that needs a subject and reaches for legal operations, when the right holder is the department, has removed a passive and introduced an overstatement (`stance.md`, section 2).

Roughly 15% of sentences carrying a passive construction is a reasonable ceiling for an article, and `npm run voice:lint` reports the figure. A higher share is a reason to look rather than a failure in itself, since a piece that reports a body of research legitimately carries more.

A few smaller rules complete the section:

- A sentence may open with "But" or "Yet" when the word carries a real turn in the argument, as it does in McKinsey and Harvard Business Review prose. Use the device sparingly: never in consecutive sentences, never to launch a clipped dramatic line, and never "And" as a landing, meaning a final sentence whose opening "And" supplies emphasis the content has not earned. An "And" that opens the last item in a run of parallel sentences is a list continuation rather than a landing, and it is correct: the run of imperative first moves that closes an article ends that way (section 3), as does a run of sentences enumerating the parts of something. A piece that replaces every such opening with "However," or "In addition," becomes stiff, which is a template of its own.
- Common contractions ("it's", "don't", "won't") are acceptable in articles when used sparingly, because the register is business-like and not stiff. Do not use them in white papers or in passages that report research findings. LinkedIn posts and tweets may use them freely.
- Use parallel structure and lists of three only when the content has that many parts.
- Use one term for one thing. Lawyers read a change of term as a change of meaning.

## 3. Paragraphs, sections, and the shape of a piece

A paragraph makes one point. It opens with a topic sentence that states the point, develops it, and supports it with evidence: a named source, a number, a case, or a reasoned comparison. Most paragraphs run 50 to 110 words and three to six sentences, and a long-form article carrying dense evidence sits at the upper end of that range. Length should vary with the argument, since uniform paragraphs are themselves a mark of template writing.

The following paragraph shows the pattern. Its statistic comes from `research-sources.md` and should be verified again before reuse.

> Most legal departments already know that their data is the constraint. In the 2025 EY Law General Counsel Study, which surveyed 1,000 general counsel and chief legal officers at companies with revenue above $1 billion, 52% of respondents reported disorganized data and 75% said they were developing or refining their technology and data strategies. The second figure matters more than the first, because it suggests that three departments in four have moved from recognizing the problem to planning a response. The open question is what those strategies should fix first.

Evidence supports a claim, not a paragraph. A claim resting on four figures takes one evidence paragraph holding all four, followed by a paragraph that says what they mean and carries no statistics at all. That break is what tells the reader which paragraph is the argument. A piece in which every paragraph contains a number reads as a literature review however good the numbers are. In a long-form article, every run of statistic-bearing paragraphs ends in an argument paragraph that carries no statistics. The run may be several paragraphs long where the figures build one case, as an evidence-heavy section legitimately does. What fails is a section, and then a piece, in which the break never comes.

Give a section to a relationship and the mechanism that changes it, not to each actor in turn. Where two sections would carry the same pair of actors, merge them and name the merged section for the mechanism.

A section that runs more than twice the budget of the median section of the piece is usually holding an inventory, and the inventory belongs distributed into the sections that need it. The plan sets the per-section budget; there is no house constant, and no reviewer asks for a long-form article to be cut to a number (`content-types/blog-post.md`, section 8).

Connectives are welcome when they carry logic. "However", "because", "as a result", "for example", "by contrast", and "therefore" tell the reader how one sentence bears on the next, and this register depends on them. The earlier instruction to cut transition words is withdrawn. What remains unwelcome is additive filler (`moreover`, `furthermore`, `additionally`, `it is worth noting that`), which joins sentences without stating a relation between them.

One-sentence paragraphs are not used for effect. The only one-sentence paragraphs in an article are functional: a lead-in to a list, a block quotation, or an exhibit. A sentence that seems to deserve its own paragraph belongs at the start or the end of a neighboring paragraph.

A paragraph ends when its evidence or reasoning is complete. It does not need a closing line that restates the point in compressed form, and a piece in which every paragraph ends on such a line reads as formula. Where a paragraph does close on a sentence of its own, that sentence says what the evidence means for the reader's department, not what it reveals about the market.

Evidence is attributed and interpreted. Attribute a statistic in the sentence that carries it, on first use: the source, the instrument, the year, and the sample. The sample is its size and its population ("657 in-house professionals in 30 countries", "135 law departments with a median revenue of $13 billion"), and it is kept where the figure carries an argument. An opening may name the source and the instrument alone, so that the claim comes first, and bring the sample in at the section that rests on it; that is weighting the apparatus to where it does work, not omitting it. A descriptor whose only function is to limit how far the finding generalises ("mostly North American", "an ACC member panel") is cut, unless the article's argument turns on that limit. The test is the difference between provenance and a discount instruction: provenance tells the reader where a number came from, a discount instruction tells the reader how much to believe it, and deciding how much to believe it is our job before the sentence is written, not the reader's after.

On every later use, refer to the source by short name and do not repeat the sample, the date, or the figure: "Harbor's verdict", "the Thomson Reuters index", "the Law360 Pulse report on legal engineers".

Report survey results as what respondents said. Interpret at the level of the claim rather than the figure, so that a claim resting on four figures takes one interpretation and not four. Where a figure needs a limitation, put the limitation in a subordinate clause and give the main clause to what the figure supports: "These are statements of intent rather than hiring records, but they show a clear plan to hold the size of the department and change the work its people do." Never write a second sentence whose only job is to retract part of the first, and never give a data limitation its own paragraph. A separate sentence of interpretation is warranted at most twice in an article, for the two figures the argument actually rests on.

Cut any caveat that defends the writer rather than informing the reader: an undisclosed sample size on a figure the argument does not rest on, a conflict-of-interest note on a vendor source whose direction and not whose size we are using, and a disclaimer that an illustrative scenario is illustrative. Never write a sentence about a statistic the research could not find; where a gap in the evidence bears on the reader's decision, give it one clause ("Adoption is visible but not yet well measured") and move on.

Two or three full limitations in a long article are a sign of care. Eight are a sign of a writer who distrusts their own evidence.

Openings state the claim first. The first sentence of a long-form article is a declarative statement of the change the article is about, in our own voice and with no source attached, and the second names the single force causing it. Evidence follows the claim and never precedes it: the first attributed statistic comes no earlier than the third sentence. A statistic that only establishes that the subject exists, such as how many departments have a legal operations role, is not a hook; it belongs in the third paragraph, where it credentials the article's subject for the claim already made.

The house shape is four beats, which usually occupy three or four paragraphs. The claim and its cause. The stakes, raised by at most two secondary shifts and closing on the one recurring decision the article is about, stated by listing its actual options. The credential, with one cross-link to our library. And one sentence naming what the article sets out to show. The last two beats commonly share a paragraph, because a single sentence given its own paragraph is barred by rule 17.

The situation, complication, answer sequence remains available for a piece that has to overturn a position the reader currently holds, because there the situation is the position being overturned. It is not the default. The thesis still arrives within the first 150 words for a short post and the first 250 words for a long-form article, and never later than the third paragraph. The 250-word figure is an outer bound and not a target.

A new term is introduced after the problem it names has been described, so that the term arrives as a label for something the reader already recognizes; the thesis does not wait for the term. The thesis is a direct declarative sentence. A framing phrase such as "we argue that" or "our position is that" is used only where the position is one a reader might reasonably contest, and never for an observation about a change already under way in the field; most Spaarke pieces describe such a change, so most theses carry no framing phrase (writer decision, 2026-09-22; `voice/examples/consulting-register.md` sections 6.1 and 6.2 show both cases).

The thesis is a formula, not a sentence written once. Choose its words and repeat them almost unchanged at three anchors: the opening, the heading of the section that proves the claim, and the close. Lawyers read a change of term as a change of meaning (section 2), and that applies to the thesis before it applies to anything else.

Sections connect to each other. Open a section with a sentence or two that link it to what the previous section established. Do not announce a section (`In this section we will explore`) and do not recap it at its end. An article may refer to itself once, at the end of the opening, to say what it sets out to establish: "This article sets out how these new forces are driving an expanded mandate for legal operations." It never refers to itself to say what it will not cover, what it does not rely on, or which of its figures are illustrative. Those are decisions for the brief and the plan, where the reviewer checks them. A disclaimer of scope in the body puts the author on the page only to step back from the argument.

Closings run in four beats, under a heading that reuses the key term from the title.

1. One short synthesis paragraph naming the changes the article described, and nothing else. This is the only place in a piece where a synthesis paragraph is permitted, and it is there to set up the instructions rather than to stand in for them.
2. The thesis, in the words the opening used, followed by the sentence that discharges the title. Where the title names something (a mandate, a blind spot, a gap), one plain sentence says what it is and what it changes: "That is the new mandate. It moves legal operations from administering the department to managing how the department delivers value to the business."
3. One paragraph naming the reader's first move. Two forms are house, and the article chooses. A run of bare imperatives, one per decision the article has argued for, is the fuller form and suits a piece that has argued for several. Naming a single first step declaratively is the other, and it suits a piece whose argument converges on one choice: "The practical first step is to choose which decision to model first. The strongest candidates are the decisions the department makes most often and records least well." What is required is that the reader leaves knowing where to start; the grammar is not: "For legal operations leaders, practical starting points include the following. Understand the department's demand at the front door. Define the guardrails for the low-risk work the business can handle, beginning with non-disclosure agreements and routine contracts." Five is a workable number.
4. Where the piece sits in a series, one or two sentences handing off to the next piece; otherwise the question the evidence leaves open.

What is forbidden is a closing section that re-argues the piece in fresh words. Naming each change in a clause is orientation, and the reader who skipped a section needs it. A close that leaves a senior reader holding a risk and no first move has not finished the job, and an article that never issues an imperative has analysed a problem for a reader it never addressed. The close carries no "Conclusion" heading and does not pitch (section 5, rules 4 and 25).

## 4. Formatting conventions

This section is reference material, so it uses labeled entries. The em dash rule is long enough to need its own subsection, which follows the list.

- **Titles**: title case, as in the published library. A title makes a statement by default. A question is permitted only under section 5, rule 2. Titles never contain an em dash.
- **Headings**: sentence case, no periods, typically 4 to 12 words. A heading renders a verdict: what something is, needs, depends on, or is having done to it. "Knowledge curation is the foundation AI depends on", "Legal operations frameworks are due for a fundamental rethink", "The economics of outside counsel are being reset", "AI is redefining the department's people and roles". A heading that reports a movement without judging it does not qualify, even though it states a point: "Knowledge curation is settling on legal operations" describes what is happening and leaves the reader to decide what it means, and "Knowledge curation is the foundation AI depends on" hands them the conclusion. Prefer the second. An imperative is permitted where the section is the move itself ("Close the loop from insight to action"), and a noun or gerund phrase is permitted where it names something the reader can act on ("The opportunity to open the legal service delivery model", "Managing ahead of events, not only in response to them"). A topic label ("Intake", "Background", "Overview") or a template (`What it is`, `Why it matters`, `How to get started`) does not qualify. The heading of the section that proves the thesis repeats the thesis formula, and the heading of the close reuses the title's key term. The test is that a reader who reads only the headings collects the article's judgments and could disagree with them. Question headings fall under section 5, rule 2.
- **Numbers**: numerals for 10 and above ("12 attorneys", "200 similar matters"); spelled out below 10 ("three workstreams") except in lists or technical contexts. Always numerals for money and percentages, with the % sign. A number that opens a sentence is spelled out, or the sentence is recast.
- **Ranges**: written with the word "to": "1,000 to 1,800 words", "20% to 25%", "from 2024 to 2026". Ranges do not use a dash of any kind.
- **Em dashes**: never. See the subsection that follows this list.
- **Semicolons**: acceptable. Use one between two closely related independent clauses, or between items in a series when the items contain commas.
- **Colons**: introduce a list, a definition, or a specification after a complete clause. A colon does not stage a reveal (section 5, rule 20).
- **Parentheses**: use for definitions, abbreviations on first use, the exact figure behind a rounded one, exhibit references, and short factual asides.
- **Hyphens**: correct inside compound words ("fixed-fee", "e-billing", "matter-level"). A hyphen with a space on each side is a dash substitute and is not used.
- **Lists**: bullets for parallel items that a reader will scan; numbered lists when order matters or the prose refers back ("the three decisions"). Long-form argument belongs in paragraphs under message-bearing subheadings. A passage that enumerates parallel items with ordinals ("First... Second... Third...") is a list written as prose, and it should be a list. Convert it to bullets with a bold label per item and cut each item to its claim in one or two sentences; the supporting citation detail usually goes with the conversion, because the label carries what the reader needs. Keep ordinal prose only where each item depends causally on the one before it, so that the order is an argument rather than an inventory. Where genuinely parallel items each need a full paragraph, give every one a paragraph and put a bold label on each, worded the same way across the set.
- **Bold lead-in bullets**: the house pattern for a parallel enumeration. The test is the items rather than the preamble: each item carries its own claim and its own evidence or worked example, and each could be defended on its own (section 5, rule 26). Four kinds of passage meet it. The article has previewed a counted framework, and each item names one part in the article's own vocabulary. The list is diagnostic, and each item is labelled with the signal or decision it tests; a diagnostic list is reference material even in the middle of a long-form piece, because the reader returns to it rather than reading through it. The enumeration arrives without a count, its lead-in says what the items have in common ("The differences are most visible where practice has moved fastest:"), and every item still carries a claim of its own. Or the passage is reference material proper: a glossary, a specification, a step list, or a rulebook such as this one. A set of labels whose items make sense only when read together fails the test, and it belongs in a paragraph.

  Two label grammars are house form, and the choice follows what the label has to do. Write "Name: what it does" where the name alone would not tell a reader what the item is, which is the usual case for a framework of capabilities or functions ("**Sourcing: choosing external providers and revisiting those choices.**"). Write the bare noun where the name is already the thing the item is about and the sentence beneath it does the defining, which is the usual case for a diagnostic list and for items named after the field's own terms ("**Risk level.**", "**Cost.**", "**Knowledge management.**"). Word the labels the same way across a set; mixing the two grammars inside one list is what makes a list hard to scan, and either grammar used consistently reads cleanly.

  Do not borrow a framework's category labels as the article's own organising vocabulary where the article has argued that the framework is out of date, because adopting its structure concedes the point the argument is making. Naming those categories in order to critique them is a different act and is correct: a list of the places a framework falls short is labelled with the framework's own terms, since those are the terms the reader will check it against.

  There is no fixed limit on how many such lists a long-form article carries. The writer's calibration of 2026-09-23 is that the lists are permitted and reviewed at use, so the count is a thing to look at rather than a rule to pass: an article whose exposition has become a run of labelled lists has lost the connected reasoning the paragraphs were doing, and the approved exemplar runs three across nine sections. The linter reports the count and does not judge it. What the ceiling exists to stop is the pattern becoming the article's default form of exposition. The 2026 audit counted 303 instances in the old library and read the count as the fault; the fault was the unsupported claims under the labels.

- **Bold inside prose**: rare. Reserve it for scannable terms or named concepts on first use. No more than three per 1,000 words of body prose.
- **Italics**: technical terms on first introduction, or single-word emphasis ("It is *plausible* bad output").
- **Exhibits**: refer to an exhibit in parentheses at the end of the sentence that states the finding, in the form "(Exhibit 1)". The exhibit's own title states the finding as a full sentence. The text never says that a chart "below" shows something. Where an exhibit carries the structure of its section rather than confirming one finding, state the structure in a single sentence, place the exhibit immediately after it, and then unpack its parts in the exhibit's own order under bold labels whose wording repeats the caption ("**Before the event: proactive readiness.**"). The caption, the alt text, the section's labels, the closing paragraph, and any `keyTakeaway` touching the exhibit all use the same words for the same parts, so the reader meets one named set rather than five variants of it.
- **Named sources**: name the institution alongside the person on first reference, because the institution is the credential that licenses the claim. The order is a style choice and three forms are house: the possessive ("Gartner's Weston Wicks"), the appositive ("Oyango Snell, CLOC's President and CEO", "Elly Meenan, founder of The Legal Ops Job Board"), and "of" ("Kevin Clem of Harbor"). Where the credential is the publication that carried the work rather than an employer, the publication supplies it ("Jeff Franke's four paradigms for the general counsel, set out in the ABA Journal on July 24, 2023"). Use the surname alone afterwards ("Snell"), and a short name for a publication ("the Thomson Reuters index", "the Law360 Pulse report on legal engineers"). Carry several findings from one source under a single attribution frame ("has predicted that ... and that ...") rather than rebuilding the frame for each.

### Em dashes are never used

The em dash (the long dash, Unicode U+2014) does not appear anywhere in a Spaarke piece. The rule covers the body, the title, headings, captions, alt text, link text, and the `description`, `summary`, and `keyTakeaways` frontmatter fields. Readers treat the mark as the most visible sign of machine-written text, and a more formal mark can do every job that it does. To replace a dash, decide what it was doing and then choose the replacement from the following table.

| The dash was doing this | Use instead |
|---|---|
| A pair of dashes set off an aside | A comma pair for a short aside; parentheses for a definition, a figure, or a list; a separate sentence if the aside is a thought of its own |
| A single dash introduced an explanation, a definition, or a list | A colon after a complete clause, or "such as" and commas |
| A single dash joined two independent clauses | A semicolon if both are short and balanced; otherwise a full stop and a new sentence, or a conjunction that names the logic ("because", "so", "although") |
| A single dash added an afterthought | A new sentence if the afterthought carries information; deletion if it carries only emphasis |
| A single dash set up a reversal or a punchline | A restructured sentence that states the point as a positive claim. A semicolon in the same position keeps the tell |

A spaced en dash, a double hyphen, and a spaced hyphen are the same gesture in a different glyph, and they are not substitutes. After replacing dashes, reread the paragraph. If it now carries three colons or three semicolons, the habit has only moved, and the sentences should be rewritten so that words carry the logic. The full decision guide, with before and after pairs, is in `examples/ai-tells.md`.

Two published titles contain an em dash: the article at slug `probabilistic-vs-deterministic` and the reference document at slug `spaarke-feature-specification`. Until those titles are changed, cite them by short title, as "Legal AI Is Not Deterministic" and "The Spaarke platform", and do not reproduce the dash in link text or in running prose.

One em dash is permitted. The workflow marker **TBD — confirm** is a tool token that flags an unverified fact in drafts and reference files. It is written exactly that way so that tools can find it. The lint ignores it and reports how many markers remain, and no marker may survive to publication.

## 5. Things we do not do

Rules 1 to 12 are the original list, revised where the September 2026 feedback required it. Rules 13 to 27 are new and cover the constructions that readers identify as machine-written. Each new rule has a worked before and after in `examples/ai-tells.md`. Rule numbers are stable, because briefs cite them.

1. **No exclamation points.** They read as breathless. Even in social posts, reserve them for real surprise and never for enthusiasm about our own product.

2. **No teaser questions in titles or headings.** A question is permitted as a title or a heading only when it is a question the reader is already asking and the article answers it directly, beginning in the opening paragraphs (for a heading, in the first sentence beneath it). "What should a legal department measure?" qualifies, because general counsel and CFOs put that question to legal operations directors and an article under that title answers it. "Is your matter management ready for AI?" does not qualify, because it withholds a claim in order to provoke a click. Statements remain the default, and the strongest titles in the library are statements: "The $20B Blind Spot" and "Institutional Knowledge Is Walking Out the Door".

3. **No `Imagine if` or `In today's world` openers, and no statistic in the first sentence.** They commit to nothing, and a statistic in the opening position makes the article's first act a citation rather than a claim. The default opening is the claim itself, stated in our own voice: "The mandate of legal operations is evolving rapidly." A sourced number, a specific observation, or a named scene may come first in a short post, and a number that opens a piece must be the number that proves the thesis, never the one that establishes that the subject exists. A statistic the reader would simply nod at is not an opening.

4. **No demo closes.** Do not end with `Schedule a demo` or `Talk to sales`. Conversion comes from credibility, and a button at the foot of an article adds none. The close ends on consequence (section 3). Related reading may follow as a plain list of links, without a recap paragraph and without a `Where to Go Next` section that restates the article. That clause governs the related-reading block only. It is not a second ban on the synthesis beat of the close, which section 3 permits. An author or contact line, where the brief calls for one, sits after the final paragraph and outside the argument (see `bylines.md`).

5. **No marketing-filler adjectives.** `powerful`, `robust`, `seamless`, `innovative`, `cutting-edge`, and `best-in-class` add nothing. If a feature deserves the adjective, the example proves it.

6. **No `transform your legal department`, `disrupting legal`, or `AI-powered everything`.** These are empty consulting and vendor phrases. Show the operational change instead.

7. **No "replace lawyers", "10x productivity", or "autonomous legal AI".** These claims are inconsistent with human-in-the-loop design and cannot be verified. Our story is AI-directed and human-controlled.

8. **No CEO-letter first-person plural.** `We are excited to announce` and `We believe the future is bright` belong to the announcement register. This rule does not restrict the analytical "we" of section 1 ("we recommend", "we found"), which states a position and then defends it.

9. **No stacked superlatives.** `categorically different`, `fundamentally transformative`, and `completely revolutionary` in the same paragraph move the writing into the breathless register. One earned superlative per piece is plenty, and zero is usually better.

10. **No reciting "Data → Memory → Inference" without using it structurally.** When the Legal IQ stack appears, it should organize the argument. Reciting it in the middle of a paragraph as a creed is a library habit that we have dropped.

11. **No series recap in the opening paragraphs.** What flattens an opening is a paragraph that summarises the earlier pieces (`Throughout this series, we have explored`). Spend the opening on the argument of the present piece.

    This rule governs the opening paragraph. It does not restrict naming our own earlier work. From the credential paragraph onward, a link to a Spaarke article is introduced in the citing first person set out in section 1: "In our earlier article, [Legal Ops Is Not IT for Lawyers](/why-spaarke/legal-ops-is-not-it-for-lawyers), we discussed how the administrative and strategic roles of legal operations work on each other." That construction is house form, not a recap, and a long-form article in a series will normally carry five or six of them. One may sit in the opening, after the thesis, where it credentials the claim just made. Rule 8 bans the announcement "we"; neither rule restricts the authorial "we" that owns our own body of work.

12. **No claims without a defense.** A claim is defended by named evidence or by a stated mechanism, and one of the two is always present.

    Where research measures the claim, cite it with its year and its sample. Vague attribution (`experts say`, `industry reports suggest`) is not evidence and substitutes for neither.

    Where no research measures it and the claim comes from practice, state it flat in the present tense and give the mechanism that makes it true: "A business unit that waits in the department's queue for a standard non-disclosure agreement loses time, and a business that routinely waits learns to work around the department." The mechanism is the defense. Do not hedge the verb to cover the absence of a citation ("tends to", "may", "can often"), and do not cut a claim you know to be true because no survey has measured it. A hedge sizes a claim whose magnitude is uncertain; it is not a substitute for a source.

    Three clarifications. First, the rule sets the presence of the defense, not its position. State the claim in the section's first paragraph and give the evidence after it. A section that lists evidence and leaves the conclusion for the reader to assemble has not made a claim at all (`consulting-register.md`, section 4.3). Second, the rule governs numbers, quoted findings, and claims about what other people have said or done. It does not govern the writer's own read of where the market is going, which may stand in its own sentence without a citation provided it is written as a claim about the world and not dressed as a finding. The brief records these under "What we assert from practice", and they are the only unsourced claims a draft may carry. Third, the rule requires a source for the number, not for the stance. Where a source reports a gap between two parties, name the obligation on each side, including the one that falls on the reader, rather than repeating the source's prescription as ours (`stance.md`, rule 6).

    The converse is also a rule. If a fact needs a sentence of disclaimer before a reader can use it, cut the fact. A vendor's survey that has to be introduced with a warning about the vendor's interest, a figure whose sample was never published, and a self-reported number with no independent confirmation each cost the reader more attention than they return. Provenance hygiene belongs in the brief and the plan, where the reviewer checks it; the article carries only the evidence that survives it.

13. **No em dashes and no dash substitutes.** The mark is the most visible trigger of reader suspicion, and formal punctuation does every job that it does (section 4).

14. **No negation followed by correction.** The patterns are `It is not X. It is Y.`, `not just X but Y`, and `Not because X. Because Y.` The construction invents a naive view in order to correct it, and it is the best-known tic of machine-written prose. State the positive claim and give the reason. When a real and attributable view has to be rebutted, name who holds it and answer it with evidence, in full sentences.

    Two constructions that look like the pattern are exempt, because in both the negated proposition is one a reader genuinely holds rather than one the writer invented. The first is a concession or a fairness qualification that protects a party the piece is criticising: "None of this is a failure of the frameworks. Each records the practice as it stood when it was written." The second is a denial of a position the argument does not take, where the reader would otherwise assume it: "The demand does not come from the business alone. Legal departments themselves recognize that certain low-risk legal-related activities can be handled properly by the business directly", and "The market is not zero-sum: in the same year a department insources drafting, it may buy more surge capacity from the same firms." The test is whether a reader would have held the negated view. If they would, the denial is doing work and stays.

15. **No `X, not Y` tagline contrasts.** Examples are `structural, not contractual` and `a flywheel, not a feature stack`. The tagline puts a slogan where an explanation should be. Earlier versions of this guide called it a "two-noun framing" and treated it as a strength. An ordinary contrast inside a sentence that goes on to explain the difference is normal English and is not the target of this rule. Neither is a heading of the form `X, not only Y` where the section treats both halves.

    When the banned tagline carries a real concession, the repair is to promote the negative half to its own sentence with the offending party as the grammatical subject, not to drop it. "Legal as an enabler, not a roadblock" becomes "Legal can become an impediment to the business", followed by the evidence and then the remedy. Deleting the negative half removes the reason the argument exists, and a place-metaphor such as "the bottleneck" is not a substitute, because it leaves the fault unattributed (`stance.md`, rule 4).

16. **No verbless fragments and no fragment stacks.** `No governance framework. No audit trail. No data boundary.` performs emphasis that the content has not earned. Write the sentence.

17. **No one-sentence paragraphs used for effect.** White space around a short sentence claims an importance that the argument should demonstrate. Fold the sentence into the paragraph it belongs to.

18. **No clipped aphoristic closers.** `One learns. The other just runs.` is the type. A pair of short symmetrical sentences at the end of a paragraph is a recognized template when it restates what the paragraph has already shown. Apply the same test section 2 applies to every short sentence: does it carry content. A second sentence that completes a comparison rather than compressing it is content and stays, as in "A request that arrives through a front door is counted, categorized, and timed. A request that arrives as an email to the lawyer who knows the business unit is none of those things." A single sentence that ends on a balanced contrast between two nouns is the same device compressed, and it is also out: "the difference between a guardrail the business trusts and a rule the business routes around" puts a shape where a claim should be. Replace it with a plain claim about what people will do, and let one comparative intensifier carry the emphasis the contrast was carrying: "Business teams are far more likely to follow guardrails they understand and trust."

19. **No rhetorical question followed by its own answer.** `The result?` and `So what can?` stage a discovery that the writer has already made. Make the claim. Diagnostic questions that readers can put to their own departments are content and remain welcome, as is a title question permitted by rule 2. The house form for a diagnostic list is a bold label naming the signal the question tests, the question addressed to "you", and one sentence saying what a "no" would reveal: "**Risk level.** Can you see where risk is concentrating, by business unit, agreement type, or jurisdiction, before it becomes a matter? This probes whether the department can act before the event rather than after it." The label is the bare noun here, which is the second of the two label grammars section 4 sets out, and it is correct for a diagnostic list. A bare list of questions leaves the reader to work out what each one is for.

20. **No colon reveals.** `The result:` and `comes down to one word:` use the colon as a drum roll. A colon introduces a list, a definition, or a specification.

21. **No signposting and no throat-clearing.** `Here is the thing`, `Here is why`, `Let us unpack`, `Now consider` standing alone as a paragraph opener, and `In this section we will` delay the point and imitate conversation. Start with the point. `Consider a security software company that rewrites its non-disclosure agreement playbook` is not signposting, with or without "for example" after the verb. It marks a scene as hypothetical inside the sentence that tells it, which is house form. This rule governs section-level and paragraph-level announcements. One article-level roadmap sentence at the end of the opening is permitted (section 3).

22. **No lists of three used for rhythm, and no anaphora runs.** Use three items when the content has three items. Consecutive sentences that open with the same words ("They expect", "They expect", "They expect") read as a template. Deliberate repetition inside a reference list is correct and is not an anaphora run. In a diagnostic list, each question may be followed by one sentence in a repeated frame saying what a "no" would reveal, and the repeated wording is what makes the list scannable.

23. **No present-participle tails.** A clause such as `, highlighting the importance of` or `, underscoring the need for` asserts significance without a subject or evidence. Make it a sentence with a subject, or cut it.

24. **No inflated abstractions, and no intensifier doing the work of evidence.** `load-bearing`, `plays a crucial role`, and `stands as a testament` claim importance instead of showing it, as does an adverb attached to a claim the sentence does not support. The target is the unsupported claim, not the word class. A comparative intensifier that sizes a claim the sentence goes on to support is correct and is house form: "Business teams are far more likely to follow guardrails they understand and trust", "The supply side is changing faster still", "The mandate of legal operations is evolving rapidly". The adjective is available where it states the scope of a change the section proves: "Legal operations frameworks are due for a fundamental rethink" is a claim about how deep the revision has to go, and the section that follows shows the four places the frameworks fall short. Adjective-free prose is not a goal; it leaves the reader to assign weight that the writer should have assigned.

25. **No closes that only summarise.** `In conclusion`, `In summary`, and `This article explored` are out, as is a closing section that restates the argument in fresh words and stops there. One short synthesis paragraph is permitted at the head of the close when it is followed by the thesis and the imperative paragraph described in section 3.

26. **No bold labels in place of reasoning.** A stack of bolded phrases each followed by two sentences of assertion is a tell when the phrases stand in for an argument the writer has not made. The test is whether each item could be defended on its own; if the items make sense only read together, they are a paragraph. A bulleted enumeration in which each item carries one supported claim under a bold label is the house pattern for parallel items and is not the target of this rule (section 4).

27. **No AI-era vocabulary.** `delve`, `tapestry`, `testament`, `landscape`, `realm`, `pivotal`, `crucial`, `underscore`, and `foster` are read as machine-written, especially in clusters. The working list is in `examples/ai-tells.md`, and the older do-not-say list is in `vocabulary.md`, section 2.

These rules remove symptoms. The cause is usually a sentence written without a specific fact to put in it, so the repair is to supply the fact: the named source, the number, the case, or the reason. Removing the surface pattern while leaving the vagueness in place produces prose that is harder to flag and no better to read.

## 6. Calibration by content type

The register scales across formats, and the rules in sections 4 and 5 apply to every format, including the ban on em dashes. Detail lives in `content-types/<type>.md` where a type file exists.

- **Long-form articles**: the primary format for thought leadership and the format this guide was written for. There is no fixed length cap. Length follows the argument and the evidence, every section must advance the argument, and padding is cut at any length. The structure is a thesis in the opening, message-bearing subheadings, evidence paragraphs, and a close on consequence. Long-form articles publish to `content/blog/`. Their calibration is in `content-types/blog-post.md`, section 2.1, and the brief sets the length.
- **Blog posts**: the shorter format for commentary, announcements, and single-point arguments, in the same register. They typically run 1,000 to 1,800 words. That range describes the short format and is not a cap on long-form work: a piece that needs more room is a long-form article.
- **White papers**: a more formal cadence, footnoted citations, a longer setup, and more headings. They remain written for practitioners and are never academic for its own sake.
- **LinkedIn posts**: looser, often first person from a named byline, with shorter sentences and contractions allowed. The hook belongs in the first two lines, because the mobile preview cuts off around line three. Hashtags are minimal and sit at the bottom.
- **Tweets**: maximum density, idiomatic for X. One idea per tweet. Threads only when each tweet earns the next.

## 7. Quick checklist

Run this before sending a draft for review. If any answer is "no", fix the draft.

1. Does the opening state the claim in our own voice, with the first attributed statistic no earlier than the third sentence? A specific observation or a named scene may open a piece, and a short post may open on a sourced number, but a number in the opening position has to be the one that proves the thesis rather than the one that establishes that the subject exists (section 5, rule 3). Does the thesis arrive within the first 150 words for a short post or the first 250 words for a long-form article, and never later than the third paragraph?
2. Does every category claim have a named source, a number with its year and sample, or an explicit comparison, and is every statistic interpreted as well as reported?
3. Are the sentences complete, with no verbless fragments and with short sentences used only where they carry content? The lint prints the mean sentence length (target roughly 15 to 25 words) and the share of very short and very long sentences; read those figures.
4. Does each paragraph have a topic sentence and development; is each claim supported by evidence somewhere in its run of paragraphs; and does every run of statistic-bearing paragraphs end in an argument paragraph that carries no statistics? The lint prints the median paragraph length (most paragraphs run 50 to 110 words).
5. Do the headings render a verdict rather than report a movement, in sentence case, with a noun or gerund phrase permitted where it names something the reader can act on (section 4), and is any question in a title or heading one that the reader is asking and that the piece answers directly (section 5, rule 2)?
6. Is the piece free of em dashes, spaced en dashes, double hyphens, and spaced hyphens everywhere, including the title and the `description`, `summary`, and `keyTakeaways` fields? Are ranges written with "to"? Has every TBD marker been resolved?
7. Is the piece free of the constructions in section 5, rules 14 to 26? Sweep it against the pre-review checklist in `examples/ai-tells.md`. Item 6 above covers rule 13 and item 8 covers rule 27, so items 6 to 8 together sweep rules 13 to 27 with no gap.
8. Is the piece free of the words and phrases in section 5, rules 5 to 9 and 27, and in `vocabulary.md`, section 2?
9. If the Legal IQ stack or LOI is invoked, does it organize the argument?
10. Does every bulleted enumeration meet the test in section 4, with each item carrying its own claim and its own evidence under labels worded the same way across the set, and does a long-form article stay inside the ceiling of three bold lead-in lists (section 4; section 5, rule 26)? Where the items make sense only when read together, has the passage been written as a paragraph?
11. Where a section lays out two courses of action, does it end by naming one as the better answer and saying what that answer consists of? A section that describes a choice and calls it a decision the reader has to make has analysed where it should have recommended (`examples/ai-tells.md`, section 6.5; `stance.md`, rule 8).
12. Does the close end on consequence, without a summary, a "Conclusion" heading, or a pitch?
13. Does the piece name one primary force and subordinate the rest, does it state the concession the argument depends on, and does it name the decision, the actors, and what each produces in the field's own terms? Check in particular: no counted list of causes and no ordinal used as a reference handle; legal operations facilitating, building, and managing rather than owning or deciding; the business managing scoped legal-related activities rather than taking on risk (`stance.md`).
14. Does the close run in four beats (synthesis, the thesis in the opening's words plus the sentence that discharges the title, a paragraph of imperative first moves, the forward pointer) under a heading that reuses the title's key term?
15. Does `npm run voice:lint -- <file>` report 0 errors, and has every warning been read and either fixed or accepted for a stated reason?
