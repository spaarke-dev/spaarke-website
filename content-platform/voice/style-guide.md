# Style guide

Spaarke writes for general counsel, legal operations directors, and CIOs who have heard the AI pitch a hundred times. They read McKinsey Quarterly and Harvard Business Review, and they measure vendor writing against that standard. They discount any sentence that could have come from a sales deck, and they trust prose that earns its claims.

This is the rulebook for how every piece sounds. Read it at the start of every writing session, together with its two companion files:

- `examples/consulting-register.md` is the positive model. It describes the register, quotes the publications we are measured against, and gives model passages.
- `examples/ai-tells.md` is the removal guide. It lists the constructions that readers identify as machine-written, with a before and an after for each.

**Why this guide was revised.** In September 2026 readers told us that the published articles sounded as though an AI had written them. A local audit traced the cause to this guide and its example files, which had endorsed em dashes, negation followed by correction, verbless fragments, short symmetrical closers, one-sentence paragraphs, and bold lead-in bullets. This revision reverses those instructions. Where an older voice document, a template, or a published article conflicts with this guide, this guide governs.

---

## 1. Voice: what we sound like

The target register is strategy-consulting long-form: the register of McKinsey Quarterly and Harvard Business Review, and of the better work from BCG, Bain, Deloitte Insights, and MIT Sloan Management Review. The writer is an informed colleague who explains a considered position to a peer. The stance remains operator to operator, which means that we write as people who build and run systems, for people who run legal departments. The writer is neither a vendor making a pitch nor an analyst delivering a lecture.

The register is business-like without being stiff. It uses complete sentences, states a clear claim, gives the evidence, and explains the reasoning. It differs from marketing copy because every claim is sized by its evidence. It differs from academic prose because the conclusion comes first and the reader is told what to do with it. Five qualities define the voice, and each is shown here with a sentence we would write and a sentence we would not.

> **Confident.** We take a position and state it before the support. Confidence comes from the evidence that follows the claim, and adjectives cannot supply it.
> We say: "At companies with revenue above $20 billion, median total legal spend rose from $50.8 million to $80 million in a single year (ACC and Major, Lindsey & Africa, Law Department Management Benchmarking Report, 2024). An increase of 57% in one year is large enough to draw the attention of any CFO."
> We do not say: "AI is completely transforming legal operations as we know it!"

> **Measured.** We say how far the evidence goes. A claim carries at most one hedge, placed on the verb ("suggests", "tends to"), and the strongest objection is conceded once and then answered.
> We say: "Among in-house lawyers who report high stress, 24% say they plan to leave within the next year (ACC, The State of Stress Among In-house Legal Professionals, December 2025). The figure measures intent, so it signals turnover risk and should not be read as a departure rate."
> We do not say: "Legal operations may potentially benefit from operational intelligence in certain circumstances."

> **Specific.** Names, dates, counts, durations, and before-and-after figures do the work that adjectives cannot do.
> We say: "When she retired, her replacement inherited the case files but not the playbook. Settlements that she would have pushed harder on were accepted too early."
> We do not say: "Knowledge loss has a major impact on operational outcomes."

> **Plainspoken.** We use plain verbs, concrete nouns, and the field's own terms, and we keep one term for one thing.
> We say: "A signed contract records the final terms. The negotiation history (what was conceded, which arguments worked, and what the fallback position was) usually stays with the negotiator, and the next negotiator starts without it."
> We do not say: "Our solution leverages enterprise-grade memory architecture to drive transformative collaboration outcomes."

> **Collegial.** The writer stands beside the reader and looks at the problem with them. We credit other thinkers by name and build on the frameworks the field already uses.
> We say: "CLOC's Core 12 already defines the functions a mature department performs. We build on that framework here and examine which of those functions depend on the same matter data."
> We do not say: "The future of legal is here, and it is more powerful than ever before."

Pronouns follow from the stance. Use "we" for Spaarke's evidence and positions ("we recommend", "we found", and "in our experience" where the experience is real). Describe readers by role in analysis ("general counsel", "legal operations directors"). Use "you" for direct advice, checklists, and diagnostic questions. The announcement register remains out of bounds (section 5, rule 8).

If a sentence could appear on a CMO's slide, it probably does not belong in our writing.

## 2. Sentences

Write complete sentences. Each sentence has a subject and a finite verb, makes one point, and connects logically to its neighbors.

Aim for an average of roughly 15 to 25 words across a piece, and let length follow content. A definition or a finding may need eight words; a qualified claim may need 35. A sentence that runs past about 40 words usually holds two ideas and should be divided.

Short declarative sentences are welcome when they carry content, such as a topic sentence, a finding, or a definition. They are never used for drama. If a short sentence exists only to land the sentence before it, merge the two or delete it. Earlier versions of this guide praised "a long claim, a short landing" and quoted two closers from the library as models. That advice is withdrawn, because readers identify the pattern as machine-written.

Verbless fragments are not used. `Not storage.`, `Faster.`, and `No audit trail.` each need a subject and a verb, or they belong inside the sentence they comment on.

Claims are stated positively. Say what a thing is and why, and do not introduce a view that nobody holds in order to correct it (section 5, rule 14).

Subordinate clauses, semicolons, and conjunctions that name the logic ("because", "although", "whereas", "so") are the normal equipment of business prose, and one well-built compound sentence is preferable to three clipped ones. The earlier instructions to split any sentence that needs "scaffolding" and to cut whatever cannot be read aloud in one breath are withdrawn.

A few smaller rules complete the section:

- Prefer the active voice. Use the passive only when the actor is unimportant or unknown.
- A sentence may open with "But" or "Yet" when the word carries a real turn in the argument, as it does in McKinsey and Harvard Business Review prose. Use the device sparingly: never in consecutive sentences, never to launch a clipped dramatic line, and never "And" as a landing. A piece that replaces every such opening with "However," or "In addition," becomes stiff, which is a template of its own.
- Common contractions ("it's", "don't", "won't") are acceptable in articles when used sparingly, because the register is business-like and not stiff. Do not use them in white papers or in passages that report research findings. LinkedIn posts and tweets may use them freely.
- Use parallel structure and lists of three only when the content has that many parts.
- Use one term for one thing. Lawyers read a change of term as a change of meaning.

## 3. Paragraphs

A paragraph makes one point. It opens with a topic sentence that states the point, develops it, and supports it with evidence: a named source, a number, a case, or a reasoned comparison. Most paragraphs run 50 to 100 words and three to six sentences. Length should vary with the argument, since uniform paragraphs are themselves a mark of template writing.

The following paragraph shows the pattern. Its statistic comes from `research-sources.md` and should be verified again before reuse.

> Most legal departments already know that their data is the constraint. In the 2025 EY Law General Counsel Study, which surveyed 1,000 general counsel and chief legal officers at companies with revenue above $1 billion, 52% of respondents reported disorganized data and 75% said they were developing or refining their technology and data strategies. The second figure matters more than the first, because it suggests that three departments in four have moved from recognizing the problem to planning a response. The open question is what those strategies should fix first.

Connectives are welcome when they carry logic. "However", "because", "as a result", "for example", "by contrast", and "therefore" tell the reader how one sentence bears on the next, and this register depends on them. The earlier instruction to cut transition words is withdrawn. What remains unwelcome is additive filler (`moreover`, `furthermore`, `additionally`, `it is worth noting that`), which joins sentences without stating a relation between them.

One-sentence paragraphs are not used for effect. The only one-sentence paragraphs in an article are functional: a lead-in to a list, a block quotation, or an exhibit. A sentence that seems to deserve its own paragraph belongs at the start or the end of a neighboring paragraph.

A paragraph ends when its evidence or reasoning is complete. It does not need a closing line that restates the point in compressed form, and a piece in which every paragraph ends on such a line reads as formula.

Evidence is attributed and interpreted. Attribute every statistic in the sentence that carries it: the source, the instrument, the year, and the sample where it matters. Report survey results as what respondents said. Then interpret the number by saying what it shows, what it cannot show, or what it should be compared with.

Openings state the thesis early: within the first 150 words for a short post and the first 250 words for a long-form article, and never later than the third paragraph. The usual shape is a situation the reader accepts, the complication, and then the answer. A new term is introduced after the problem it names has been described, so that the term arrives as a label for something the reader already recognizes; the thesis does not wait for the term.

Sections connect to each other. Open a section with a sentence or two that link it to what the previous section established. Do not announce a section (`In this section we will explore`) and do not recap it at its end.

Closings end on consequence. The final paragraph states what follows from the argument for the reader: a decision to make, a risk that grows, or a question that remains open. It does not summarize the article, it carries no "Conclusion" heading, and it does not pitch (section 5, rules 4 and 25).

## 4. Formatting conventions

This section is reference material, so it uses labeled entries. The em dash rule is long enough to need its own subsection, which follows the list.

- **Titles**: title case, as in the published library. A title makes a statement by default. A question is permitted only under section 5, rule 2. Titles never contain an em dash.
- **Headings**: sentence case, no periods. A heading states the point of its section ("Intake data determines what the department can report"), typically in 4 to 12 words. A topic label ("Intake", "Background", "Overview") or a template (`What it is`, `Why it matters`, `How to get started`) does not qualify. The test is that a reader who reads only the headings can follow the argument. Question headings fall under section 5, rule 2.
- **Numbers**: numerals for 10 and above ("12 attorneys", "200 similar matters"); spelled out below 10 ("three workstreams") except in lists or technical contexts. Always numerals for money and percentages, with the % sign. A number that opens a sentence is spelled out, or the sentence is recast.
- **Ranges**: written with the word "to": "1,000 to 1,800 words", "20% to 25%", "from 2024 to 2026". Ranges do not use a dash of any kind.
- **Em dashes**: never. See the subsection that follows this list.
- **Semicolons**: acceptable. Use one between two closely related independent clauses, or between items in a series when the items contain commas.
- **Colons**: introduce a list, a definition, or a specification after a complete clause. A colon does not stage a reveal (section 5, rule 20).
- **Parentheses**: use for definitions, abbreviations on first use, the exact figure behind a rounded one, exhibit references, and short factual asides.
- **Hyphens**: correct inside compound words ("fixed-fee", "e-billing", "matter-level"). A hyphen with a space on each side is a dash substitute and is not used.
- **Lists**: bullets for parallel items that a reader will scan; numbered lists when order matters or the prose refers back ("the three decisions"). Long-form argument belongs in paragraphs under message-bearing subheadings. If each item needs two or three sentences of reasoning, the items are paragraphs and should be written as paragraphs.
- **Bold lead-in bullets**: no longer a signature pattern. Use them sparingly, and reserve them for reference material (glossaries, specifications, step lists, and rulebooks such as this one) in which each item is a separate entry that a reader looks up. They do not carry the argument of an article.
- **Bold inside prose**: rare. Reserve it for scannable terms or named concepts on first use. No more than three per 1,000 words of body prose.
- **Italics**: technical terms on first introduction, or single-word emphasis ("It is *plausible* bad output").
- **Exhibits**: refer to an exhibit in parentheses at the end of the sentence that states the finding, in the form "(Exhibit 1)". The exhibit's own title states the finding as a full sentence. The text never says that a chart "below" shows something.

### Em dashes are never used

The em dash (the long dash, Unicode U+2014) does not appear anywhere in a Spaarke piece. The rule covers the body, the title, headings, captions, alt text, link text, and the `description`, `summary`, and `keyTakeaways` frontmatter fields. Readers treat the mark as the most visible sign of machine-written text, and a more formal mark can do every job that it does. To replace a dash, decide what it was doing and then choose the replacement from the following table.

| The dash was doing this | Use instead |
|---|---|
| A pair of dashes set off an aside | A comma pair for a short aside; parentheses for a definition, a figure, or a list; a separate sentence if the aside is a thought of its own |
| A single dash introduced an explanation, a definition, or a list | A colon after a complete clause, or "such as" and commas |
| A single dash joined two independent clauses | A semicolon if both are short and balanced; otherwise a full stop and a new sentence, or a conjunction that names the logic ("because", "so", "although") |
| A single dash added an afterthought | A new sentence if the afterthought carries information; deletion if it carries only emphasis |
| A single dash set up a reversal or a punchline | A restructured sentence that states the point as a positive claim. A semicolon in the same position keeps the tell |

A spaced en dash, a double hyphen, and a spaced hyphen are the same gesture in a different glyph, and they are not substitutes. After replacing dashes, reread the paragraph. If it now carries three colons or three semicolons, the habit has only moved, and the sentences should be rewritten so that words carry the logic. The full decision guide, with before and after pairs, is in `examples/ai-tells.md`.

Two published titles contain an em dash: the article at slug `probabilistic-vs-deterministic` and the reference document at slug `spaarke-feature-specification`. Until those titles are changed, cite them by short title, as "Legal AI Is Not Deterministic" and "The Spaarke platform", and do not reproduce the dash in link text or in running prose.

One em dash is permitted. The workflow marker **TBD — confirm** is a tool token that flags an unverified fact in drafts and reference files. It is written exactly that way so that tools can find it. The lint ignores it and reports how many markers remain, and no marker may survive to publication.

## 5. Things we do not do

Rules 1 to 12 are the original list, revised where the September 2026 feedback required it. Rules 13 to 27 are new and cover the constructions that readers identify as machine-written. Each new rule has a worked before and after in `examples/ai-tells.md`. Rule numbers are stable, because briefs cite them.

1. **No exclamation points.** They read as breathless. Even in social posts, reserve them for real surprise and never for enthusiasm about our own product.

2. **No teaser questions in titles or headings.** A question is permitted as a title or a heading only when it is a question the reader is already asking and the article answers it directly, beginning in the opening paragraphs (for a heading, in the first sentence beneath it). "What should a legal department measure?" qualifies, because general counsel and CFOs put that question to legal operations directors and an article under that title answers it. "Is your matter management ready for AI?" does not qualify, because it withholds a claim in order to provoke a click. Statements remain the default, and the strongest titles in the library are statements: "The $20B Blind Spot" and "Institutional Knowledge Is Walking Out the Door".

3. **No `Imagine if` or `In today's world` openers.** They commit to nothing. Open with a specific observation, a named scene, a sourced number, or a direct claim.

4. **No demo closes.** Do not end with `Schedule a demo` or `Talk to sales`. Conversion comes from credibility, and a button at the foot of an article adds none. The close ends on consequence (section 3). Related reading may follow as a plain list of links, without a recap paragraph and without a `Where to Go Next` section that restates the article. An author or contact line, where the brief calls for one, sits after the final paragraph and outside the argument (see `bylines.md`).

5. **No marketing-filler adjectives.** `powerful`, `robust`, `seamless`, `innovative`, `cutting-edge`, and `best-in-class` add nothing. If a feature deserves the adjective, the example proves it.

6. **No `transform your legal department`, `disrupting legal`, or `AI-powered everything`.** These are empty consulting and vendor phrases. Show the operational change instead.

7. **No "replace lawyers", "10x productivity", or "autonomous legal AI".** These claims are inconsistent with human-in-the-loop design and cannot be verified. Our story is AI-directed and human-controlled.

8. **No CEO-letter first-person plural.** `We are excited to announce` and `We believe the future is bright` belong to the announcement register. This rule does not restrict the analytical "we" of section 1 ("we recommend", "we found"), which states a position and then defends it.

9. **No stacked superlatives.** `categorically different`, `fundamentally transformative`, and `completely revolutionary` in the same paragraph move the writing into the breathless register. One earned superlative per piece is plenty, and zero is usually better.

10. **No reciting "Data → Memory → Inference" without using it structurally.** When the Legal IQ stack appears, it should organize the argument. Reciting it in the middle of a paragraph as a creed is a library habit that we have dropped.

11. **No series recap openers.** `Throughout this series, we` and `In our previous article, we introduced` flatten the first paragraph. Work the connection to earlier pieces into the body, and spend the opening on the argument of the present piece.

12. **No claims without a defense.** Every category claim is paired with named evidence, such as CLOC Core 12, the ACC Maturity Model, the Center for American Progress replacement-cost range of 100% to 213% of salary, or a survey cited with its year and sample. Vague attribution (`experts say`, `industry reports suggest`) is not evidence. If a sentence asserts something the reader could challenge, the next sentence answers the challenge.

13. **No em dashes and no dash substitutes.** The mark is the most visible trigger of reader suspicion, and formal punctuation does every job that it does (section 4).

14. **No negation followed by correction.** The patterns are `It is not X. It is Y.`, `not just X but Y`, and `Not because X. Because Y.` The construction invents a naive view in order to correct it, and it is the best-known tic of machine-written prose. State the positive claim and give the reason. When a real and attributable view has to be rebutted, name who holds it and answer it with evidence, in full sentences.

15. **No `X, not Y` tagline contrasts.** Examples are `structural, not contractual` and `a flywheel, not a feature stack`. The tagline puts a slogan where an explanation should be. Earlier versions of this guide called it a "two-noun framing" and treated it as a strength. An ordinary contrast inside a sentence that goes on to explain the difference is normal English and is not the target of this rule.

16. **No verbless fragments and no fragment stacks.** `No governance framework. No audit trail. No data boundary.` performs emphasis that the content has not earned. Write the sentence.

17. **No one-sentence paragraphs used for effect.** White space around a short sentence claims an importance that the argument should demonstrate. Fold the sentence into the paragraph it belongs to.

18. **No clipped aphoristic closers.** `One learns. The other just runs.` is the type. A pair of short symmetrical sentences at the end of a paragraph is a recognized template, and it restates what the paragraph has already shown.

19. **No rhetorical question followed by its own answer.** `The result?` and `So what can?` stage a discovery that the writer has already made. Make the claim. Diagnostic questions that readers can put to their own departments are content and remain welcome, as is a title question permitted by rule 2.

20. **No colon reveals.** `The result:` and `comes down to one word:` use the colon as a drum roll. A colon introduces a list, a definition, or a specification.

21. **No signposting and no throat-clearing.** `Here is the thing`, `Here is why`, `Let us unpack`, `Now consider`, and `In this section we will` delay the point and imitate conversation. Start with the point.

22. **No lists of three used for rhythm, and no anaphora runs.** Use three items when the content has three items. Consecutive sentences that open with the same words ("They expect", "They expect", "They expect") read as a template.

23. **No present-participle tails.** A clause such as `, highlighting the importance of` or `, underscoring the need for` asserts significance without a subject or evidence. Make it a sentence with a subject, or cut it.

24. **No inflated abstractions and no intensifiers.** `fundamentally`, `genuinely`, `enormously`, `load-bearing`, `plays a crucial role`, and `stands as a testament` claim importance instead of showing it. State what happened and let the reader judge its weight.

25. **No summary closers.** `In conclusion`, `This article explored`, and a final section that restates the argument add nothing that the reader does not already have. End on consequence (section 3).

26. **No bold lead-in bullets carrying the argument.** A stack of bolded phrases with two-sentence explanations fragments reasoning that should be connected. Write paragraphs, and keep the pattern for reference material (section 4).

27. **No AI-era vocabulary.** `delve`, `tapestry`, `testament`, `landscape`, `realm`, `pivotal`, `crucial`, `underscore`, and `foster` are read as machine-written, especially in clusters. The working list is in `examples/ai-tells.md`, and the older do-not-say list is in `vocabulary.md`, section 2.

These rules remove symptoms. The cause is usually a sentence written without a specific fact to put in it, so the repair is to supply the fact: the named source, the number, the case, or the reason. Removing the surface pattern while leaving the vagueness in place produces prose that is harder to flag and no better to read.

## 6. Calibration by content type

The register scales across formats, and the rules in sections 4 and 5 apply to every format, including the ban on em dashes. Detail lives in `content-types/<type>.md` where a type file exists.

- **Long-form articles**: the primary format for thought leadership and the format this guide was written for. There is no fixed length cap. Length follows the argument and the evidence, every section must advance the argument, and padding is cut at any length. The structure is a thesis in the opening, message-bearing subheadings, evidence paragraphs, and a close on consequence. Long-form articles publish to `content/blog/`. Their calibration is in `content-types/blog-post.md`, section 2.1, and the brief sets the length.
- **Blog posts**: the shorter format for commentary, announcements, and single-point arguments, in the same register. They typically run 1,000 to 1,800 words. That range describes the short format and is not a cap on long-form work: a piece that needs more room is a long-form article.
- **White papers**: a more formal cadence, footnoted citations, a longer setup, and more headings. They remain written for practitioners and are never academic for its own sake.
- **LinkedIn posts**: looser, often first person from a named byline, with shorter sentences and contractions allowed. The hook belongs in the first two lines, because the mobile preview cuts off around line three. Hashtags are minimal and sit at the bottom.
- **Tweets**: maximum density, idiomatic for X. One idea per tweet. Threads only when each tweet earns the next.

## 7. Quick checklist

Run this before sending a draft for review. If any answer is "no", fix the draft.

1. Does the opening make a specific observation, name a scene, or state a sourced number or a direct claim, and does the thesis arrive within the first 150 words for a short post or the first 250 words for a long-form article, and never later than the third paragraph?
2. Does every category claim have a named source, a number with its year and sample, or an explicit comparison, and is every statistic interpreted as well as reported?
3. Are the sentences complete, with no verbless fragments and with short sentences used only where they carry content? The lint prints the mean sentence length (target roughly 15 to 25 words) and the share of very short and very long sentences; read those figures.
4. Does each paragraph have a topic sentence, development, and evidence, with connectives that carry logic and no one-sentence paragraph used for effect? The lint prints the median paragraph length (most paragraphs run 50 to 100 words).
5. Do the headings state the point of each section in sentence case, and is any question in a title or heading one that the reader is asking and that the piece answers directly (section 5, rule 2)?
6. Is the piece free of em dashes, spaced en dashes, double hyphens, and spaced hyphens everywhere, including the title and the `description`, `summary`, and `keyTakeaways` fields? Are ranges written with "to"? Has every TBD marker been resolved?
7. Is the piece free of the constructions in section 5, rules 14 to 25? Sweep it against the pre-review checklist in `examples/ai-tells.md`.
8. Is the piece free of the words and phrases in section 5, rules 5 to 9 and 27, and in `vocabulary.md`, section 2?
9. If the Legal IQ stack or LOI is invoked, does it organize the argument?
10. Are bullets limited to parallel items that a reader will scan, with the argument carried in paragraphs?
11. Does the close end on consequence, without a summary, a "Conclusion" heading, or a pitch?
12. Does `npm run voice:lint -- <file>` report 0 errors, and has every warning been read and either fixed or accepted for a stated reason?
