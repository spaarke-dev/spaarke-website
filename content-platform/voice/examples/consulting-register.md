# Consulting register: the positive model

This file shows what to write toward. The rules are in `../style-guide.md`, and the constructions to remove are in `ai-tells.md`. Load this file before drafting a long-form article, and measure the draft against the model passages in section 6.

The research behind the file is in `content-platform/research/2026-09-loi-series/notes/`: `consulting-voice-and-tone.md`, its fact-check in `consulting-voice-and-tone.verified.md`, and the follow-up on McKinsey in `gap-mckinsey-primary-text-for-voice.md`.

---

## 1. What the register is

The register is strategy-consulting long-form: the way McKinsey Quarterly, Harvard Business Review, MIT Sloan Management Review, strategy+business, Bain, BCG, and Deloitte Insights write for senior managers. The writer is an informed colleague who explains a considered position to a peer. The prose is business-like without being stiff. It uses complete sentences, states a clear claim, gives the evidence, and explains the reasoning.

The publications describe their readers in similar terms. Harvard Business Review tells contributors that "Our readers are smart, skeptical, and busy" (guidelines for contributors, updated June 10, 2026). MIT Sloan Management Review's editorial director wrote that "Rigor is necessary but not sufficient. Our readers are busy, and they seek utility." (Lisa Burrell, September 10, 2019). strategy+business asks for "clear, accessibly written articles that avoid jargon and approach readers with intelligence, wit, and perspective" (writers' guidelines, undated, read on September 21, 2026). Spaarke's readers fit the description. They are general counsel, legal operations directors, and CIOs who check numbers and who have heard every vendor pitch.

Four qualities define the tone, and the style guide adds a fifth, plainspoken, for word choice. The writing is confident, because it takes a position and states the claim before the support. It is measured, because it says how far the evidence goes, concedes the strongest objection once, and interprets its own numbers with some skepticism. It is specific, because names, dates, counts, and before-and-after figures do the work that adjectives cannot do. It is collegial, because the writer stands beside the reader, credits other thinkers by name, and frames advice as something the reader can test.

The publications also say what they reject, and the list applies to us. Harvard Business Review does not publish pieces that "come across as promotional of your products or services", and strategy+business declines "articles that seek primarily to market a product, consulting practice, or service". According to the Harvard Business Review guidelines, "One of the most common reasons we turn down proposals is because the findings or prescriptions aren't surprising." The same guidelines now add that "The ideas should not be easily replicable by simply asking a large language model (LLM)." The practical consequence for Spaarke is that the company appears in an article as a source of evidence and of positions, and the reader's decision remains the subject.

## 2. How the register differs from marketing copy and from academic prose

| Marketing copy | Consulting register |
|---|---|
| Benefit claims with superlatives | Claims sized by evidence, with the limits stated |
| The company is the hero | The reader's decision is the subject, and the company appears as a source of evidence |
| Urgency by exhortation | Urgency by consequence, shown with a case |
| Unattributed statistics, or a vendor's own | Statistics with source, sample, and date, with third-party data and our own data kept distinct |
| Problem, product, call to action | Situation, complication, answer, application, stakes |
| Adjectives such as `powerful`, `seamless`, and `cutting-edge` | Nouns and numbers |
| Ends with an invitation to contact sales | Ends with a consequence, or with a tool the reader can use alone |

| Academic prose | Consulting register |
|---|---|
| Builds toward the conclusion | States the conclusion first, or by the third paragraph |
| Opens with a literature review | Credits prior work in a clause, at the point where it is used |
| Stacks hedges for safety | Allows one calibrated hedge per claim |
| Devotes a section to method | Discloses method in one or two plain sentences and puts the detail in a note |
| Prefers the passive voice and abstract nouns | Prefers the active voice and named actors |
| Uses author and date citations | Attributes inline |
| Uses topic headings | Uses headings that carry a message |
| Aims at a contribution to theory | Aims at what a leader should do next |
| Stays in the third person | Uses "we" and "you" |

## 3. The evidence base and its limits

The observations in this file come from a research pass on September 21, 2026. The researcher read the official guidance pages of Harvard Business Review, strategy+business, and MIT Sloan Management Review, and then more than 20 articles from Harvard Business Review, Deloitte Insights, Bain, MIT Sloan Management Review, and strategy+business. An independent fact-checker then fetched the sources again. Every direct quotation that the fact-checker tested matched the source, and three corrections from that check are applied here. Deloitte Insights articles are cited by the date printed on the page, which falls one day after the timestamp in the page's metadata. The Harvard Business Review guideline says "one of the most common reasons", which is weaker than "the most common reason". The Slideworks guidance on action titles says "up to 15 words".

McKinsey needs a separate note. McKinsey's website refused automated access during the research: requests timed out, a public reader proxy returned an access-denied error, and an undisguised headless browser was refused. The first research pass therefore contained no McKinsey text. A follow-up task read 11 McKinsey pages from Internet Archive snapshots taken between October 2025 and August 2026, and it matched every quotation by script against the snapshot text. That text was not read from the live site, and the independent fact-checker did not review it. McKinsey observations in this file are therefore labeled "archive snapshot", and a person with a browser should check them against the live pages before they are cited outside this repository.

Each quotation in section 4 carries one of three labels:

- **Fact-checked** means that the independent fact-checker confirmed the wording against the source.
- **Researcher-checked** means that the researcher matched the wording against the downloaded page text, and the fact-checker did not test that sentence.
- **Archive snapshot** means that the wording was matched against an Internet Archive copy of a McKinsey page.

The target publications differ from our house style in two ways, and the file should be honest about both. First, all of them use em dashes. The typical density in the Harvard Business Review sample and its sibling publications was 2 to 7 per 1,000 words, and the median McKinsey article in the archive sample had 4.7. The register does not depend on the mark, however. A Harvard Business Review research article of 1,756 words, published on September 21, 2026, contains none, and Bain's 2019 article on legal departments contains two. Second, Harvard Business Review authors use the corrective frame (a denial followed by the real answer) freely, and one 2026 article in the sample uses it more than a dozen times. Our readers identify that frame as machine-written, so house style does not use it. The quotations chosen for this file contain no em dash, and the model passages in section 6 show that the register survives without either device.

## 4. The teachable traits

### 4.1 State the thesis early

The reader should know the claim within the first 150 words of a short post or the first 250 words of a long-form article, and never later than the third paragraph. State it in one sentence that the reader could repeat, with a plain verb of commitment such as "recommend", "find", "argue", or "suggest". McKinsey articles in the archive sample place the thesis in the opening paragraphs, and Harvard Business Review features sometimes delay it behind a short scene, but both deliver it within about 300 words.

> "Our research suggests that assumption is wrong."

- Source: Briscoe and DesJardine, "Research: The True Cost of Corporate Scandals? Talent.", Harvard Business Review, September 21, 2026. [hbr.org](https://hbr.org/2026/09/research-the-true-cost-of-corporate-scandals-talent). Fact-checked. The article opens with the received view of regulatory penalties, and this sentence overturns it at about word 60.

### 4.2 Open with a situation, a complication, a question, and an answer

Begin with a situation that the reader already accepts. Introduce the complication, which is what has changed or what is going wrong. The complication raises a question in the reader's mind, and the article answers it. Barbara Minto's own site calls the device the Situation, Complication, Question framework, and consultants add the Answer to make SCQA. The question usually stays implicit.

> "That close relationship is being disrupted."

- Source: Ertel and Gordon, "Points of Law" (cited by short title), Harvard Business Review, July to August 2012. [hbr.org](https://hbr.org/2012/07/points-of-law-unbundling-corporate-legal-services-to-unlock-value). Fact-checked. The sentence arrives at about word 95, after a paragraph on the traditional relationship between law firms and corporate legal departments, and the authors' answer follows two paragraphs later.

### 4.3 Put the answer first

Place the conclusion before the support, both in the article as a whole and inside each section. Legal training teaches writers to build toward a conclusion, so this is the habit that writers from a legal background most need to reverse. A 2014 Harvard Business Review article on business writing quotes David McCombie, who went from Harvard Law School to McKinsey and was told to reach the point more quickly. Minto's principle gives the structure: ideas are "organized as a pyramid under a single point".

> "One of the great diseases of business writing is postponing the message to the middle part of the writing"

- Source: Bryan Garner, quoted in O'Hara, "How to Improve Your Business Writing", Harvard Business Review, November 20, 2014. [hbr.org](https://hbr.org/2014/11/how-to-improve-your-business-writing). Fact-checked.

### 4.4 Count the framework, and keep the labels

Preview the framework as a counted list ("three decisions", "four failure modes"). Deliver it in the same order, and use the same labels in the preview, the headings, and the close. The count tells the reader how much is coming, and the stable labels allow a reader to find a part again.

> "Decision 2: Decide Where Human Authority Remains"

- Source: a section heading in Shunko and Netessine, "Stop Automating Old Processes. Design New Ones Instead.", Harvard Business Review, September 14, 2026. [hbr.org](https://hbr.org/2026/09/stop-automating-old-processes-design-new-ones-instead). Fact-checked. The article previews three decisions and then uses the numbered labels as headings. Harvard Business Review sets headings in title case, and our house style uses sentence case.

### 4.5 Write headings that carry a message

A heading or an exhibit title states a finding or an instruction. A reader who reads only the headings should be able to reconstruct the argument. One secondary source on consulting practice advises that an action title should fit "within one or max two lines, up to 15 words" (Slideworks, updated September 20, 2023). The archive sample qualifies the usual claim about McKinsey. Its survey articles and its exhibit titles state findings in full sentences, whereas several of its practice articles use plain topic labels. The instruction to the drafting agent is therefore to write message-bearing headings because they serve the reader, and the agent should not assume that every McKinsey article does so.

> "Many AI uses by legal groups have already been proven"

- Source: a figure title in Heric and Goldman, "Corporate Legal Eagles Start to Embrace Artificial Intelligence", Bain & Company, February 5, 2019. [bain.com](https://www.bain.com/insights/corporate-legal-eagles-start-to-embrace-artificial-intelligence/). Fact-checked. A section heading in the same article reads "Still lagging other functions".

### 4.6 Keep sentences and paragraphs at a medium length

The fact-checker's recount of eight Harvard Business Review articles found mean sentence lengths from 15.2 to 27.8 words, and the archive sample of eight McKinsey articles averaged 22.9. Bain's 2019 article averaged 76.9 words per paragraph, the other publications in the sample fell between 50 and 77, and the McKinsey sample fell between 60 and 76. Short sentences appear in every article, and the useful ones carry content: a topic sentence, a finding, or a definition. Our house targets are an average of roughly 15 to 25 words per sentence and paragraphs of 50 to 100 words.

> "Governance is one barrier to scaling."

- Source: "How to Architect for Agentic AI", Bain & Company, May 29, 2026. [bain.com](https://www.bain.com/insights/how-to-architect-for-agentic-ai/). Researcher-checked. This six-word topic sentence states a claim, and the rest of its paragraph proves the claim.

### 4.7 Attribute statistics inline, with sample and date

The source, the instrument, the sample, and the date belong in the sentence that carries the number, and survey results are reported as what respondents said. McKinsey works differently. In the archive sample it names its own surveys in the text, places the sample and the field dates in an "About the research" box, and often leaves third-party sources to footnotes. Web articles without that apparatus would be left with orphan statistics, so our house rule follows Harvard Business Review. For any survey that Spaarke runs itself, the McKinsey box is worth borrowing.

> "BCG's January 2026 'AI Radar,' surveying 640 CEOs across 16 markets, reports that corporations expect to roughly double AI spending this year, from 0.8% of revenue to about 1.7%."

- Source: Shunko and Netessine, Harvard Business Review, September 14, 2026 (link in section 4.4). Fact-checked. The fact-checker confirmed the Harvard Business Review sentence and did not examine BCG's underlying report. The inner quotation marks are shown here as single marks.

### 4.8 Interpret the number

A figure is followed by a sentence that says what it shows, what it cannot show, or what it should be compared with. Bain's 2019 article gives an example of comparison. It reports that 20% to 25% of legal departments used AI in at least one area, according to a LexisNexis survey, and it sets that figure beside 40% in finance and 54% in human resources. The survey is seven years old and should not be reused as a current figure.

> "Those figures demonstrate the scale of the redesigned workflow, not by themselves its business value"

- Source: Shunko and Netessine, Harvard Business Review, September 14, 2026 (link in section 4.4). Fact-checked. The sentence follows two large operating figures and tells the reader what those figures do not yet prove.

### 4.9 Tell cases in one paragraph, named and quantified

A case takes one paragraph and has three beats: the situation, the action, and the measured result, which is given as a before-and-after figure. Use one case per point. When the organization cannot be named, describe it with specifics. Deloitte Insights, for example, describes "a leading European telecommunications provider" that consolidated four knowledge silos serving 19 million customers (June 18, 2026; [deloitte.com](https://www.deloitte.com/us/en/insights/topics/talent/knowledge-management-plan.html)).

> "Coca-Cola launched a self-service portal that reduced contract-drafting time for many matters from up to 10 hours to around 15 minutes."

- Source: Heric and Goldman, Bain & Company, February 5, 2019 (link in section 4.5). Fact-checked.

### 4.10 Allow one calibrated hedge per claim

Be exact about what is known, and then commit. The hedge belongs on the verb ("suggests", "tends to", "appears to"), and hedges are never stacked. Firm-published research hedges more heavily than Harvard Business Review authors do, and the heavier style is not a model. The Deloitte article quoted here also says that organizations "could be up to 88 times more likely" to achieve a high return, a phrase that manages to be both hedged and extravagant.

> "Taken together, these patterns suggest that AI-driven business outcomes peak when decision rights are distributed among complementary leaders."

- Source: "How the right mix of C-suite leadership can drive outsized AI returns", Deloitte Insights, December 18, 2025. [deloitte.com](https://www.deloitte.com/us/en/insights/topics/digital-transformation/c-suite-leadership-ai-returns.html). Fact-checked.

### 4.11 Use "we" and "you" deliberately

"We" refers to the authors as researchers, as experienced advisers, and as people who take a position. In the McKinsey archive sample, "we", "our", and "us" appear 3.3 times per 1,000 words, and "you" is close to absent, because readers are addressed by role. Harvard Business Review columns address "you" throughout. Our house rule takes the McKinsey pattern with one allowance. "We" is used for Spaarke's evidence and positions, readers are described by role in analysis, and "you" is used for direct advice, checklists, and diagnostic questions.

> "Our final sample covers 735 of the largest U.S. public companies over eight years"

- Source: Briscoe and DesJardine, Harvard Business Review, September 21, 2026 (link in section 4.1). Fact-checked. The authors speak as researchers and disclose their method in one plain sentence.

### 4.12 Refer to exhibits in parentheses

The sentence states the finding, and the exhibit reference follows in parentheses at the end of that sentence. The exhibit's own title states the finding as a full sentence. The text never tells the reader that a chart "below" shows something. McKinsey writes "(Exhibit 1)", or "(exhibit)" when an article has only one, and Deloitte Insights writes "(figure 1)". Our house form is "(Exhibit 1)".

> "44 percent now report that AI is scaling across their enterprise, up from 38 percent a year ago (Exhibit 1)."

- Source: an excerpt from a longer sentence in Tinkoff, Van der Veken, and Chui, "The state of AI in 2026: On the road to ROI", McKinsey, August 25, 2026. [mckinsey.com](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/quantumblack/our-insights/the-state-of-ai). Archive snapshot. McKinsey spells out "percent", and our house style keeps the % sign.

### 4.13 End on consequence

The final section is short. It returns to the stakes, says what follows for the reader, and stops. It does not summarize, and it carries no "Conclusion" heading. McKinsey articles in the archive sample often close on a sentence about which organizations will succeed. That formula should be rationed, because machine-written prose relies on it as well.

> "In a future crowded with agents, speed to market will matter, but not as much as trust, governance, and human capabilities."

- Source: "How will AI agents fit into the human workplace?", Deloitte Insights, September 10, 2026. [deloitte.com](https://www.deloitte.com/us/en/insights/topics/technology-management/ai-agents-human-workplace.html). Fact-checked. It is the last sentence before the article's methodology note.

## 5. Where the models differ, and the house choice

The following table compares the two main models on the conventions where they differ, and it gives the house choice for each. The McKinsey column rests on the archive sample described in section 3.

| Convention | McKinsey | Harvard Business Review | House choice |
|---|---|---|---|
| Percentages | The word "percent" (138 uses of the word and none of the sign in eight articles) | A numeral with the % sign | A numeral with the % sign, as in the published library |
| Em dash | Used, with a median of 4.7 per 1,000 words | Used, from 0 to 8.4 per 1,000 words | Never used |
| Heading case | Sentence case | Title case | Sentence case |
| Thesis placement | The first paragraph | Often after two or three paragraphs of scene | Within 150 words for a short post and 250 words for a long-form article, and by the third paragraph |
| Attribution of statistics | Split between the text, footnotes, and a research box | Inline | Inline |
| Address to the reader | By role, with "you" rare | "You" throughout | By role in analysis, with "you" for advice |
| Exhibit reference | "(Exhibit 1)" at the end of the sentence | "As shown in exhibit 1" in running text | "(Exhibit 1)" at the end of the sentence |
| Contractions | Light | Free in digital pieces | Sparing in articles; none in white papers or in passages that report research |
| Corrective frame | Rare | Frequent | Not used (style guide, section 5, rule 14) |
| Close | One or two paragraphs that look forward | A consequence, often a contrasted pair | A consequence, without a clipped pair |

## 6. Model passages

The five passages were written for this file, in Spaarke's subject area, at the standard the style guide sets. Every statistic in them is illustrative, and a source name in square brackets is a placeholder. Do not reuse the figures.

### 6.1 Opening: situation, complication, answer

> Most large corporate legal departments now have a legal operations function. Over the past decade the role has acquired a recognized scope: outside counsel management, e-billing, matter intake, technology, and reporting to the general counsel. CLOC and the Association of Corporate Counsel both publish frameworks that describe these responsibilities in detail, and most directors know them well.
>
> The frameworks describe what the function does. They say much less about how the function decides, and that gap has become expensive. Suppose a CFO asks whether $4.2 million was a reasonable amount to spend on employment litigation last year. The answer depends on matter data, invoice data, and outcome data. In most departments those records sit in three systems that were never designed to be read together.
>
> We argue that managing legal operations now means managing that shared data as a single asset. Doing so requires three decisions. Someone must own the matter taxonomy, one system must hold the record of each matter, and outcomes must be recorded when a matter closes.

What it does: the first paragraph gives a situation that the reader accepts, the second introduces the complication through a concrete case, and the third states the answer and previews a counted framework. The thesis arrives at about word 140, inside the limit for a short post and well inside the limit for a long-form article. Sentences average about 17 words, and the short ones are short because they carry a point. The three decisions should reappear as headings, and the dollar figure is illustrative.

### 6.2 Opening: a tension between two numbers

> In [survey name]'s 2026 survey of 412 corporate legal departments, 71% of respondents said that their department had deployed a generative AI assistant. Only 18% said that the assistant could retrieve the department's own precedents and playbooks. The distance between those two figures is the subject of this article. An assistant that cannot reach the department's prior work answers from general knowledge, which outside counsel and every competitor can also obtain. It saves individual lawyers time, but it does not change how the department performs.
>
> Our position is that knowledge management has become the limiting factor in legal AI. Many departments allowed the discipline to lapse after earlier document-management projects disappointed, and they are now paying for that decision. The sections that follow explain why retrieval depends on curation and describe a workable knowledge program for a department of 20 to 60 professionals. They close with two investments that should come before any further AI licensing.

What it does: the first sentence attributes two figures to a named instrument with its sample and year and reports them as what respondents said. The gap between the figures is the complication, and the second paragraph commits to a position with "Our position is that". The roadmap lists three real parts of the article across two sentences. Sentences average about 20 words. The figures are illustrative.

### 6.3 Evidence paragraph: a statistic introduced and interpreted

> Invoice review is the control that most departments rely on, and its reach is narrower than its reputation suggests. In [study name]'s 2026 benchmarking study of 380 legal departments, respondents reported that line-item review adjusted a median of 3.1% of billed fees, and 64% of the adjustments corrected rate or timekeeper errors. These figures show that review catches mistakes in billing. They cannot show whether the work was staffed sensibly, because a correctly billed invoice for an overstaffed deposition passes every rule. A department that wants to influence the larger cost has to act earlier, through staffing plans and budgets agreed when the matter opens.

What it does: the topic sentence makes the claim, the second sentence attributes the statistic inline, and the third and fourth sentences say what the numbers show and what they cannot show. The final sentence draws the consequence. The paragraph runs to about 100 words, and the figures are illustrative.

### 6.4 Transition between sections

> If the matter taxonomy determines which questions a department can answer from its own data, then ownership of the taxonomy is a management decision before it is a technology decision. Departments usually assign that ownership to one of three places: the legal operations team, the e-billing administrator, or the practice group leads. Each choice produces a different failure pattern, and the pattern becomes visible in the department's reports within a year. The most common choice, the e-billing administrator, also fails in the most predictable way.

What it does: the conditional clause carries forward what the previous section established, without the words "as we have seen". The paragraph then counts what comes next, and its last sentence leads into the first subsection. It neither announces the section nor recaps it.

### 6.5 Closing: consequence

> The cost of informal intake grows with the department. A team of 12 can hold its open requests in memory and in a shared mailbox. A team of 40 cannot. The first visible symptom is usually a business unit that stops asking for early advice, because nobody can say when an answer will arrive.
>
> General counsel who plan to add headcount in 2027 should settle the intake process first. Otherwise each new lawyer inherits a queue that nobody can see. A department that can measure its intake can show the CFO where the next lawyer is needed, and that evidence makes a stronger budget case than a description of workload that only the legal team can feel.

What it does: the close states what follows for the reader (a decision about sequence) and adds nothing by way of summary. The short sentences carry facts ("A team of 40 cannot" completes a comparison), and none of them exists only to land the sentence before it. The final sentence ends on the stakes for the general counsel. Sentences average about 17 words. The close contains no pitch and no restatement.

## 7. How to use this file

Read sections 4 and 6 before drafting, and match a draft's opening, evidence paragraphs, transitions, and close against the model passages. The two openings in section 6 are options and not a template, so vary the shape of the opening across the pieces in a series. Do not pattern-match against articles published before September 2026. They predate the revision of the style guide, and they use constructions that it now prohibits. Where they conflict with this file or with the style guide, the style guide governs.
