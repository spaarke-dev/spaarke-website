# Stance: the posture behind the prose

`style-guide.md` says how a sentence sounds. `brand-positioning.md` says
what we argue about Spaarke. This file says where the writer stands:
toward the evidence, toward the causes, toward the reader, and toward
the other parties in the story. On sentence mechanics the style guide
governs; on posture this file governs.

Each rule below ends with the sentence from the pipeline draft that the
published article replaced, and with the published sentence too where
the rule does not already quote it. "Published" is
`content/blog/2026-06-16-managing-legal-operations.mdx`, rewritten by
the writer in September 2026 and approved as house voice. "The draft it
replaced" is the same article at commit `e72f68f`. Both are real text.

The quoted sentences show a rule being followed in one article, and they
are evidence rather than wording to reuse. Article-specific analytical
material stays in the article it was written for, and the rule is what
carries across. Where a rule here forbids something the writer
published, treat the published text as evidence that the rule needs
revising, and raise it rather than resolving it in a draft. One
occurrence is evidence and not yet a ruling, and
`examples/house-exemplar.md`, section 5, records the questions that are
still open on that basis.

---

## 1. Standing

We run legal operations and we use research to corroborate. We do not
write as people who are careful about what the research licenses.
Practitioner observation is evidence. Where a claim comes from running
the work and no survey measures it, state it flat in the present tense
with the mechanism that makes it true, and move on (`style-guide.md`,
section 5, rule 12).

The author carries the risk of saying things no survey measures. That
risk is the point of publishing under our own name, and a piece that
pins every claim to a survey has outsourced its judgment to whoever ran
the survey.

> **Published:** "Adoption is visible but not yet well measured."
>
> **The draft it replaced:** "No neutral adoption statistic exists for
> front doors, intake portals, or non-disclosure agreement self-service
> in 2025 or 2026. Adoption is therefore visible and unmeasured, and no
> number belongs in a sentence about it."

## 2. Who holds which authority

Three parties appear in almost everything we publish, and the verbs are
not interchangeable.

**The general counsel and the department** own the risk positions, the
terms of any delegation, and the decision itself. They decide, own, set,
approve, and accept.

**Legal operations** builds what makes those decisions workable and
consistent: the processes, the tools, the information, the playbooks,
the templates, the workflows, the routing rules, the risk thresholds,
the escalation paths, and the data that returns from every channel. It
facilitates, catalyzes, builds, curates, manages, and enables. "Owns"
and "decides" are not available to it in a sentence about legal
judgment.

The shape is: "The department sets the terms of that delegation. Legal
operations builds what makes the delegation workable." Writing that
legal operations decides what the business may do overstates the
function to the reader who holds it, and loses the general counsel
reading over their shoulder. It puts the function in competition with
its own principal for authority a legal operations director does not
claim and a general counsel will not concede. The pipeline draft wrote
that sentence five times, because section 2 of `style-guide.md` printed
it as the model sentence for the active voice.

**Business teams** manage certain legal-related activities that are
low-risk, clearly understood and scoped, inside guardrails the
department sets, with the position taken recorded. Legal risk never
attaches to the business as something it holds, participates in, shares,
or takes on. A general counsel reads risk-sharing language as legal risk
leaving the department, which is the opposite of the argument.

> **The draft it replaced:** "The decision about what the business may
> complete on its own therefore sits with legal operations."

## 3. The prime mover

When several forces act at once, name in the thesis sentence the one
force driving the others and make the rest grammatically subordinate to
it: "Driven by the AI imperative, general counsel and their departments
are reimagining how legal services are delivered."

An article whose spine is a numbered list of drivers gives the reader a
survey to agree with in place of a thesis to accept or reject. If an
idea or a brief arrives with a numbered list of causes, that list is the
raw material for the ranking, not the outline. Call the prime mover a
force; call what it produces a shift, a trend, or a development.

> **The draft it replaced:** "Six drivers push those changes in the same
> direction. The largest is a new view of the resource requirements of
> legal and legal operations, driven by AI."

---

## 4. The concession we make

We say plainly that legal can become an impediment to the business, and
we give the concession a mechanism instead of leaving it as an
adjective. Name the delay or the omission, say what it costs the
business, and say what the business does next when it recurs. The
mechanism is what makes the concession credible to the reader whose
function it describes.

We say it about the function, never about a named department or person,
we put it in the department's own mouth where we can ("departments
increasingly acknowledge that..."), and we pair it with the remedy in
the same section. Conceding it is what earns us the right to argue for
self-service and delegation to a legal operations reader. Where an
argument proposes a remedy, the fault it remedies is stated first, as a
direct predicate with the reader's own function as the subject.

What stays off the table is unchanged: taking sides between in-house and
firms, on the billable hour, or on whether legal is "really" strategic
(`domain-knowledge.md`, section 7).

> **Published:** "Opening the model also addresses a harder
> acknowledgment: legal can become an impediment. A business unit that
> waits in the department's queue for a standard non-disclosure
> agreement loses time, and a business that routinely waits learns to
> work around the department."
>
> **Published, the same concession from the other side:** "Legal is too
> often brought in after the decisions that shape a risk have been made,
> when issues it could have identified and addressed early have become
> harder and costlier to resolve."
>
> **The draft it replaced:** "The purpose of the guardrails, the
> thresholds, the playbooks, the automated and AI-assisted workflows,
> and the escalation paths is to take legal out of the bottleneck
> wherever technology can manage the risk. That is what legal operations
> means by legal as an enabler of the business."

## 5. State the other party's need as its own claim

The business's claim on legal work is a legitimate need, not a
permission the department dispenses. Put the need in the business's own
subject position, attach the department's control to it as a proviso
rather than as the frame, and add, where it is true, that the department
wants the same thing.

Permission verbs ("may complete", "may accept") describe the mechanism
and should not carry the frame.

> **Published:** "Business teams need the flexibility to manage certain
> legal-related activities and risks themselves, provided they are
> clearly understood and scoped, and inside guardrails the department
> sets."
>
> **Published, the department's own demand:** "The demand does not come
> from the business alone. Legal departments themselves recognize that
> certain low-risk legal-related activities can be handled properly by
> the business directly, or with minimal input from the department."
>
> **The draft it replaced:** "The business is taking a direct part in
> its legal risk through the front door and self-service, and legal
> operations decides which work it may complete alone, within which
> thresholds, and with what escalation path."

## 6. Name the obligation on both sides of a gap

Where a piece reports a gap between two parties, name the obligation on
each side in the same section, including the one that falls on the
reader. Report the source's prescription where it is useful; do not
adopt the source's stance as ours.

Article 1 is the worked case. The 86% of general counsel who see legal
as a significant contributor against the 17% of the C-suite who agree is
not only a reporting problem the reader fixes with better metrics. Legal
is also too often brought in too late.

> **Published:** "Visibility also works in the other direction. Legal is
> too often brought in after the decisions that shape a risk have been
> made, when issues it could have identified and addressed early have
> become harder and costlier to resolve."
>
> **The draft it replaced:** "Recognition inside the business has not
> kept up with what the department contributes. Thomson Reuters calls
> the distance between the 86% of general counsel and the 17% of
> C-suite respondents a visibility gap." The draft then passed the
> source's metrics prescription through as ours and asked nothing of
> legal.

## 7. Spend the sentence on what is newly possible

When restating a constraint the reader already lives with, concede that
it is familiar and spend the sentence on what has changed. "Budget
pressure is not new, but the options for responding to it are. A
department that once could only ration its lawyers' time can now
redesign which work requires a lawyer at all."

Do not write "Closing the distance between those two lines is what a
legal operations director is asked to do, and in most departments adding
people is no longer among the available methods", which restates the
burden and hands it back. Our readers know their constraints. They are
reading us for the new option.

Both sentences above are real. The first is the published article and
the second is the draft it replaced.

---

## 8. Recommend where there is a choice

A section that lays out two courses and calls the choice "a design
decision the reader has to make" has analysed where it should have
recommended (`ai-tells.md`, section 6.5). Name one as the better answer
and say what it consists of: "The better answer is to agree in advance
which activities the business can handle and on what terms, with legal
still part of the decision."

> **The draft it replaced:** "Meeting that expectation or declining it
> is itself a design decision, and the business tends to work around a
> department that declines it."

## 9. Our position on AI: two claims, never one without the other

**The macro claim.** AI is the force reshaping how the corporate legal
department delivers legal work, and operating models are being rebuilt
around what it now makes possible. State it as a claim about the
department and its operating model, and never as a claim about a
product.

**The tool claim.** Today's AI has limits that a legal reader already
suspects, and we name them in our own voice. Say what the limitation is,
say what legal work requires that the limitation does not supply, and
then recommend the deterministic alternative by name and not as a
fallback, listing the parts of it the department would actually build.
A path that returns the same result every time is easier for both sides
to accept than one that does not.

Never publish one without the other. A piece carrying only the macro
claim reads as vendor enthusiasm; a piece carrying only the tool claim
reads as a competitor attack. This is the article-level form of the "not
AI-first" position in `brand-positioning.md`, section 5.

The wording below is article 1's. Each piece states both claims in the
terms its own argument needs, and names the limitation that bears on the
work it is about.

> **Published, the macro claim:** "AI is the defining force on the
> corporate legal department, and legal operating models are realigning
> to take advantage of the new capabilities."
>
> **Published, the tool claim:** "AI carries a limitation of its own,
> and users should know it before they rely on one: probabilistic
> outputs can be variable, inconsistent, and inaccurate. Legal decisions
> often require a level of precision that is not well matched to the
> limitations of today's AI tools."
>
> **Published, the deterministic alternative named:** "Established
> workflow and forms-based processes, such as a standard non-disclosure
> agreement template, a guided intake form, and a defined approval path,
> combined with regular collaboration between the business and legal,
> are often the better fit."
>
> **The draft it replaced:** "Deciding which answers a system may give
> without a lawyer is the difficult part of that design, because a
> generative model returns a different answer to the same question on a
> second run." The draft carried the tool observation as a design
> obstacle and made no macro claim at all.
