Ontology is a term every legal operations leader should know.

Every legal department runs on systems of record: matter management, e-billing, contract management, document management. Each one does its job well. An e-billing platform, for example, records every invoice line with a description, hours, rates and often a reference to the work product behind it.

What no single system provides is the broader context across them. That means the matter's overall health, its risk assessment, its likely resolution timing, and quick access to the work product and correspondence behind each line. That context is spread across systems, inboxes and people. The reasoning behind the department's decisions, such as why a scope change was approved or an exception granted, rarely sits anywhere a system can use it.

An ontology brings that context together. It's a governed model of the department's matters, agreements, people, obligations, documents and workflows, connected across the systems already in place. It defines the relationships between them, the actions the department may take, and the rules that govern those actions. It's the foundation of a legal operations intelligence platform.

It's also the department's intellectual capital. It holds how the department defines its work, which positions it accepts and what it has decided, in a form that people, reports and AI can all use. AI models will change. The ontology belongs to the department, and it has to be protected.

Our new article covers what a legal ontology contains, what makes it different from generic enterprise models, and how to build one around the decisions legal actually makes.
