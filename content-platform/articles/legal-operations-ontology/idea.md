## The one-sentence topic

What an ontology is when it serves as the foundation of legal
operations intelligence: a model of the entities a legal department
works on, the relationships among them, and the actions it may take on
them, bound to the systems where the department's data sits, which
makes information actionable for people, for business intelligence,
and for AI agents.

## The argument (what the reader should walk away believing)

1. **The ontology is the foundation of legal operations intelligence
   (F2).** Article 2 describes the platform as the department's
   capabilities across process, people, and technology, with
   technology as the tangible instantiation. This piece argues that
   those capabilities stand on a shared model of the department's
   work, which people, business intelligence, and AI agents all read
   from and act through. A data lake stores data, and a data warehouse
   organizes data for reporting. An ontology models the entities, the
   relationships between them, and the actions permitted on them, so
   it sits alongside the lake and the warehouse and replaces neither.
   The article makes this three-way distinction in one paragraph and
   does not use a feature table.
2. **An ontology is an entity and action model (F1, F3).** The plain
   definition for this piece: an ontology is a model of the things a
   legal department works on, the attributes of those things, the
   relationships among them, the actions that may be taken on them,
   and the rules that govern those actions, bound to the systems
   where the data already sits. The definition uses "relationships";
   after the definition the article may use "links" as the shorter
   working word (the writer's answer to rev. 3 question 3). The
   binding is Spaarke's design principle, and the article does not
   attribute live or no-copy behaviour to any researched source.
   Microsoft Learn informs the definition and may be named: it defines
   an ontology as "a shared, machine-understandable vocabulary of your
   business", made of things, facts, and connections, kept consistent
   by constraints and rules, and linked by data bindings to the
   organization's data sources (in Microsoft's product, sources in
   OneLake). The same page says that changes in upstream data must be
   refreshed manually before they appear in the ontology item. The
   operational platform model research (`notes/palantir-intelligence-platform-model.md`)
   adds decisions to that vocabulary. Stated from the legal
   department's side, the model holds, for each decision the
   department takes, the rule that evaluated it, the person who
   released it, and the outcome, alongside the records the decision
   was about; that is why actions, the logic that evaluates them, and
   the controls that govern them belong inside the model. The article
   states that idea in Spaarke's own words and attributes it to no
   one, and neither the article nor the plan reuses the source's
   headline construction ("represents the decisions ... as well as
   the data") in any wording. The writer's
   decision is that the article does not name that company anywhere,
   so the one neutral clause that v1 permitted is removed, and the
   ban on discussing its commercial model becomes moot (C6, C21,
   C24). The series vocabulary is *entities and actions*, and v1's
   "objects" becomes "entities" throughout. The three-part build in
   v1 stays: the entities, with matter, project, and invoice as the
   spine; the relationships, where v1's point that every department
   has a matter table and few have the connections still holds; and
   the actions, each carrying authority, provenance, and a gate
   (automatic release, a single confirmation, or counsel review).
   Policy and obligation remain first-class entities.
3. **Its purpose is to make information actionable (F4, F8).** The
   second belief in v1 stands in substance, and C25 rewords it so
   that the report and the dashboard are no longer its foil. The unit
   of value is a governed decision. A dashboard is a legitimate BI
   output, and it does its job when it shows that a matter is over
   budget. The reader must still work out which decision is due,
   gather the supporting information from other systems, and leave
   the tool to act. An entity and action model attaches state, rules,
   and available actions to the entity itself. The decision that is
   required, the information needed to make it, and the means to act
   therefore arrive in one place, and the outcome is recorded against
   the same entity. The worked example in v1 (the budget inquiry, run
   twice) carries this point and remains the longest section.
4. **The ontology aligns with business intelligence and works in
   conjunction with it (F5).** Business intelligence is the
   deterministic dimension of the platform: it computes facts such as
   the budget variance the same way every time. Article 4 develops
   that argument at length, and it carries a later display date, so
   this piece makes the complementary point itself in one section and
   points to article 4 only in the navigation block. The ontology
   gives BI consistent entities to read across systems, gives the
   department somewhere to act on what BI shows, and returns the
   record of decisions and outcomes to BI as new input (the outside
   counsel report card in v1's example). Two Microsoft sources dated
   before the cut-off corroborate the pairing. The Fabric IQ overview
   (revision of 2026-07-09) lists business intelligence and
   operational intelligence as separate layers of business context,
   beside unified data, and notes that an ontology can be generated
   from a BI semantic model already in production. The Build
   announcement of 2026-06-02 says that ontologies "extend semantic
   models by adding operational context". The reading that the
   ontology item supplies the operational layer is the researcher's
   mapping, and the article presents it as a reading. One
   practitioner's distinction is also usable: a BI semantic layer
   standardizes measurement, and an ontology standardizes meaning.
5. **AI has raised what an ontology architecture can do, and AI agents
   need one (F6).** The argument runs in two directions. AI lowers the
   cost of building and maintaining the model, and agents raise the
   value of having it. The article develops six mechanisms, each with
   a source listed in the evidence section.
   - *Grounding.* An agent that reads governed entities and
     deterministic facts has less room to invent them.
   - *A shared vocabulary across several agents and tools.* Every
     agent that shares the model uses the same definitions and rules,
     which addresses the difficulty that a department running
     several AI tools faces when each tool defines "matter" in its
     own way. The pre-cut-off Microsoft source for this point is the
     Fabric IQ overview's sentence that its three layers "ensure that
     every agent starts with the same understanding of the business";
     the agent-integration page that rev. 3 quoted is out.
   - *Questions asked in business terms.* People and agents ask about
     matters, firms, and obligations, and the model translates the
     question into queries against the underlying tables.
   - *Extraction and entity resolution from unstructured documents.*
     Language models can now pull parties, clauses, dates, and
     obligations out of documents and match them to existing entities,
     which used to require manual abstraction. Brief v1 rightly
     treats identity resolution across systems as an open
     architectural question, so the article states it as a requirement
     and does not describe it as a shipped capability.
   - *Actions exposed to agents as governed tools.* An agent receives
     the same defined actions a person has, with the same
     preconditions, permissions, and audit record, in place of open
     write access to the underlying systems. The neutral sources are
     the MCP specification's own guidance (a human in the loop able to
     deny tool invocations; confirmation prompts) and Microsoft's
     how-to page on exposing an ontology as an MCP server.
   - *Human review of proposed actions.* An agent stages an action and
     a person with authority releases it. Latitude widens as the
     department gains confidence. This is v1's release envelope, and
     it is what "AI-directed, human-controlled" means in structural
     terms. The article states this as Spaarke's design and cites no
     vendor's default for it.

   The third belief in v1 follows from these mechanisms. An AI model
   can be replaced through configuration, whereas the semantic work
   that makes a department's data intelligible takes years and belongs
   to the department.
6. **The ontology integrates the information and context of the
   systems already in place into the platform (F7).** The settled
   positioning governs this section: Spaarke provides both system of
   record capabilities and ontology architecture over third-party
   systems, and the article supports both modes without setting one
   against the other. Where a department keeps its document
   management system and its e-billing platform, those systems own
   the documents and the invoices, and the ontology binds each entity
   to the system where the data lives and references what it does not
   own. Where a department uses Spaarke as the system of record for
   the process entities that no other system holds (the request, the
   disposition, the obligation, the policy, the inquiry, and the
   action record), the model and the record live in one place. The
   honest point stays in either mode: an ontology binds to systems it
   does not own wherever that is how a department deploys it, and the
   article says so plainly. The earlier principle "originate the
   workflow, reference the record" and v1's ban on consolidation
   claims are withdrawn (C15). The "context graph" point belongs
   here. The two legal announcements of May 2026 cover document
   management content and activity. Neither mentions spend, e-billing,
   outside counsel management, or matter management. Context drawn
   from documents is therefore one layer of the model, and the
   department's operations reach further. The article makes this
   observation generically, names neither vendor, and argues the idea
   on its merits; the research found no published critique that makes
   the point.
7. **Building it depends on deep knowledge of process workflows,
   information architecture, and the context of business decisions
   (F9).** The build starts from a decision the department has to
   make, where many data projects start from a source system or a
   dashboard request. It models the department's reality in the
   department's own terms, because a model that copies each system's
   tables column by column reproduces the fragmentation it was meant
   to resolve. It defines each entity once, binds it to the systems
   where the data lives, adds the relationships, and then adds
   policies and gated actions. It grows one use case at a time and is
   tested against the questions that leaders repeatedly ask their
   teams. Each of those steps requires people who know how intake,
   matter management, invoice review, and outside counsel management
   actually run, where the information sits and in what shape, and
   what a general counsel or a finance partner needs in order to
   decide. Independent observers of the general-purpose offerings make
   the same point: the tooling does not remove the hard work, and it
   does not create agreement on definitions where none exists. The
   five structural differences in v1 (privilege travels with the
   entity, the document is often the record, counterparty paper is an
   adversarial source, rule density is extreme, and ethical walls
   partition the graph) are the legal expression of this knowledge.
   Existing legal standards supply vocabulary and exchange formats
   that the model uses and does not replace: SALI's matter taxonomy
   supplies values for matter attributes, and LEDES and UTBMS define
   the shape of spend facts. The build method draws on the
   operational platform model research for background, and the
   article states each step in its own words with no attribution.
   This section respects v1's exclusions: no roadmap, no waves, no
   binding modes as a commercial claim, and no internal layer or
   entity names.
8. **APIs and the Model Context Protocol make access and
   bi-directional flows practical, which makes vendor openness a
   condition of customer success (F10).** The writer's answer to
   rev. 3 question 4 gives this argument one section; an expanded
   article on the subject will follow. An entity and action model
   needs to read from the systems of record and, where an action
   completes in one of them, to write back through an interface that
   system governs. Between May and early July 2026 the legal solution
   providers and one frontier model provider moved quickly in that
   direction. Document management, e-discovery, agreement, legal
   research, and matter management providers published MCP servers or
   appeared as connectors. The confirmed write examples are
   administrative and workflow actions, and they rest on two kinds of
   source. The Box, Datasite, Relativity, and Docusign examples
   (create or update content; set up folder structures and invite
   users; stand up matters and shape workspace schema; orchestrate
   agreement workflows) are connector descriptions on the model
   provider's launch page, so they are Anthropic's words about those
   connectors. The LawVu examples (create matters, trigger contract
   workflows, update matter status, create tasks, set deadlines) are
   in the vendor's own launch article, stated in the future tense for
   the initial release. The iManage release describes governed access
   to content and states no write action. The article attributes each
   example to the source that states it and stays accurate on three
   limits. First, the research could not confirm whether several
   connectors are read-only, so the article applies no read-only or
   write-capable label beyond what the cited source states. Second,
   the research found no confirmed example of an agent writing spend
   data into an e-billing or matter management system of record; the
   only spend connector found is dated after the display date.
   Third, the case that legal solution vendors must provide open
   environments is voiced mainly by vendors that ship connectors, by
   the trade press, and by individual practitioners; the research
   found no formal statement from CLOC, ACC, or ILTA. The article
   makes the case as Spaarke's own argument: a department can succeed
   with an entity and action model only if the systems it depends on
   expose documented, permission-aware interfaces for reading and
   writing. It also gives the counter-positions one paragraph: a
   content provider that is selective about MCP on grounds of
   authority and attribution (LexisNexis, 2026-06-10); the security
   responsibilities the protocol leaves to implementers and operators,
   drawn from the specification of 2025-06-18 (authorization is
   optional; a human in the loop should be able to deny tool
   invocations; annotations are untrusted unless the server is
   trusted), the maintainers' post of 2026-03-16 (a tool with no
   annotations is assumed potentially destructive, and clients vary in
   how strictly they honour the defaults), and the release candidate
   of 2026-05-21 that hardened authorization and was due to become
   final on 2026-07-28; and the new dependence that forms one layer
   up when a department's playbooks are encoded inside a single AI
   provider's plugin format (ComplexDiscovery, 2026-05-13). In v1's
   worked example nothing writes to the e-billing system, and that
   remains correct.
9. **Legal departments want more control and the ability to build
   their own solutions, and an owned model is what makes building
   safe (F11).** This argument also gets one section, with an
   expanded article to follow. The commentary on this point is
   consistent, and the documented builds are few. One in-house legal
   operations manager forecasts, in Bloomberg Law, that the teams that
   stand out will build and iterate on solutions quickly. That
   statement is a forecast, and it describes no build. CLOC
   programming appears to assume that some members have already built
   agents: a roundtable page of 2026-02-03 asks whether members have
   built their own agents or are just getting started, and it names
   no builds. That reading is **TBD — confirm**. The CLOC AI
   Intensive was announced after the display date and is out. One AI
   company's own legal department has published what it built. The
   research notes call every signal of a named corporate build weak.
   The research found no corporate legal survey that reports a
   build-versus-buy percentage, no survey that ranks openness or API
   access as a selection criterion, and no verified named build by a
   corporate legal department outside the AI industry. The article
   says so and presents building as an emerging practice. The
   argument connects to v1's third belief. A department's requirements
   are specific to its business, so it will need custom solutions, and
   custom solutions built on a governed entity and action model
   inherit its definitions, permissions, and audit record, whereas
   solutions built directly on each system's tables must establish
   all three again for each tool. The article gives the cautions a
   sentence each: tools built by domain experts with AI assistance are
   often not ready for production use, and someone has to maintain
   them. This is the natural place for the one product reference the
   writer has allowed: Spaarke may be named as providing a Legal
   Operations Intelligence ontology solution, once, stated as a fact
   about what the company offers and without a comparison.
