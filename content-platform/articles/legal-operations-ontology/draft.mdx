Every legal department has a matter table. It also has a document repository, an invoice feed from the e-billing platform, and a reporting layer that reads across them to tell the general counsel what was spent last quarter and which matters are open. Business intelligence does that job well, and most departments have spent a decade making it work. Each of those systems was built around one part of the work, and each holds the part that its own data model has a field for.

What none of them holds is the relationships among those parts. The email thread in which the firm agreed to a revised scope sits in an inbox, unconnected to the matter it changed. The invoice line that records the resulting work sits in the e-billing platform, unconnected to the budget it consumed. The department's matter and the firm's matter are two records that nobody has joined. Nor does any system hold what the department decided when the variance report came back. Someone read the report, wrote to the firm, and noted the outcome in a spreadsheet, so next quarter the reporting layer knows no more about that firm than it knew before.

The foundation of legal operations intelligence is an ontology: a shared model of the entities a department works on, the relationships among them, the actions it may take on them, and the rules that govern those actions. That model is the one layer that people, business intelligence, and AI agents can all read from and act through, and it holds the context across every system the department uses, which is what turns the records those systems hold into insight.

## An ontology adds relationships, actions, and rules to the tables a department already has

The word comes from information science and has arrived in enterprise software over the past year, which makes it worth defining in operational terms before it becomes one more item of vendor vocabulary. An ontology is a model of the things a legal department works on, the attributes of those things, the relationships among them, the actions that may be taken on them, and the rules that govern those actions, bound to the systems where the data already sits. That is the working definition this article uses, and it describes the structure underneath the category set out in [What Is Legal Operations Intelligence?](/why-spaarke/what-is-legal-operations-intelligence).

Two things follow from the definition, and together they are the reason the model is worth the effort it takes. It reaches across every system the department uses, so a question can be answered from records that no one system holds together. Without it the same records are rows that a person has to reconcile before anyone can decide anything. The model is also the one layer that people, business intelligence, and AI agents can all read from and act through. Everything else in legal operations intelligence rests on it: the reports, the agents, the audit record, and the decisions the department has to defend a year later.

The definition is not peculiar to legal work. Microsoft's documentation for its own ontology feature, in the revision of May 14, 2026 that stayed live through the following July, describes an ontology as "a shared, machine-understandable vocabulary of your business". It is made of things, facts, and connections, kept consistent by constraints and rules, and linked by data bindings to the organization's data sources. The same page says that changes made upstream are refreshed manually before they appear. That is a useful corrective to the assumption that binding a model to a source system is the same as reading that system live. Binding is an engineering commitment with a refresh policy attached, and a department should ask what the policy is.

The definition also carries one element that data models built for reporting rarely include. For each decision the department takes, the model holds the rule that evaluated it, the person who released it, and the outcome, alongside the records the decision was about. A budget variance is a computed number in any warehouse. The approval of a scope change, the delegation policy that allowed the approver to approve it, and the write-off that followed are computed nowhere. In most departments they exist only in an inbox and in whatever the approver remembered to type into a spreadsheet.

[How to Build the Legal Operations Intelligence Platform](/why-spaarke/building-the-legal-operations-intelligence-platform) ends by naming five properties of this model. The first is data and information combined with context, by integrating multiple sources and augmenting them with what the department knows. The second and the third form a pair: the one-dimensional insight a system of record gives, bounded by its own data model, and the multidimensional insight the ontology gives across those systems. Fourth are the actions, inquiries, policies, typed outputs, and governance the model carries. The fifth is a dependence on a deep understanding of the department's processes and its people. This article takes the five in turn, beginning with the difference between one dimension and several.

A data lake and a data warehouse are where most departments already put their records. A lake stores data in whatever shape it arrived in, which is what makes it cheap to fill. A warehouse organizes data for reporting, so that the same question asked twice returns the same number. Both hold records, and neither holds the context that makes a record mean something to the person reading it.

The ontology holds that context in eight elements. It holds the entities the department works on, their attributes, the relationships among them across every system, and the actions permitted on them. It also holds the inquiries that can be put to them, the policies that govern the actions, the typed outputs those actions produce, and the governance around all of it. Those eight elements are what turn the records a lake and a warehouse hold into something a person, a business intelligence measure, or an agent can act on. A database returns raw data; the ontology returns the same data with the context that makes it insight. The lake and the warehouse keep their jobs, and the ontology sits beside them.

## A system of record holds one dimension, and the ontology holds the context

The systems a department already runs are where its records live, and each of them was designed around one dimension of the work. Matter management holds the matter and its status, contract lifecycle management the agreement and its stage, document management the file and its versions, and e-billing the invoice and its line items. Each is bounded by its own data model, and where a system has no field for a piece of information, that information does not exist for it.

Take a single matter and look at it through each system in turn. The matter system shows the budget and the status; the document system holds the drafts and the executed agreement; the e-billing platform holds the invoice lines; and the inbox holds the correspondence with the firm, including the exchange in which the scope was renegotiated in March. Every fact about the matter sits somewhere in that set. None of the four holds the relationships among the slices, so nobody can read from any one of them which invoice lines belong to the March scope change, or which email settled the question that a disputed invoice describes. The reader supplies those connections from memory, and the reader is the only place they exist.

Each of those systems returns raw data, because raw data is what its data model contains. The context described above, meaning the relationships across all four systems, the actions permitted, the policies that govern them, and the record of what was decided, has no home in any of them. The foundation of legal operations intelligence therefore has to be the layer that holds the context across the systems, and no single system of record holds it. That is a statement about data models rather than about products, and it applies to any system that owns one slice of the work, however well it owns it.

## The relationships are the asset

If no single system holds the connections, the first question for anyone building the model is which entities it has to connect and which connections are worth naming. The entities are the easier half of that question, and most departments could list theirs in an afternoon.

Three entities form the spine, because nearly everything else a department does connects to one of them: the matter, the project, and the invoice. Around the spine sit the operational entities:

- request
- document
- communication
- party
- engagement
- timekeeper
- obligation
- deadline
- clause
- budget
- policy

Two of those deserve a sentence each, because departments rarely model them as entities at all. Policy is one: outside counsel guidelines, triage rules, service level definitions, and delegation thresholds are the same kind of thing, in that each is written by the legal team, versioned, and evaluable the same way on every run. Obligation is the other: it is extracted from a document, linked to the parties and the dates that bind it, and it changes status over the life of the agreement. A document repository has no way to represent that change, because a document has no status of its own.

The value of the model sits in the relationships, and they are the half that no system holds today. A communication belongs to a thread, and the thread belongs to a matter, which is how a question asked in March can be found in September by someone reading the invoice that the answer produced. An invoice line belongs to a timekeeper and to an activity code, and through the matter it belongs to a budget. That chain makes a variance attributable to particular work instead of to an accounting period, and it lets a finance partner read the same number the legal team reads ([Breaking the Silo Between Legal, Finance, and the Business](/why-spaarke/breaking-the-silo) makes the case for those crossings).

Two more chains do the same work elsewhere in the department. A document contains clauses, a clause creates an obligation, the obligation binds a party, and it falls due on a date. A request becomes a matter, so that intake volume and matter volume are one measurement taken at two points. When each is counted in its own system, the department holds two figures that never agree.

The engagement relationship binds the department's matter to the firm's matter. Few departments hold it, and it decides whether the department can compare what it planned with what it was billed without a person performing the comparison by hand (Exhibit 1). Taken together, these relationships give the department a view that runs across its systems, and each of them answers a question that the department currently answers by hand or leaves unanswered.

## Actions belong on entities, and each carries a gate

Relationships make the model legible. They do not by themselves make it operational, because reading a model and acting through it are separate capabilities. A department that can see a problem without acting on it inside the same system is in the position the opening described.

An action in this model is bound to an entity. Sending a budget inquiry to a firm is an action on an engagement, approving a scope change is an action on a matter, and releasing a litigation hold is an action on a document set. A workflow engine binds the same action to a screen or to a process definition, and its record of the action then sits apart from the thing the action changed. Because the action is stored where the thing it acted on is stored, the record of it can be found later, and not in a workflow log that nobody opens.

Every action carries two pieces of information that a workflow tool usually leaves implicit. Authority says who may take it and under which delegation policy, so that the right to approve a write-off is a property of the model, and it does not depend on what the team remembers. Provenance says which rule produced the action, which version of the policy that rule belonged to, which evidence it read, and which person confirmed it. Provenance makes a decision defensible a year later, when the person who confirmed it has changed roles and the policy has been revised twice.

Actions are released through one of three gates, and the gate is a property of the action type rather than of the person in front of the screen. Some actions release automatically, because the rule that produces them is deterministic and the cost of an error is small, such as recalculating an accrual or updating a matter's status when the docket changes. A second class needs a single confirmation from a named person, which is the ordinary case for anything that leaves the department. Counsel review is the third, and it is reserved for acts that only an attorney may perform. The envelope widens as the department gains confidence in a rule, and it widens by changing the gate on an action type, which is a configuration change with an audit record. That envelope is what "AI-directed, human-controlled" means in structural terms: the direction comes from a rule or an agent proposing an action, and the control is the gate a person holds.

Where actions change the state of the model, inquiries read it. An inquiry is a question put to the model in the department's own terms, so a director asks which matters are running over budget without knowing which table holds the answer. Which obligations fall due this quarter, and which firm's invoices carry the most rejected lines, are inquiries of the same kind. The model answers from the entities and the relationships, which is why no single system's tables can answer the same questions. The second question needs obligations linked to documents, parties, and dates; the third needs invoice lines linked to firms and to the rules that rejected them.

Actions and inquiries produce typed outputs, meaning that each result has a defined shape that a person, a business intelligence measure, or an agent can read without interpreting it first. Four of those shapes carry most of the work. A fact is a value computed the same way on every run. An observation is what a policy makes of a fact. An outcome records what was decided and what followed from it. A report card aggregates outcomes over a firm, a practice area, or a period.

The rules and the release envelope taken together are the model's governance, and using that word for them is more than a naming convention, because the governance is enforced by the same model that answers the questions. A department that writes its outside counsel guidelines into a document and its delegation thresholds into a spreadsheet has two governance artifacts that no system reads. A department that writes them into the model has one that every action, every inquiry, and every agent reads before it acts.

## The unit of value is a governed decision

Entities, relationships, actions, inquiries, and governance are easier to judge in use than in definition. The sentence that follows is one a legal operations director hears in some form every quarter: this matter is running over budget, so ask outside counsel to investigate. Run that sentence twice, once against reporting alone and once against reporting with an entity and action model beneath it, and the difference shows up in what the department holds at the end of the year.

In the first run, business intelligence does its job. It reads the invoice lines and the budget, computes the variance, and shows on a report that the matter has passed the threshold the department set. Nothing in the reporting tool can act on that. Someone opens the report, switches to Outlook, and writes to the partner from memory of what the matter was supposed to cost. A reply arrives two days later. The director reads it, decides that the overage reflects a scope change the business had approved, and notes as much in a spreadsheet that four people know how to find. The variance was correct and the decision was sound, and next quarter the system knows no more than it knew the quarter before, because the only records it kept were the invoice lines that started the exchange.

In the second run, business intelligence does the same job, and the entity and action model sits underneath it. The variance is a fact on the matter, computed deterministically from the invoice lines and the budget and carrying its own provenance, so the number can be traced to the lines that produced it. A policy the department wrote evaluates that fact and produces an observation, linked to the matter, to the invoice lines that drove the variance, and to the engagement with the firm. The observation proposes an action, a budget inquiry to the firm, which goes out through its gate: in most departments a single confirmation from the legal operations director, and in a few, automatic release below a threshold the department set.

The firm replies, and the reply is classified back onto the inquiry thread, so the question and the answer are held together on the engagement. A typed outcome is then recorded against the matter, drawn from a defined set: a write-off, a revision to the accrual, an approved scope change, or no action. Nothing in that sequence writes to the e-billing system, which continues to own the invoices and to be the system that processes them.

The two runs differ little inside a single quarter, and they differ a great deal after four of them. Across 200 matters, the accumulated outcomes are the outside counsel report card: which firms produced variances, which variances were justified by approved scope changes, which ended in write-offs, and how long each firm took to answer. That evidence exists only because each intervention was captured as an entity at the time it happened, and it returns to business intelligence as new input, which is where the next quarter's questions start.

The comparison here is between reporting alone and reporting with entities and actions beneath it. A dashboard does its job when it shows that a matter is over budget, and a department without one is worse off than a department with one. What a dashboard leaves to the reader is the rest of the sequence: working out which decision is due, gathering the supporting information from the other systems, leaving the tool in order to act, and remembering to record what happened. An entity and action model attaches state, rules, and available actions to the entity itself. The decision that is required, the information needed to make it, and the means to act therefore arrive in one place, and the outcome is recorded against the same entity. That is what making information actionable means in this article.

[The New Mandate for Legal Operations](/why-spaarke/managing-legal-operations) describes a function now expected to act on what it sees, and whether it can act is settled by the design of the system, however diligent the director is. A warehouse records what happened to the business, an ontology also records what the organization decided to do about it and the context that connects them, and business intelligence reads both records.

## Business intelligence and the ontology read the same record

Business intelligence had a role in both runs of that example, and the role was the same in each. It is the deterministic dimension of the platform. It computes the variance the same way every time, and no model call produces that number, which matters because a figure a general counsel takes to the CFO cannot vary between two readings of the same data.

That constraint is easier to hold when the model separates three classes of statement. The fact and the observation are two of them, and the worked example used both. An inference is the third, and it is probabilistic, such as an obligation that a language model extracted from a contract or the classification of a firm's reply. Keeping the three apart allows a department to use probabilistic systems without letting probabilistic output into places that require the same answer twice, and [Legal AI Is Not Deterministic](/why-spaarke/probabilistic-vs-deterministic) sets out why that split matters.

The ontology gives business intelligence consistent entities to read across systems, so that a measure of spend by practice area does not depend on which of three systems supplied the matter list. It gives the department somewhere to act on what the measure shows, which is the gap the first run of the example exposed. It also returns the record of decisions and outcomes to business intelligence as new input, which is how the report card in the example comes to exist at all.

Microsoft describes the same division in its own documentation. Its Fabric IQ documentation, in the revision of July 9, 2026, describes three layers of business context: unified data, business intelligence, and operational intelligence. The same page says that "Ontologies can be generated directly from semantic models already in production, keeping business language consistent across experiences", and the Build announcement of June 2, 2026 by Arun Ulag says that ontologies "extend semantic models by adding operational context". Reading the ontology as the operational layer beside the reporting layer is our reading of those two statements, and not a claim the documentation makes, and the ontology item was still labeled a preview in the documentation of July 9, 2026. Jessica Talisman put the same distinction more briefly in Metadata Weekly on January 22, 2026, writing that "meaning isn't the same as measurement". A semantic layer built for business intelligence standardizes measurement, and an ontology standardizes meaning.

Reporting tools have been moving toward alerting and triggering of their own, so the two capabilities are converging from both ends. What a trigger fired from a dashboard still lacks is the entity it should attach its outcome to.

## AI has lowered the cost of the model and raised its value

Most of what this article describes was technically possible in 2015, at a cost that put it well beyond a legal department of 40 people. Two things have changed since. Building and maintaining the model has become cheaper, and having one has become worth more, because agents are now among the things reading it. Six mechanisms account for the change, and they run in both directions.

The first is grounding. An agent that reads governed entities and deterministic facts has less room to invent them, because a question about a matter's budget resolves to a fact with provenance, and the system returns that number instead of composing a plausible one. Gartner put a number on the effect in a press release of May 11, 2026. It predicted that organizations which prioritize semantics in AI-ready data will improve agentic AI accuracy by up to 80% and reduce costs by up to 60% by 2027. Those are predictions rather than measurements, and "up to" carries weight in both of them, so the figures are worth less than the mechanism behind them. Rita Sallam, speaking at the firm's Data and Analytics Summit in London, stated that mechanism directly: "Agentic AI outcomes depend on context including semantic representations of data."

A shared vocabulary across tools is the second mechanism. CLOC's 2026 State of the Industry report, released on March 2, 2026, covers 135 departments and draws on the 2025 Harbor Law Department Survey. It found that 85% of departments have dedicated AI oversight or resources and that 80% name technology strategy as a priority. Those figures measure attention rather than tool count, and survey evidence on how many AI tools a department runs is thin. The argument holds without a survey. A department running an assistant in Word, a review tool in the contract process, and an agent over its spend data needs all three to mean the same thing by "matter", "budget", and "obligation", and the only way they can is to read the same definitions. Microsoft claims the same property for its own layers, writing in the Fabric IQ documentation of July 9, 2026 that they "ensure that every agent starts with the same understanding of the business and can apply it correctly across workflows".

The third is the shape of the question a person can ask. A director who wants to know which matters ran over budget in the second quarter asks that, and the model turns the question into queries against the tables where the data sits. Microsoft's ontology documentation, in the revision of May 14, 2026, describes the same property: "This enables you to ask questions using business terms, instead of needing to know the details of how your data is stored in different systems." The business terms in question are the department's own, which is why the model has to be built from the department's processes rather than from the schemas underneath them.

Extraction is the fourth, and it is where the cost fell furthest. Pulling parties, clauses, dates, and obligations out of a contract used to require a person to abstract each document by hand, which is why most departments abstracted only the agreements above a value threshold. Language models do that work now. The harder half of the job is matching what was extracted to entities that already exist: the same counterparty under three spellings, and the same matter under the firm's number and the department's. Jason Cui and Jennifer Li of a16z, writing on March 10, 2026, named "canonical entities, identity resolution" as part of the context layer that data agents need. We treat identity resolution across systems as a requirement the model has to satisfy rather than as a solved problem, and we describe no shipped capability for it.

The fifth is that actions defined on entities give an agent something safer to call than the systems beneath. The agent receives the same defined actions a person has, with the same preconditions, permissions, and audit record, in place of open write access to a matter table. The Model Context Protocol specification of June 18, 2025 states the accompanying requirement as a protocol rule: "For trust & safety and security, there SHOULD always be a human in the loop with the ability to deny tool invocations." Microsoft's documentation describes an ontology exposed the same way, in a how-to page dated April 14, 2026 that carries a preview banner: "Ontology can function as an MCP server, exposing an API so that external AI systems can interact with it through the MCP protocol."

Human review closes the list, and it is the release envelope described earlier, applied to work an agent proposes rather than to work a rule proposes. An agent stages an action, and a person with the authority to release it does so. Latitude widens as the department watches a rule perform, and it widens by changing the gate on an action type while the actions the agent may attempt stay the same.

One test follows from the six that a department can put to any system it is considering. An answer should carry its own freshness and provenance, in the manner of "this obligation was inferred from a contract indexed four hours ago and confirmed by counsel on June 8". Every clause in a sentence of that shape names something a reader can check, and it uses the terms the department set. That is the property the six mechanisms have in common, and it is why the model underneath and the AI reading it accumulate value on different schedules.

## A legal ontology carries rules that generic models lack

That semantic work looks different in a legal department from the way it looks in a manufacturing business or a bank, and the differences are structural. Five of them change how the model has to be built, and a department evaluating a general-purpose ontology tool should test the tool against each one.

Privilege and work product travel with the entity, and not with the folder that happens to hold it. A generic enterprise model decides access from outside the model, through a permission on a folder, a role in an application, or a rule in a security product, and the record itself carries no notion of who may read it. Privilege attaches to the communication and to the advice inside it, and it survives every copy, export, and extract that touches the record. A model that stores the attribute on the entity can answer whether a document is privileged wherever that document has traveled, and a model that leaves the question to the container answers only for the container.

The document is frequently the record itself. In most enterprise models a row describes a thing that exists elsewhere: an order that was shipped, a customer who exists in the world, a payment that cleared a bank. The executed agreement is the obligation itself, and the court's order is the instruction and not a note about it. A legal model therefore treats the document as an entity with its own clauses, versions, dates, and obligations, and not as an attachment hanging off a matter row.

Counterparty paper arrives from an adversary, and the model has to record that fact about every value it takes from it. A limitation of liability clause extracted from a supplier's draft records what the supplier proposed, which is a different kind of statement from the same clause taken from the department's own template or from an executed agreement. Values that arrive from the other side carry a different trust class, and the model holds the provenance of each extracted value so that a later reader can tell a proposal from a term the department accepted. Commercial data models rarely need the distinction, because a vendor master record has no adversary.

The density of rules is the difference that shows up soonest in a build. Outside counsel guidelines, billing guidelines, retention schedules, ethical walls, and delegation thresholds all govern what may be done, by whom, and on which records, and each of them changes on its own schedule. Most of what a legal model holds is a rule about what may be done rather than a fact about what happened, which inverts the usual proportions of an enterprise data model. A model that cannot hold rules as content leaves them in documents and spreadsheets, where no action can read them and where compliance depends on the memory of whoever is on duty.

An ethical wall has to partition the graph as well as the rows. Row-level security keeps records away from a person who should not see them. A wall has to keep the relationships away as well, because three things are each a disclosure in their own right: that an engagement exists, who the party on the other side is, and that a particular timekeeper worked on the matter. In a model whose value sits in its relationships, the wall applies to the connections between entities, and the department has to be able to show afterwards that the wall held.

## The build starts from a decision

None of those five differences can be read off a schema. They are known by the people who run intake, manage matters, review invoices, and deal with firms, which settles where a build has to start. It starts from a decision the department has to make, where many data projects start from a source system that is available or from a dashboard request that somebody escalated.

Take a decision that recurs and that matters: whether to approve a scope change, which firm should receive the next matter of a given type, or whether an accrual is right. Work back from it to the entities the decision touches, beginning with the spine of matter, project, and invoice. Define each entity once, bind it to the system where its data lives, and add the relationships the decision depends on. Then add the policies that govern the decision and the gated actions that carry it out. Grow the model one use case at a time, and test it against the questions leaders put to their teams over and over. Those questions are the real specification, and most of them are already written down in a dozen email threads.

Each of those steps depends on what people in the department know. Somebody has to know where the information sits and in what shape, which of the three matter numbers in circulation the firm bills against, and what a general counsel or a finance partner needs in order to decide. The ontology has to be built with a deep understanding of the department's processes and its people, because a model built from the systems' tables alone reproduces the limits of those tables, including the fragmentation it was meant to resolve.

Observers of the general-purpose offerings make the same point about the effort involved. Barry Briggs is an analyst at Directions on Microsoft and was formerly the chief technology officer of Microsoft's own IT organization. He wrote on December 2, 2025 that "Creating an ontology, in Fabric or elsewhere, is a major effort and requires on-going maintenance, so there's no getting out of the hard work". Nikola Ilic, writing at Data Mozart on February 3, 2026, gave the governance version of the same warning: "If your organization struggles to agree on basic definitions now, an ontology won't magically create consensus". Both observations describe where the cost sits. The work is agreement about definitions, and the software arrives after that agreement has been reached.

Legal work already has standards that supply part of the vocabulary, and the model uses them. SALI's matter specification publishes over 18,000 tags, each with a unique identifier, and those tags supply values for matter attributes that would otherwise be free text. LEDES and UTBMS define the shape of spend facts, which is what makes an invoice line comparable across two firms at all. What the standards do not supply is an operational model. They say how to label a matter and how to format a bill, and the relationships, the actions, the policies, and the decision record are the department's to define. The five differences above are the legal expression of the knowledge that goes into defining them.

## The ontology sits with the systems already in place

Defining entities and relationships raises the question of where the data behind them lives, and for most departments the answer is that it lives where it lives today. The model binds each entity to the system that owns its data and references what it does not own. The document management system and the e-billing platform stay where they are. Those systems go on holding the documents and the invoices, enforcing their own permissions, and serving as the record for the things they record. The model holds the entities, the relationships among them, and the decisions taken about them.

Some entities have no owner today. In most departments no system holds the request that arrived as an email, the disposition that answered it, the obligation extracted from a contract, the policy that governed an approval, the inquiry sent to a firm, or the record of the action taken. Those are the entities that get reconstructed from memory whenever somebody asks. A department can hold those process entities in the platform that holds the model, in which case the model and the record live in one place, or it can bind them to whatever system it already uses for each of them. The architecture is the same in both cases, and so is the honest point about it. Wherever the data belongs to another system, the model binds to that system and does not build a second record of truth beside it, and binding carries a refresh policy that a buyer should ask about before signing anything. Identity is the precondition for all of it, since the same matter carries one number in the matter system, another on the firm's bill, and a third in a folder name.

The same question of reach applies to the word the field converged on this year. In May 2026 two document management providers announced context layers within weeks of each other, on the 14th and the 22nd. The first described a layer over documents organized by matter, together with communications, people, activity, and permissions including ethical walls, and its announcement made no mention of spend, e-billing, outside counsel management, or matter management. The second was reported with a similar scope. Context drawn from documents is a real layer of the model and a useful one, and it is one layer. A department's operations reach into intake, spend, obligations, and the decisions taken about all three, so a context layer that stops at the document boundary leaves undefined exactly the relationships that cross it.

Neil Cameron, writing in Legal IT Insider on May 14, 2026, noted the convergence on "context" and left an open question beside it, which is how a department audits which context fed a given AI output. The term has a traceable origin: Jaya Gupta and Ashu Garg of Foundation Capital, writing on December 22, 2025, described a context graph as "a living record of decision traces stitched across entities and time so precedent becomes searchable". That definition is close to what this article has described, and it is broader than a layer over a document repository. The practical version of Cameron's question for a legal department is which systems the layer reaches, because the answer decides how much of the department's work the model can govern. It is also the point at which the ontology becomes the way the information and the context held in the systems already in place enter the platform that [How to Build the Legal Operations Intelligence Platform](/why-spaarke/building-the-legal-operations-intelligence-platform) describes.

## Open interfaces make the model practical

Binding to systems the department does not own works only if those systems allow it. The model has to read from the systems of record, and, where an action completes in one of them, to write back through an interface that system governs. A department can succeed with an entity and action model only if the systems it depends on expose documented, permission-aware interfaces for reading and for writing. That makes the interface question one for the procurement process to settle.

Between May and early July 2026 several legal technology providers and one frontier model provider moved in that direction. Anthropic's Claude for Legal announcement of May 12, 2026 described more than 20 new MCP connectors and 12 plugins. Its descriptions of those connectors include administrative and workflow actions: Box to "create or update content", Datasite to set up folder structures and invite users, Relativity to "stand up matters, shape workspace schema, govern access", and Docusign to "orchestrate agreement workflows across the contract lifecycle". Those are the model provider's words about the connectors rather than each connector vendor's own statement.

iManage released an MCP server on May 14, 2026, naming Harvey, Legora, ChatGPT, Claude, Microsoft Copilot, and a firm's own agents as clients. The release states that "All AI access to iManage content via MCP is authenticated, permission-bound, and fully logged, respecting existing ethical walls and access controls." It describes governed access to content and states no write action, so what it supports is what it names. Its commercial argument fits in one sentence: "A single MCP connection replaces a growing list of custom API integrations." Neil Cameron read the release the same way in Legal IT Insider that day, writing that "The product's value proposition is that one MCP connection replaces a proliferating list of custom API integrations, and that new AI systems can be added or swapped without an IT project each time."

Two other announcements from the same period belong beside those. Thomson Reuters and Anthropic said on May 12, 2026 that CoCounsel Legal would be reachable from Claude through the protocol, and Joel Hron described the point of it in terms of where the work happens: "Wherever lawyers are working, the full power of CoCounsel Legal is available to them." LawVu announced an MCP server on June 2, 2026. Its launch article, written by Sam Kidd and updated on June 8, states the write actions in the future tense for the initial release: "Create matters or trigger contract workflows directly from an AI tool", and "Update matter status, create tasks, and set deadlines from connected AI workflows".

Three limits belong beside those announcements. First, the research behind this article could not confirm whether several of the connectors read only or also write, so no label is applied here beyond what each cited source states. Second, it found no confirmed example of an agent writing spend data into an e-billing or matter management system of record, which is consistent with the worked example above, where nothing writes to the e-billing platform. Third, the case for open environments is made mainly by the vendors that ship connectors, by the trade press, and by individual practitioners. Michael Dineen of Brightflag advised buyers on May 26, 2026 to ask an enterprise legal management vendor whether it has an MCP server, and the research found no formal statement on the subject from CLOC, ACC, or ILTA. Most of these announcements were also addressed to law firms and not to corporate legal departments, which makes them evidence of a direction and not of availability inside a department.

Openness carries costs of its own. LexisNexis has been selective about which MCP-based workflows it supports, on grounds of authority, attribution, and the consistency of answers, as Greg Dickason set out on June 10, 2026, and a provider whose product is authority has more to lose from an answer it cannot stand behind. The protocol itself leaves a great deal to implementers and operators. Its specification of June 18, 2025 states that "Authorization is OPTIONAL for MCP implementations." The maintainers wrote on March 16, 2026 that "Every property is a hint", that a tool carrying no annotations "is assumed to be non-read-only, potentially destructive, non-idempotent, and open-world", and that "Many servers ship without them, and clients vary in how strictly they honor the pessimistic defaults." A release candidate published on May 21, 2026 hardened the authorization specification with six proposals that align it more closely with how OAuth 2.0 and OpenID Connect are deployed in practice, and that revision tells a buyer how young this plumbing is. A second dependence forms one layer up, because ComplexDiscovery observed on May 13, 2026 that a team which teaches a plugin its house style and thresholds "is building institutional knowledge inside the model", which "deepens the lock-in to Claude". A department that encodes its playbooks inside one provider's plugin format has moved its dependence and not removed it, and the model it owns keeps that dependence reversible. The subject is larger than one section of an article about ontologies, and an expanded treatment will follow.

## An owned model makes building safe

The same interfaces that let a model read a department's systems let the department build things of its own, and departments are moving in that direction for reasons that have nothing to do with ontologies. In the survey that Everlaw and the Association of Corporate Counsel released on October 14, 2025, which reached 657 in-house professionals in 30 countries, 64% of respondents said they expect to rely less on outside counsel. The figure measures expectation rather than behavior, and the survey asked nothing about open platforms or about building software. What it records is a direction of travel, which is more work and more control held inside the department.

Commentary has run ahead of documented practice. Patricija Corey, Legal Operations Manager at HUMAN, told Bloomberg Law on January 5, 2026 that "The teams that stand out will use AI, vibe coding, and light tech skills to build and iterate on real solutions quickly, without waiting on outside help." That is a forecast, and it describes no build. The clearest published example comes from a company in the AI industry. Anthropic's legal team wrote on December 8, 2025 that it had built four internal tools and had cut marketing review turnaround from two or three days to 24 hours. A legal department inside an AI company is an unusual case, and the account does not say that no engineers were involved.

The evidence thins quickly after that. Mark Pike, a member of that same legal team, told Artificial Lawyer on May 12, 2026 that in-house teams are "open-sourcing outside counsel management tools on GitHub so peers can fork them", naming no team, which leaves the report as hearsay. The research found no corporate legal survey that reports a build-versus-buy percentage, no survey that ranks openness or API access as a selection criterion, and no verified named build by a corporate legal department outside the AI industry. Building is an emerging practice rather than an established one, and the people closest to it say the same. The founders of Vibecode-Law told Artificial Lawyer on January 26, 2026 that such projects "aren't production-ready out of the box", and, asked whether building supplants platforms, answered "No, we don't think so." Someone also has to maintain what gets built, and that cost arrives in the second year.

What the model changes is the floor a built tool starts from. A tool built on a governed entity and action model inherits the definitions, the permissions, and the audit record the model already enforces, so the person building it spends their time on the question the tool exists to answer. A tool built directly against each system's tables establishes all three again, in its own code, for every system it touches, and the next tool starts over. Across five or six small tools that difference is the difference between one governed surface and six ungoverned ones, and it decides whether a department can let a domain expert build something without acquiring a new risk each time. Building inside a legal department deserves an article of its own, and one will follow. Spaarke provides a Legal Operations Intelligence ontology solution for corporate legal departments.

## An AI model can be replaced through configuration, and the ontology belongs to the department

Once a department builds on its own model, it holds two assets with very different lifespans. It can change the AI model it runs through configuration, adding a second provider for a particular task or moving to the next frontier model when it arrives, and at least one vendor is building for that expectation. Cameron's sentence about the iManage release states it plainly, that new AI systems can be added or swapped without an IT project each time. What cannot be swapped on that schedule is the agreement about what a matter is, the relationships that carry the questions the department asks, the outside counsel guidelines written as rules an action can read, and the record of what was decided under them. That work takes years, no vendor performs it on the department's behalf, and its product belongs to the department whichever tools read it.

[The Legal IQ Stack: Data, Memory, Inference](/why-spaarke/the-iq-stack) gives the same division its structure. The ontology is what the Data and Memory layers are made of: the entities and relationships bound to the systems where the data lives, and the operational memory of decisions, provenance, and outcomes that accumulates on those entities as the department works. Inference reads over both, and it is the layer that changes most often and costs least to change. Where a department invests in the top layer alone, it rents its intelligence from whichever model it happens to be running this year.

Expectations are already ahead of what buyers see delivered. In the Thomson Reuters summary of its 2026 Future of Professionals Report, published on July 2, 2026, 78% of corporate clients said that AI-enabled quality improvements are very important or essential to them, while 6% said that most of their providers deliver them. Those figures describe what corporate clients want from their outside providers, and the report measures nothing inside a department. Our reading is that the same distance runs there, between a department that has licensed assistants and what it can point to as changed in how it works.

The decision that lasts, for a legal operations director, is who defines "matter", "budget", and "obligation" for every tool the department runs, and where the record of the department's own decisions is kept. A department that leaves those definitions to each tool establishes definitions, permissions, and an audit record once per tool, and establishes them again with the next one, at the pace new tools arrive. It also finishes each year without the asset the second run of the example produced: the record, across 200 matters and four quarters, of what was decided, by whom, under which rule, and what followed. That record cannot be reconstructed later from invoices and inboxes, and every quarter a department goes without keeping it is a quarter it will not get back.
