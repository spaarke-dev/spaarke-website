## Spine

Systems of record give legal operations data, and a legal operations intelligence platform gives it intelligence: the context, the policy and authority, and the governed action that turn a record into a decision. A system of record is a database with a defined scope and structure, and a department that has data without intelligence stays data-rich and insight-poor, and its AI gives fragmented answers faster. The platform defines the entire operation and the business around it, across every dimension and from every source, so it has to be built from the knowledge of experienced practitioners and the organization's own end users, who take part from the first day and in every phase, with forward-deployed legal engineers working alongside them. Spaarke builds that platform in five phases that form a loop, one decision at a time, with technology, consulting, and managed services joined through one ontology that the organization owns. The same phases build the organization's internal AI capability, because context, definitions, permissions, an evaluation set, and widening autonomy each come out of one phase.

### Section 1: Systems of record give legal operations data. A legal operations intelligence platform gives it intelligence
- **Why the distinction is critical (writer's review, 2026-10-05: "SORs give data, LOI gives intelligence", and the draft must drive it home):** data records what happened and intelligence turns a record into a decision, by bringing it with its context to the person with authority, acting through a governed process, and recording the outcome. A department without it stays data-rich and insight-poor, and AI added to its data gives fragmented answers faster, because AI can read what was recorded and cannot read how the department decides. No single system of record can supply intelligence, because the limitation is inherent in the architecture of legal systems. The section builds this in order: systems of record give data; the decision (counsel selection) needs more than data; intelligence defined; the cost of lacking it and what AI does to the cost; how it has to be built.
- **Claim:** A system of record is a database with a specifically defined scope and structure: a matter table, an invoice feed, a document repository, each fixed in advance for one function and holding one dimension of the work. A legal operations intelligence platform defines the entire operation and the business around it, multi-dimensional and from every source, including the systems of record, the email and documents no system structures, and the knowledge held by the people who do the work. That is a different kind of solution and it is built differently, from knowledge about how people decide rather than from requirements about what people record, so experienced practitioners and end users take part from the beginning and in every phase.
- **Opening of the section (the concession, as a direct predicate):** "A legal operations function can buy a platform, engage advisers, and start an AI pilot without deciding which decisions any of them is meant to improve." Then the mechanism: those that buy first and define later pay for the definitions anyway, in configuration work and change requests, and a build that starts before an owner is named stalls at the first action that needs a named approver. The function's own acknowledgment is the preferred voice ("legal operations teams increasingly acknowledge that...").
- **Evidence:** The definition and the mechanism, stated from practice (brief, "What we assert from practice"). Article 3 already carries "each system of record holds one dimension of the work", so the section references it in the citing first person. The section says what a system of record does well in one clause, and says that Spaarke's own system of record is one source among the platform's many. A team that builds from requirements and schemas produces a reporting layer, because the decision logic never reached the specification (two sentences at most). ### Section 2: The Spaarke Method builds that intelligence one decision at a time, through five phases that form a loop
- **Claim:** The five phases are envision, design, build, deploy, and manage, and Manage feeds the next Envision. The phases overlap in practice. Each ends with something the organization can inspect, each asks specific things of its practitioners and end users, and each contributes one part of the organization's AI capability.
- **Shape:** One lead-in paragraph (about 80 words) stating the structure and introducing the exhibit, then five labelled paragraphs, one per phase, with bare-noun bold labels in the same grammar (**Envision.**, **Design.**, **Build.**, **Deploy.**, **Manage.**). This is the one bold lead-in list of the piece that carries the counted framework. Each paragraph runs about 170 to 190 words and says, in order: what happens, who takes part (the decision owners, the practitioners, the end users, and the legal engineers), what exists at the end, and in a clause what it means in a law firm where that differs. A scene opens or closes each paragraph.
- **Per-phase content, all from the brief:**
  - **Envision.** Start from the outcomes the general counsel, the CFO, and business unit leaders need, or for a firm, the managing partner, the practice leaders, and the client's expectations. Write each decision as one sentence. Find the decisions with no owner or three. Choose the first decision, name the platform's owner, and set a baseline (how long it takes, how often it is revisited, what it risks or delays). Decision owners name and test the decisions. *Ends with:* a ranked list of decisions, one chosen first use case with a named owner and a baseline.
  - **Design.** Build the model backward from the decision. Agree the definitions with the people who own them. Record each entity's source of truth and the limits that follow. Define permissions, ethical walls, and what an agent may do; for a firm, design includes the engagement it shares with each client. *Ends with:* a written model of one decision's entities, relationships, policies, and actions, with a source map and signed definitions.
  - **Build.** Work on real data from the first days, with the decision owners and the end users. Connect sources, create the entities the organization lacks a system for, define the governed actions, build the surface. Test with the questions the general counsel or the managing partner asks repeatedly, put to the surface, to a new joiner, and to the AI, and timed. *Ends with:* a working model and surface on real data, tested by the people who will use it.
  - **Deploy.** Put the platform where people already work, in the organization's tenant under its own identity and governance. Start AI in proposing mode with a person confirming. Agree who is accountable when the platform and a person disagree. Measure against the baseline. End users work in the platform and confirm or correct what it proposes. *Ends with:* the decision made through the platform in live work, with a record of each decision.
  - **Manage.** Ongoing execution and continuous improvement, and the phase where Spaarke's managed services, technical and legal, run alongside the organization. Keep definitions and playbooks current, operate the platform and its connections, review the decision record, widen or narrow AI latitude on that evidence, and choose the next decision. *Ends each cycle with:* a measured result against the baseline, an updated model, and the next decision.
- **Scenes (one per phase, told with "Consider" in the present tense, no invented figures, each closing on which part of the work was hard; the writer confirmed these on 2026-10-05):**
  - Envision, corporate department: a business unit asks for a standard agreement, and nobody can say who decides whether it needs a lawyer. Value point: speed to the business and the guardrails that make it safe. The hard part is that the decision has three owners.
  - Design, law firm: a firm decides whether to accept new work after a conflicts check, a capacity check, and a view of the client. Value point: growth and risk together. The hard part is agreeing what counts as capacity.
  - Build, corporate department: a regulatory inquiry arrives, and the department needs to know who must be told, what must be preserved, and who decides. Value point: readiness. The hard part is the thresholds nobody wrote down.
  - Deploy, law firm: what a client's general counsel needs to see this week about an engagement. Value point: the relationship. The hard part is agreeing that the client and the firm read the same record.
  - Manage, corporate department: early legal involvement, and which transactions trigger a review before terms are agreed. Value point: risk caught earlier, with the agent's latitude widened for one kind of review and narrowed for another. The hard part is reading the decision record honestly.
  - The matter budget decision from article 2 appears at most once, in a passing reference, and carries no phase.
- **Evidence:** The method itself (stated as Spaarke's practice), the scenes, and the earlier articles for the decision sentence, the baseline, and the few-day working session ("a working session of a few days, on real data, with the people who own the decision", article 2).
- **Exhibit:** Exhibit 1. Title: "Each phase ends with something the organization can inspect, and the last phase begins the next cycle." A loop of the five phases with the thing each ends with. Cited in parentheses at the end of the lead-in sentence that states the structure. Caption, alt text, and the five bold labels in the body use the same words for the same parts. The five labels in the exhibit are the five bare nouns.
- **Cross-link:** In our earlier article, [How to Build the Legal Operations Intelligence Platform](/why-spaarke/building-the-legal-operations-intelligence-platform), we described the decision sentence and the order of work (the lead-in carries this as the citing "we").
- **Voice notes:** Bare-noun labels, one grammar, each followed by complete sentences and no fragments. No ordinal handles ("the third phase"); phases are referred to by name. The firm clause in each paragraph is part of the sentence it belongs to. Value points spread across the scenes: speed to the business, growth and risk, readiness, the relationship, risk caught earlier.
- **Word budget:** 1,000.

### Section 4: One ontology joins technology, consulting, and managed services
- **Claim:** In Spaarke's method the three share one ontology and the same people carry the work across the phases, so what the consulting work learns about how an organization decides is held in the model the platform runs on, what the platform shows is tested against the organization's real questions, and what the managed service learns from running it changes the next design. The ontology is supported by data and information from systems of record, and the source of that data shapes the work.
- **Evidence:** The integration, stated as Spaarke's account of its own method. One passage that acknowledges how the three have usually been bought ("often separately, sometimes in combination of two") without naming any provider or making any claim about a provider's method, and states that Spaarke's structure fits neither description, as a bundle or as a sequence that leads from one to another. The systems-of-record passage: Spaarke can provide the system of record, and an organization can connect third-party systems of record. Working from the Spaarke system of record has advantages (entities, identifiers, permissions, ethical walls, and the decision record are native to the platform, so there is no identity resolution across systems to build, no refresh cycle to schedule, and no write-back limit to design around). Where the organization needs data from systems it keeps, those processes and their limits are addressed in design: the source of truth for each attribute, how identifiers are matched, how often data refreshes, and what the third party's interface permits for reading and for writing. The engagement record a firm shares with each client is named here once as an example of data that crosses an organizational boundary.
