## The one-sentence topic

Legal departments have practised knowledge management for a long
time, and AI now raises its importance and adds new dimensions to it,
because the department's own knowledge is the context an AI system
needs in order to be accurate and useful; that changes what counts as
knowledge, what knowledge is for, and how legal operations curates it
within the legal operations intelligence platform.

terms. Knowledge management supplies the source material, which is the
context that AI needs. The ontology gives that material structure and
makes it actionable. Business intelligence is the deterministic
dimension. The platform is the set of department capabilities
(process, people, technology, with technology as the tangible

knowledge management as a replacement for any of the other three.

## Why this matters, why now

- Law departments and legal operations have practised knowledge
  management for a long time, and the piece says so at the start.
  In the CLOC 2025 State of the Industry Report (186 departments;
  data from the 2024 Harbor Law Department Survey in collaboration
  with CLOC; released 2025-02-12), 79% of legal operations teams
  count knowledge management among their responsibilities, the sixth
  most common of fifteen services. Both the CLOC Core 12 and the ACC
  Legal Operations Maturity Model 2.0 name a knowledge management
  function, and neither text is recent (the revision dates, which are
  from a gap track, are in group D). What has changed is the reader
  of the department's knowledge. AI raises the importance of the
  function and adds new dimensions to it and to its role in the legal
  operations platform, and the piece is about those dimensions.
- The industry's account of the problem has changed within about a
  year. Knowledge management conferences, named knowledge leaders, the
  trade press, the legal publishers, and the consultancies now
  describe the organization's knowledge as the constraint on legal
  AI; a year earlier the debate was about the models. Inside Practice
  built its April 2026 New York program on the line "Artificial
  intelligence is no longer the bottleneck for law firms. Knowledge
  is." Harbor's brief on ILTACON 2026 (2026-09-11) reports that
  "clean, connected data is the precondition for AI producing anything
  useful," and CCBJ's editor wrote from the same conference that the
  bottleneck "may not always be model quality; it may be record
  quality."
- The organising concept is context. The AI field's own vocabulary
  has moved from prompt engineering to context engineering, which
  Anthropic defines as "the set of strategies for curating and
  maintaining the optimal set of tokens (information) during LLM
  inference" (2025-09-29), and two legal AI executives said the same
  thing for legal in January 2026 (Ed Walters of Clio: "Out: Prompt
  engineering. In: Context engineering"; Winston Weinberg of Harvey:
  "In 2026, context wins"). Every kind of knowledge management content
  a department maintains maps to an AI mechanism that consumes it, and
  the piece shows the mapping.
- In-house adoption of generative AI has passed law firm adoption (47%
  of corporate legal departments against 41% of law firms; Thomson
  Reuters 2026 AI in Professional Services Report, as summarized by
  Thomson Reuters on 2026-07-02). The article's own inference, which
  the source does not state, is that in-house departments reach the
  knowledge problem at least as early as firms do. The further point
  that they reach it with less knowledge management capacity is
  discussion supported by two confirmed facts: the median legal
  operations function is 5 FTEs (CLOC 2025 report, page 11), and the
  ACC model lists one full-time knowledge management professional as
  an advanced-stage marker. No in-house knowledge management headcount
  benchmark exists in the sources reached (see group G), so the piece
  presents the capacity comparison as discussion rather than as a
  measured fact.
- Professionals state the requirement themselves. In the Thomson
  Reuters 2026 Future of Professionals Report, 94% demand AI outputs
  grounded in authoritative content and 96% demand safeguards for
  confidential data (same 2026-07-02 summary; sample size
  **TBD — confirm**).
- The frameworks that define the legal operations function still
  describe knowledge management as people reusing work product. The
  knowledge management text in the CLOC Core 12 and in the ACC Legal
  Operations Maturity Model 2.0 does not mention AI. The research
  found no framework or association text that describes what the
  function becomes when the first reader of the department's
  knowledge is an AI system, and this piece supplies that description.
- The accuracy evidence gives the argument weight. Legal research
  tools built on retrieval over curated content still hallucinated
  between 17% and 33% of the time in the Stanford RegLab study, now
  citable in its peer-reviewed form (Journal of Empirical Legal
  Studies, 2025), so the quality of the corpus and the ability to test
  outputs have become management responsibilities.
- The risks became concrete in 2026. A federal court held in February
  that exchanges with a consumer AI tool were not privileged (United
  States v. Heppner, S.D.N.Y.), and a June client alert from Mayer
  Brown describes how AI notetaker transcripts widen retention and
  discovery exposure. Opening a department's knowledge to AI is now a
  governance decision as well as a productivity decision.
- The writer's direction for article 1 says that legal operations is
  taking on the curation of the department's knowledge. The CLOC 2025
  figure shows that the responsibility already sits with legal
  operations in four departments out of five; no industry body has
  yet described what the responsibility now requires, which leaves
  the position open for this piece to argue.

## The argument (what the reader should walk away believing)

1. **The constraint has moved from the model to the knowledge, and
   the knowledge is something departments already manage.** Open with
   the established practice, so the reader knows the piece is about
   what has changed rather than about the discipline itself: four in
   five legal operations teams already count knowledge management
   among their responsibilities (CLOC 2025 State of the Industry
   Report, 186 departments), and both in-house frameworks name the
   function (argument 4 quotes both texts). Then give the change in
   the industry's own account. The earlier debate concerned whether the models were
   good enough. By 2026 the knowledge management community, the trade
   press, the publishers, and the consultancies describe an
   organization's own knowledge as the limiting factor. Inside
   Practice framed its April 2026 program on that claim. Oz Benamram
   wrote in January 2026 that clean, structured, context-rich
   knowledge management becomes decisive, and his reading of the 2026
   SKILLS survey of 130 of the world's largest law firms is that
   firms have identified the knowledge management layer as the next
   priority. The ILTACON 2025 roundtable described knowledge
   professionals shifting from maintaining knowledge bases to
   orchestrating AI workflows and ensuring data quality, and Harbor's
   brief on ILTACON 2026 reports that clean, connected data was the
   recurring theme of the knowledge management sessions. The ILTA
   2026 Technology Survey, whose 508 respondents are law firms, gives
   the same account in survey form: 94% of responding firms are
   engaged with generative AI, and the survey's authors write that
   knowledge infrastructure "remains dramatically underdeveloped." The
   piece reports this consensus with its limits stated. Most of the voices
   are law firm knowledge leaders and vendors with an interest in the
   conclusion, the hard numbers are thin, and a skeptical view
   (Stephen Embry, Above the Law, 2026-08-25) holds that buyers
   cannot yet measure the impact of AI at all; Harbor's own report
   says that none of its participating firms and in-house departments
   has a mature framework for measuring it. A further view, associated
   with Winston Weinberg of Harvey and Ed Walters of Clio, holds that
   situational context matters more than the size of the library.
   That view supports the argument, because context is also something
   the organization has to supply, and it introduces the concept that
   organizes the rest of the piece.

2. **Context is what a department supplies, and the reasons are
   technical.** A general-purpose model knows nothing about a
   department's positions, history, or standards. Everything it can
   see when it answers is its context, which Anthropic defines as
   "the set of tokens included when sampling from a large-language
   model" and which sits in a window of fixed size whose recall
   degrades as it fills (2025-09-29). Context engineering is the
   discipline of deciding what information, instructions, examples,
   and tools the model is given for a task, and it is the discipline
   in which a department's knowledge management output is consumed.
   The model becomes specific to a department through four mechanisms,
   and each one consumes a knowledge asset that someone has to
   maintain.
   - Grounding and retrieval. The system searches the department's
     own material at the time of the question and hands the best
     matches to the model before it answers (retrieval-augmented
     generation, Lewis et al., 2020). The asset is a curated corpus
     that is current, authoritative, permissioned, and tagged well
     enough to be found. Microsoft describes its semantic index as
     providing "the grounding data for knowledge retrieval" (Microsoft
     Learn, 2026-04-23), and 94% of professionals say they demand
     outputs grounded in authoritative content.
   - Instruction. System prompts, playbooks, clause libraries with
     fallback positions, and agent skills tell the system what the
     department's position is and when to escalate. Anthropic's
     Agent Skills are "folders that include instructions, scripts,
     and resources that Claude can load when needed" (2025-10-16),
     and the company's legal plugin (released 2026-01-30) is
     configured with the organization's playbook, which defines
     standard positions, acceptable ranges, and escalation triggers.
     Both are neutral examples under the naming rule.
   - Example. Few-shot examples (Brown et al., 2020) and gold-standard
     answers show the system what good work looks like in this
     department.
   - Evaluation. Evaluation sets, built from real questions with
     approved answers and correctness criteria, tell the department
     whether the system is right. The Vals Legal AI Report
     (2025-02-27) shows the pattern in practice: law firms supplied
     the questions, reference documents, ideal responses, and
     correctness criteria, so finished work product became the
     benchmark.

   Retrieval alone does not make AI accurate, which is why the last
   two mechanisms matter as much as the first. The Stanford RegLab
   study found that leading legal research tools built on retrieval
   over curated content still hallucinated between 17% and 33% of the
   time (Journal of Empirical Legal Studies, 2025; arXiv 2024-05-30).
   Rebecca Fordon's review of the literature (AI Law Librarians,
   2026-02-19) attributes the better performance of legal platforms
   to curated, verified databases, and still concludes that roughly
   one in five responses contains errors. The first consequence is
   that the quality of the retrieved corpus is the variable a
   department controls. The second is that a department needs a way
   to test accuracy on its own work, which is the purpose of an
   evaluation set.

   The research found no evidence that departments or firms fine-tune
   models on their own knowledge as a mainstream practice. The legal
   research products describe themselves as retrieval-based
   (LexisNexis, 2024-05-03), and the one vendor case study of a
   custom-trained legal model concerns public case law rather than a
   customer's knowledge (OpenAI on Harvey, 2024-04-02). A department's
   influence over the quality of its AI therefore runs through what it
   supplies at answer time and how it tests the result. The section
   carries the context map below, which pairs each AI term with the
   knowledge management content that feeds it.

3. **What knowledge is, and what it is for, have both changed.**
   Traditional knowledge management collected finished work product:
   memos, templates, precedents, and know-how notes. AI systems can
   draw on a much wider set of material, most of which the department
   already produces and none of which was written to be knowledge:
   - email and chat;
   - meeting transcripts;
   - matter metadata;
   - negotiation and redline history;
   - decision logs and approvals;
   - outside counsel work product;
   - billing narratives;
   - intake requests and the answers given to them;
   - expert corrections of AI output.

   For each source, the piece says what it teaches an AI system that
   a finished document does not. Negotiation history shows which
   positions the department conceded and under what conditions.
   Billing narratives describe the work a matter type actually
   required. Intake requests and answers show what the business asks
   and what legal has already said. Expert corrections may be the most
   valuable source and the least collected, because each one records a
   gap between what the system produced and what the department's
   standard requires, and each one becomes the next gold-standard
   example or evaluation case. The evidence for this widening is
   uneven, and the piece says so. Practitioners confirm it: Tom
   Humberstone of Womble Bond Dickinson says repositories now reach
   beyond curated know-how to transactional and third-party content,
   Benamram lists precedents, outcomes, and expertise signals, and the
   ILTACON 2025 roundtable described knowledge scattered across
   OneDrive, SharePoint, Teams, and personal folders. The research
   found no neutral source that describes billing narratives, decision
   logs and approvals, outside counsel work product, intake answers,
   or expert corrections being managed as knowledge assets. Those
   items are the article's own argument and are presented that way.

   The purpose of knowledge has changed with its sources. Finished
   work product used to be the end product of knowledge management: a
   lawyer found the memo and reused it. That use continues. The larger
   use now is as material that trains AI systems, which then help
   produce the work product. The piece uses "training" in the sense
   legal AI systems use it, which is grounding, instruction, example,
   and evaluation, and it says so in a sentence when the word first
   appears. The CLOC Global Institute 2026 case study from Uber's
   legal operations team describes "training the model on our curated
   legal knowledge base" in exactly that sense (**TBD — confirm**;
   session description). The piece avoids any suggestion that
   departments train models in the narrow technical sense of
   fine-tuning (see argument 2). The shift changes the criteria for
   curation. A document kept for human reuse needed to be findable
   and roughly right, because the lawyer who reused it applied
   judgment. A document that grounds an AI answer needs a known author
   and date, a known status (current, superseded, or withdrawn), known
   permissions, and where possible a known outcome, because the system
   applies it without that judgment. The technical root of the new
   criteria is chunking: retrieval pipelines cut documents into
   passages small enough to embed and search (Microsoft Learn,
   2026-06-08), and a passage loses its document-level wrapper of
   author, date, status, and permissions unless the pipeline carries
   that metadata with every chunk. Josh Baxter of NetDocuments
   described the result of uncontrolled copying to CCBJ as "micro and
   incomplete DMSs" (2026-08-31), which is the failure mode a
   curation standard exists to prevent.

4. **The frameworks that define the function have not caught up.** The
   CLOC Core 12 defines the knowledge management function as
   implementing "processes and standards to collect, structure, and
   organize knowledge to save time and improve outcomes." The ACC
   Legal Operations Maturity Model 2.0 gives this definition: "The
   process of capturing, distributing, and effectively using both
   structured and tacit knowledge assets, from work products (such as
   legal memos) to understanding of an issue due to prior experience."
   Neither knowledge management text mentions AI. The ACC model does
   mention AI in other functions (eDiscovery, intellectual property,
   metrics and analytics, technology), which makes the silence in the
   knowledge management function more noticeable. Both definitions
   describe people reusing work product. The article fills that gap,
   and the piece states it as an observation about the published
   text, with respect for both bodies. It closes the section by noting
   that AI is entering CLOC's program through training: CLOC announced
   a practitioner-led AI Intensive on 2026-08-19, with a first workshop
   in October 2026 covering prompting, workflow and agent design, and
   implementation planning, and the release does not mention knowledge
   management. The Core 12 text is unchanged.

5. **Legal operations already holds the responsibility, and it is the
   natural curator of the department's knowledge for AI.** The
   responsibility is not new: 79% of legal operations teams count
   knowledge management among their services (CLOC 2025 report), and
   the piece opens the section with that fact. What is new is what the
   responsibility requires, and the capacity available for it. Law
   firms meet the knowledge problem with knowledge departments,
   professional support lawyers, and chief knowledge officers, and the
   ILTA 2026 Technology Survey reports that many responding firms
   anticipate additional headcount in knowledge management and data
   science, among other functions, without giving a percentage. The
   median in-house legal operations function is 5 FTEs (CLOC 2025
   report, page 11) and carries knowledge management alongside eleven
   other services, and the ACC model lists "At least one full-time
   dedicated KM professional" as one advanced-stage marker among
   thirteen. The research found no in-house knowledge management
   staffing benchmark, so the piece presents the capacity point as
   discussion built on those two facts. The research also found that
   no industry body yet describes legal operations as the curator of
   the department's knowledge for AI; CLOC and Harbor frame the legal
   operations role in AI as governance and operating model, and 85% of
   departments now have dedicated AI oversight or resources (CLOC 2026
   State of the Industry Report, 135 departments, 2026-03-02). The
   article therefore argues the case as its own position. It rests on
   three observations. Legal operations already runs the systems in
   which the new sources are created: intake, matter management,
   e-billing, outside counsel management, and the technology stack.
   Legal operations is also the function that CLOC and Harbor already
   associate with AI governance, and ownership elsewhere is
   fragmented: Harbor's ILTACON 2026 brief reports "a recurring
   frustration raised in roundtable discussions" that ownership of AI
   security and governance decisions is split across IT, Risk, the
   general counsel's office, and knowledge management, and concludes
   that "Governance needs a clear owner." The piece uses that
   observation as an analogy, says that it describes law firm
   roundtables, and notes that no equivalent in-house data was found.
   Finally, curation is operational work (ownership, standards, review
   cycles, retirement, and measurement) of the kind the function
   already performs for spend and vendors. Lawyers remain the authors
   of the knowledge and the judges of its quality, and legal operations
   owns the system that collects, qualifies, and maintains it. The
   in-house example is the CLOC Global Institute 2026 session led by
   legal operations and knowledge staff from JPMorganChase and
   Guggenheim Investments, whose speakers include an in-house knowledge
   management librarian and a managing director whose remit joins
   eDiscovery, legal operations, and knowledge management; the piece
   describes the session in its own words without quoting its title.
   The curator role is stated and then limited: the piece names the
   owner, the standards, the review and retirement cycle, and the
   measures, and it stops there. A later article will expand the
   operating model, the roles involved, and the case for CLOC and ACC
   to update their definitions. Article 1 states this shift in a
   sentence; this piece supplies the argument and links back to
   article 1 in the body.

6. **Curation includes deciding what AI must not see.** The risks are
   specific, and each one becomes a curation rule about what enters
   the corpus, who may retrieve it, when it is reviewed, and when it
   is retired.
   - Privilege. In United States v. Heppner (S.D.N.Y., written
     memorandum filed 2026-02-17), Judge Rakoff held that exchanges
     with a consumer generative AI tool were not protected by
     privilege or work product.
   - Confidentiality. In the Thomson Reuters 2026 data, 96% of
     professionals demand safeguards for confidential data.
   - Ethical walls and need-to-know access. The OWASP Top 10 for LLM
     Applications 2025 lists vector and embedding weaknesses as a
     top-ten risk, warns that "inadequate or misaligned access
     controls can lead to unauthorized access to embeddings containing
     sensitive information," and prescribes "permission-aware vector
     and embedding stores" with strict partitioning between groups
     (OWASP, list released 2024-11-17). Microsoft Learn documents how
     an enterprise index enforces the rule: the semantic index honors
     "the user identity-based access boundary so that the grounding
     process only accesses content that the current user is
     authorized to access" (2026-04-23); every item indexed through a
     Graph connector carries an access control list in which deny
     takes precedence over grant, and non-directory groups such as
     ethical-wall teams are replicated as external groups
     (2024-11-07); and Microsoft warns that "Overshared or poorly
     governed content can affect Copilot results and increase risk"
     (2026-08-17). The operational lesson is stated neutrally: any
     pipeline that copies documents into a separate store has to
     rebuild permission trimming, and OWASP treats the failure to do
     so as a top-ten weakness. Jenni Tellyn of 3Kites observes that
     stricter access means "you have to work even harder at your
     knowledge management."
   - Stale content. A superseded precedent that a lawyer would have
     recognized as out of date can be retrieved and applied by a
     system that does not.
   - Provenance. The department must be able to show which sources
     produced an answer.
   - Retention and meeting transcripts. Mayer Brown (2026-06-03) notes
     that AI notetaker transcripts create records that may be accessed
     by people who were not present and that they widen legal hold
     scope and discovery cost.
   - Unregulated tools. In the iManage Knowledge Work Benchmark Report
     2026 (vendor research from a document management provider; 3,185
     respondents across several industries), nearly one-third of
     organizations report a policy-impacting incident related to
     unregulated AI tools.

   The piece cites rulings as facts and describes operational
   consequences. It gives no legal advice on privilege.

7. **Where knowledge management sits in legal operations
   intelligence.** Near the end of the article, place knowledge
   management within the series. It supplies the source material,
   which is the context that AI needs. The ontology (article 3) gives
   that material structure by attaching it to the entities and actions
   it concerns, and that structure is what makes it actionable.
   Business intelligence (article 4) is the deterministic dimension,
   which consists of the measures and facts that are computed from
   records and are never generated by a model. The platform
   (article 2) is the set of department capabilities (process, people,
   and technology) that brings them together, with technology as the
   tangible instantiation. In the vocabulary of the existing library,
   this is the Memory layer of the Legal IQ stack, which Spaarke calls
   operational memory. Use that term once, at this point, with the
   link to `/why-spaarke/the-iq-stack`. Spaarke references stay
   limited: this sentence is the default, and one further mention is
   permitted where it helps the reader, for example a statement that
   Spaarke provides the Legal Operations Intelligence platform and
   supports both system-of-record and ontology-over-third-party-system
   modes, if the piece touches where the department's knowledge lives.
   No product features are described.

8. **Where a department starts.** The article ends on practice, with
   steps a department can take. A department can begin with an
   inventory of the knowledge sources it already has and who controls
   each one, a named owner in legal operations, and one recurring
   deliverable (NDA review, for example, or the answer to a common
   intake question) for which the corpus, the playbook, and a small
   evaluation set are built and maintained. Rules for review and
   retirement follow. Harbor's advice in its ILTACON 2026 brief, which
   draws on law firm sessions, is similar: "govern only the data behind
   a specific, recurring deliverable, such as a report produced
   regularly or a request fielded constantly, and expand from there"
   (confirmed verbatim). The close tells the reader what can be done
   in the next quarter and adds something new instead of restating
   the argument.

Mapping to the writer's six content types:

- Precedents: grounding, retrieval-augmented generation, embeddings
  and chunking (they are the corpus).
- Clause libraries: playbooks as configuration (fallback positions)
  and grounding (the approved text).
- Playbooks: system prompts and instructions, agent skills, playbook
  configuration files.
- Matter histories: retrieval and grounding (what the department did
  before); context engineering (situational context, the Weinberg
  point that context wins).
- Decision logs: evaluation sets (they record the right call and why)
  and few-shot examples.
- Expert corrections: gold-standard answers and evaluation sets (each
  correction is a labelled test case); the least collected and the
  most direct feedback signal.

## What this should NOT become

- **An introduction to knowledge management.** Law departments and
  legal operations have practised the discipline for a long time, and
  the reader has done so personally. The piece offers a current and
  advanced view of what AI changes, and it says in its opening that
  the function is established.
- **A repeat of the institutional knowledge article.** See the note
  under cross-references.
- **A law firm knowledge management article.** Most of the available
  evidence comes from law firm knowledge leaders. The piece is about
  in-house departments, so it must translate that evidence and state
  where the in-house record is thin.
- **A report that the industry has named legal operations the
  curator.** No body has done so. The article argues the case as its
  own, on the confirmed fact that the responsibility already sits with
  legal operations in most departments.
- **A retrieval tutorial or a vendor tour.** The context map is there
  to show alignment between knowledge management content and AI
  mechanisms, and each term gets one sentence of definition. Products
  may be named under the series naming rule, neutrally and with a
  source. The piece is about the department's knowledge and its
  management, and no product's features are the subject.
- **Knowledge management set against the ontology, business
  intelligence, or the platform.** The four work together, as the
  series decisions state.
- **A claim that departments train or fine-tune models.** The research
  does not support it. See arguments 2 and 3 for the permitted sense
  of the word "training," which is the sense legal AI systems use.
- **A full operating model for the curator.** The role is stated and
  limited to the owner, the standards, the review and retirement
  cycle, and the measures. A later article expands it.
- **Vendor statistics presented as neutral research.** The iManage and
  Harbor figures carry their source labels, the Gartner figure is
  labeled a prediction, and event-marketing statistics are left out.
- **Alarm about risk, or legal advice about privilege.** The piece
  cites rulings as facts and describes operational consequences.
- **A product piece.** Spaarke's term for the layer appears once, in
  argument 7, one further mention is permitted where it helps the
  reader, and the piece does not position Spaarke against any named
  vendor (`voice/brand-positioning.md` section 3).
- **Anything dated on or after the display date**, including the CLOC
  certification release, the Inside Legal KM London program, and the
  CCBJ recap of RLLB 2026. The ILTA 2026 survey passes since the
  display date moved to 2026-09-15 and is used with its law firm
  scope stated.
- The house word list applies (`voice/vocabulary.md` and
  `voice/examples/ai-tells.md`). Two terms need particular care in this
  piece: write "institutional knowledge," never "tribal knowledge,"
  and reserve "operational memory" for the single permitted use.
