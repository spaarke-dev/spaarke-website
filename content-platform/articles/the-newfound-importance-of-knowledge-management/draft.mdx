Legal departments have managed knowledge for years, and in most of them the responsibility already sits with legal operations. In the CLOC 2025 State of the Industry Report, 79% of respondents placed knowledge management within their legal operations team's responsibilities, which ranked it sixth among the 15 services listed. The figures come from the 2024 Harbor Law Department Survey, conducted with CLOC across 186 organizations. The CLOC Core 12 and the ACC Legal Operations Maturity Model 2.0 both name a knowledge management function, and neither text is recent. An earlier article in this library, [Institutional Knowledge Is Walking Out the Door](/why-spaarke/institutional-knowledge), made the case that a department's context leaves with the people who hold it.

What has changed is the reader. In many departments the first reader of that knowledge is now an AI system. In the Thomson Reuters 2026 AI in Professional Services Report, as summarized on the company's legal blog in July 2026, 47% of corporate legal departments said their legal teams were using generative AI, up from 23% a year earlier. The in-house figure passed the 41% law firms reported. A general-purpose model knows nothing about a department's positions, history, or standards, and everything it can see when it answers is what the department put in front of it.

A legal department's own knowledge has become the context on which the accuracy of its AI systems depends, and curating that knowledge for AI is now part of the legal operations mandate. Legal operations already carries the responsibility in most departments, and it already runs the systems in which the new sources of knowledge are created. AI changes five things about that work: what counts as knowledge, how it is captured and codified, where it goes, what it is for, and what has to be kept out of it.

## The constraint on legal AI has moved from the model to the knowledge

That change of reader arrived in the middle of an argument the industry was having with itself about model quality. Through 2024 and much of 2025 the question in legal technology was whether the models were good enough to be trusted with legal work. By 2026 the knowledge management community, the trade press, the publishers, and the consultancies had largely stopped asking it and had started describing an organization's own knowledge as the limiting factor.

Inside Practice built its New York program of April 2026 on the line "Artificial intelligence is no longer the bottleneck for law firms. Knowledge is." The sentence is event-organizer copy and states the position in its strongest available form, and the practitioners quoted elsewhere in the field put it in much the same terms.

Oz Benamram, writing in Artificial Lawyer's 2026 predictions in January 2026, described what the work now has to produce: "Clean, structured, context-rich KM (precedents, outcomes, expertise signals) becomes decisive." He read the 2026 SKILLS survey the same way two months later, reporting that firms "have identified the knowledge management layer as the next priority." That survey collected responses from 130 of the world's largest law firms, and the write-up appeared in March 2026 on the blog of the legal AI vendor Harvey.

The same account appears in how practitioners describe their own jobs. At a law firm knowledge management roundtable at ILTACON 2025, relayed by Artificial Lawyer in August 2025 from a vendor's notes of the session, knowledge professionals were described as "shifting from maintaining knowledge bases to orchestrating AI workflows and ensuring data quality." Harbor, a legal consultancy, reported from ILTACON in September 2026 that the recurring theme of the law firm knowledge management sessions was that "clean, connected data is the precondition for AI producing anything useful." Kristin Calve, writing from the same conference for Corporate Counsel Business Journal in August 2026, drew the consequence in her own sentence: "The bottleneck may not always be model quality; it may be record quality."

The survey evidence points the same way, although it comes from law firms rather than from corporate legal departments. The ILTA 2026 Technology Survey executive summary, published on September 14, 2026, reports that 94% of responding firms are now engaged with generative AI. Its sample is 508 firms representing more than 139,000 lawyers, and it describes the knowledge infrastructure those firms depend on, including enterprise search and expertise management, as badly underdeveloped, while reporting that the platform migration those firms undertook is largely finished. The equivalent argument for in-house departments was made earlier in 2026 by [The AI Readiness Gap in Legal Departments](/why-spaarke/the-ai-readiness-gap), which treats data and knowledge readiness as the precondition for useful AI rather than as a later phase of it.

Two qualifications belong with that consensus. Most of the voices in it are law firm knowledge leaders and technology vendors whose businesses improve if the conclusion holds, and the hard numbers behind it are thin. Stephen Embry is the field's most consistent skeptic on this point. Writing in Above the Law in August 2026 about a consultancy's law firm technology research, he objected to the evidence rather than to the claim: without obtaining the methodology and the information about the data, he wrote, "we have to take them with a grain of salt." The caution is worth keeping, and it leaves the argument standing, because the technical reasons that a department's own knowledge determines what its AI can answer do not rest on any of these surveys.

The same set of 2026 predictions carried a second observation that names what the department has to supply. Ed Walters of Clio wrote "Out: Prompt engineering. In: Context engineering." and Winston Weinberg of Harvey wrote "In 2026, context wins." Both are vendor voices, and both are making the same point about where the work has moved. The material that surrounds a question determines the answer, and a department holds most of that material itself.

## Context is what a department supplies

Context, in the sense those two lines use it, is a technical term before it is a slogan, and a department's own knowledge is a core part of what drives it. [How to Build the Legal Operations Intelligence Platform](/why-spaarke/building-the-legal-operations-intelligence-platform) names context as the platform's differentiator and gives it two sources, integration across the systems the department already runs and augmentation with the organization's own knowledge. In the AI sense used here, context is everything the model sees once both are combined, and the knowledge is the part that only the department can supply. The records in the matter system, the e-billing platform, and the document repository describe what happened. The department's knowledge describes what the department concluded, conceded, approved, or corrected, and why.

Anthropic's engineering team gave the term a working definition in September 2025. Context is "the set of tokens included when sampling from a large-language model (LLM)," and context engineering is "the set of strategies for curating and maintaining the optimal set of tokens (information) during LLM inference." Tokens are the units of text a model reads. The definition therefore amounts to saying that context is everything placed in front of the model for a single answer, whether it arrived from the question, a standing instruction, a retrieved document, or a tool the system called.

The vocabulary predates that post. Simon Willison's page of June 2025 collects the earlier formulations. It quotes Tobi Lütke on "the art of providing all the context for the task to be plausibly solvable by the LLM," and Andrej Karpathy on "the delicate art and science of filling the context window with just the right information for the next step."

The capacity is finite, and its usefulness falls off before it is full. The same Anthropic post describes context rot, the observation that a model's recall of what sits in its window degrades as the window fills, and it treats attention as a budget to be spent rather than as a container to be filled.

For a legal department the consequence is direct. Every precedent, playbook, transcript, and intake answer the department keeps competes for the same limited opening. A system that retrieves 40 plausible documents for a question that needed three has spent the budget badly. Selection is therefore part of the work, and curation is what makes it possible. A corpus in which every item carries a status, a date, and an owner can be filtered before retrieval, and a repository of everything the department ever filed cannot. Each part of that context reaches the model by a different route.

## Four mechanisms make a model specific to a department

A general-purpose model becomes useful to a particular department through four mechanisms: grounding, instruction, example, and evaluation. Each draws on a different kind of material the department holds, and each consumes an asset that someone has to build and keep current. They are worth taking in order, because departments routinely invest in the first and stop there, and it is the last two that tell a department whether the first is working. One worked example runs through all four. A department wants its assistant to answer questions about non-disclosure agreements, covering which positions the department takes, which it will concede, and what a business unit should do with an incoming form.

### Grounding consumes the corpus

Grounding ties an answer to source material supplied at the time the question is asked, so that the answer can be checked against something the department recognizes. Lewis and colleagues described the technique that does most of this work in a 2020 paper on retrieval-augmented generation (arXiv 2005.11401), which paired a pre-trained model with a searchable document store. [What Attorneys Need to Know About AI Architecture](/why-spaarke/what-attorneys-need-to-know-about-ai) explains how retrieval behaves in a legal setting, and that explanation is not repeated here. Microsoft describes its own semantic index as providing "the grounding data for knowledge retrieval" for Copilot (Microsoft Learn, page dated April 2026 and updated in August 2026), which is the same arrangement under a product name.

The asset the mechanism consumes is a corpus, meaning a body of the department's own material that is current, authoritative, permissioned, and tagged well enough to be found. In the NDA example the corpus is the approved template and the prior negotiated agreements, each with a known status and, where the department recorded it, a known outcome.

### Instruction consumes the playbook

Grounding tells the system what the department has, and instruction tells it what the department does. System prompts, negotiation playbooks, clause libraries with fallback positions, and the newer packaging of procedures as agent skills all carry standing rules: the department's position, the range it will accept, and the point at which a person has to decide. Anthropic describes agent skills as "folders that include instructions, scripts, and resources that Claude can load when needed" (October 2025). The legal plugin the company released on January 30, 2026, as reported by LawSites in February 2026, is "configurable to your organization's playbook," with the configuration held "in a local settings file to define standard positions, acceptable ranges, and escalation triggers." Both are cited for what the published pages say about the mechanism rather than as recommendations.

The asset is the playbook, and its quality is a legal judgment, because the positions in it are the department's positions and they go out of date when its risk appetite or the regulatory position changes. In the NDA example the playbook states the standard positions, the acceptable ranges, and the escalation triggers, and it is the document a lawyer has to approve.

### Examples consume the department's best work

Instruction states the rule, and an example shows what following it looks like. Brown and colleagues established in 2020 (arXiv 2005.14165) that models can be steered by a handful of worked examples placed in the prompt, a technique the field calls few-shot prompting, and it has remained standard practice since. What a department supplies at this point is its best work: the model documents, the memos a practice lead would hold up as the standard, and the intake answers that someone approved before they went out.

The selection matters. A system shown an average sample of the department's output will reproduce the average, and the average includes the memo written under deadline at the end of a quarter. In the NDA example the material is three of the department's strongest negotiated agreements, chosen by someone qualified to say why they are the strongest.

### Evaluation consumes approved answers

The first three mechanisms improve the system's chances, and the fourth is how a department finds out whether they worked. An evaluation set is a collection of real questions with approved answers and criteria for what counts as correct, and running it after a change tells the department whether accuracy moved.

The pattern is established in the research the field already publishes. The Vals Legal AI Report of February 2025 was built this way, and the report states that "The Consortium Firms together created the dataset used for the study." Eight firms, four of them named, supplied more than 500 samples of questions, reference documents, ideal responses, and correctness criteria, so finished work product became the benchmark. That is the same move a department makes when it turns its own closed matters into test cases.

In the NDA example the evaluation set is a dozen real questions the business has asked about NDAs, each with an answer a lawyer approved. When a lawyer corrects an answer the system produced, the correction becomes the next test case.

Taken together, the four mechanisms give the department's existing knowledge management output a new set of consumers. Every kind of content a department already maintains maps to at least one mechanism that consumes it (Exhibit 1). Precedents are the corpus, and they are what grounding and retrieval search. Clause libraries serve twice, as material to ground on and as configuration that states positions. Playbooks become system prompts, agent skills, and configuration files.

Matter histories feed retrieval and grounding, and they carry the situational detail that Weinberg's line points at, because a question about a counterparty is answered differently when the system can see what happened the last three times. Decision logs supply evaluation sets and few-shot examples, since a record of what the right call was is what a test case is made from. Expert corrections of AI output map to gold-standard answers and evaluation sets, and in most departments they are the least collected content and the most direct signal available.

## Curated retrieval still leaves roughly one answer in five wrong

The mapping in Exhibit 1 would settle the question if grounding on curated content were sufficient by itself, and the accuracy research puts a limit on what it achieves. In the Stanford RegLab study, now citable in its peer-reviewed form in the Journal of Empirical Legal Studies (volume 22, issue 2, 2025), the legal research tools tested from LexisNexis and Thomson Reuters "each hallucinate between 17% and 33% of the time." All of those tools are built on retrieval over curated and professionally maintained content.

Rebecca Fordon's review of the literature for AI Law Librarians in February 2026 attributes the better performance of legal platforms, relative to general-purpose assistants, to their search over "curated, verified legal databases with citators." She still concludes that "roughly one in five responses contains errors, even from top-tier specialized legal tools." The figures show that curation improves accuracy without delivering it.

Neither study can show what a particular department's error rate would be, because the tools tested search a published legal corpus while a department's assistant searches its own material, which is smaller, more specific, and maintained to a standard the department itself sets. The only instrument that measures a department's own rate is its own evaluation set. That is the argument for treating the fourth mechanism as an operating commitment rather than as a step in a pilot. [Legal AI Is Not Deterministic](/why-spaarke/probabilistic-vs-deterministic) sets out why the same question can produce different answers on different runs, which is also why one successful demonstration says little about a system's reliability.

A department's influence over the quality of its AI therefore runs through what it supplies at answer time and how it tests the result. The research behind this article found no source describing a legal department or a law firm fine-tuning a model on its own knowledge as a mainstream practice. The legal research products describe themselves as retrieval-based, and LexisNexis published a description of its own architecture in May 2024 that names retrieval-augmented generation as the technique behind the product. No dated Thomson Reuters equivalent was located.

A vendor case study of a custom-trained legal model, OpenAI's April 2024 write-up of its work with Harvey, concerns public case law rather than a customer's own knowledge. Absence of evidence is not proof that nobody does it, and a department planning the next 12 months still has to work with the levers it can reach today. Those are the corpus, the playbook, the examples, and the test set.

## What counts as knowledge has widened past finished work product

All four assets have to be built out of material the department already produces, and traditional knowledge management collected only part of that material. That part was finished work product: memos, templates, precedents, and know-how notes, all of them written by someone who intended them to be reused. AI systems can draw on a much wider set of material, most of which the department already produces and almost none of which was written to be knowledge:

- email and chat;
- meeting transcripts;
- matter metadata;
- negotiation and redline history;
- decision logs and approvals;
- outside counsel work product;
- billing narratives;
- intake requests and the answers given to them;
- expert corrections of AI output.

Each of these teaches a system something that a finished document omits. Negotiation and redline history shows which positions the department conceded and under what conditions, which is the information a signed agreement deliberately removes. Decision logs and approvals record what the right call was and who was entitled to make it, which is what an evaluation case and a gold-standard example are built from. Outside counsel work product carries reasoning the department paid for and rarely absorbed, and it is the material an assistant will otherwise answer around. Billing narratives describe, in the words of the people who did the work, what a matter type required in practice, which is a better guide to scoping than a budget template. Intake requests and the answers given to them record what the business asks and what legal has already said, and in most departments that exchange survives only in individual mailboxes. Matter metadata carries the classifications that let any of the rest be compared across matters. Meeting transcripts and chat hold reasoning that never reached a document at all.

Expert corrections of AI output may be the most valuable source in the list, and they are almost certainly the least collected. Each one records a specific gap between what the system produced and what the department's standard requires, which is the signal a gold-standard example or an evaluation case is made from. A department that captures nothing else from its AI deployment should capture these, because every other source on the list describes work the department did before the system existed, and this one measures the system's distance from the department's judgment.

Practitioners describe the same widening, although the published accounts come from law firms. Tom Humberstone of Womble Bond Dickinson told the consultancy Tiger Eye in December 2025 that knowledge repositories "have evolved beyond traditional, curated know-how to encompass a broader set of sources now." Benamram's list of what a firm needs runs to precedents, outcomes, and expertise signals, which reaches past documents into the record of what happened and who knows about it. The ILTACON 2025 roundtable described the practical state of that wider material as knowledge "scattered across OneDrive, SharePoint, Teams, and personal folders," which is a fair description of most in-house environments as well.

The evidence here is uneven, and it is worth saying where it runs out. The research behind this article found no neutral source that describes billing narratives, decision logs and approvals, outside counsel work product, intake answers, or expert corrections being managed as knowledge assets in a legal department. Those five items are this article's own argument rather than a reported practice, and they are offered on that basis. The argument for them rests on the mechanism. Each is material that answers a question an AI system will be asked, and each sits today outside whatever the department calls its knowledge base.

## The work is capture and codification, and the destination is the ontology

Naming the sources is the easy part of the problem. Most of the material in that list is created in the course of doing something else: a position taken during a negotiation, a reason given when an invoice line is reduced, an answer typed into an email at intake, a correction a lawyer makes to a draft an AI system produced. Each of these arrives as a by-product of the work, and storing it changes nothing on its own. The practical work has three steps, and legal operations can name an owner for each of them.

Capture is the act that turns work into a record. It means that the obligation buried in an executed contract is extracted and written down as an obligation, that the position conceded in a negotiation is recorded with its date and its reason, that the reviewer who reduces an invoice line records why, that the answer given at intake is filed as an answer to a question and not as a sent message, and that a lawyer's correction of an AI draft is kept instead of overwritten.

Each of these is a small addition to a step that already exists in the work, and each depends on a system that asks for it at the moment the work happens. In the NDA example, capture is the line recorded when the department accepts an indemnity cap it would normally resist, together with the date, the type of counterparty, and the reason it was accepted.

Codification gives each record a defined shape. The record carries an owner, a date, a status that says whether it is current, superseded, or withdrawn, and permissions that travel with it wherever it goes. Codification is what separates a knowledge record from a note, and it is the step departments skip when they treat a shared drive as a knowledge program. The shape does not have to be elaborate, and five fields applied consistently are worth more than a taxonomy of 300 terms that three people understand.

The destination of a codified record is the entity it concerns. [The Legal Operations Intelligence Ontology](/why-spaarke/legal-operations-ontology) describes the entity and action model a legal operations platform needs, and a knowledge record becomes useful at scale once it is bound to the matter, the party, the policy, or the obligation it is about. A measure that reaches the matter then reaches the knowledge attached to it, and so does a workflow, and so does an agent. That is the division the series keeps: knowledge management supplies the material, and the ontology organizes it and makes it actionable. A department that builds one without the other ends up with either a well-ordered library its systems cannot read or an entity model with nothing attached to it.

## Knowledge now trains the systems that produce the work product

The four mechanisms also change what a department's knowledge is for. Finished work product used to be the end product of the discipline: a lawyer searched for the memo, found it, and reused it, and that use continues and remains valuable. The larger use now is as the material that trains the systems that help produce the work product in the first place.

"Training" here means what the four mechanisms do, which is grounding, instruction, example, and evaluation. The word also carries a narrower technical sense, changing a model's weights through further training, and the absence of evidence described above gives a department little reason to pursue it. In practice the shift means maintaining the corpus, the playbook, the examples, and the test set so that a system's answers are relevant to this department and can be verified against something the department approved.

The shift changes who reads the department's knowledge first, and how often. A precedent bank consulted by 12 lawyers a few times a month has different requirements from the same content consulted by an assistant on every question the department's business clients ask. The change in volume matters less than the change in the reader, because an assistant applies whatever it retrieves without asking whether the memo has aged or whether the position still holds.

## A document that grounds an answer needs an author, a date, a status, and permissions

A lawyer who pulled a memo from the precedent bank supplied the judgment the document itself lacked. She could tell which parts of it had aged, which position had been taken under a regulatory regime that has since changed, and whether the partner who wrote it would still stand behind the reasoning. The requirements a department set for that memo followed from her presence, and they were modest: the document had to be findable, and it had to be roughly right. A document that grounds an AI answer has to carry what the lawyer used to supply. That means a known author and date, a status that says whether the item is current, superseded, or withdrawn, and permissions that state who may see it. Where the department recorded the outcome the document produced, that belongs with it too.

There is a technical reason that material has to travel with the content rather than sit in a column beside it. Retrieval systems index documents as passages. Each document is cut into pieces small enough to embed and to search, and a single piece is what reaches the model. Microsoft's guidance for Azure AI Search (page dated June 2026 and updated in August 2026) suggests starting from a chunk size of "512 tokens (approximately 2,000 characters)" with an overlap of 25% between neighboring passages. A 30-page agreement enters the index as several dozen fragments, and any one of them can be retrieved on its own.

What a fragment loses is the wrapper. The cover page, the folder it sat in, the version label, and the access rights that governed the file are properties of the document, and a passage carries them only if the pipeline attaches them to every chunk it creates. That inference is this article's own rather than a finding in the Microsoft documentation, and it comes with a test a director can run. Ask whoever operates the index which fields travel with a retrieved passage. Where status and permissions are missing from the answer, the system can return a withdrawn clause from a file that only the deal team was meant to open, and nothing in the output will say so.

The same problem appears at a larger scale when material is copied rather than indexed where it lives. Josh Baxter, chief executive of the document management provider NetDocuments, told Corporate Counsel Business Journal in August 2026 that copying content into separate application stores produces "micro and incomplete DMSs." Kristin Calve glossed the phrase in the same piece as "partial representations of institutional knowledge assembled for particular applications and potentially governed differently." The observation was made about law firms, and the arrangement it describes is common in corporate departments, where a contract tool, an intake portal, and a research assistant each hold their own copy of overlapping material. A department that allows every application its own copy acquires as many curation problems as it has applications, so the standard it writes has to name where the authoritative copy lives.

Two short examples show what the criteria do. The first is a superseded position. A department withdrew an indemnity position after a regulatory change, and the memo that argued for it stayed in the repository, because departments rarely delete memos. A lawyer who found it would place it by its date and set it aside. A retrieval system with no status field ranks it by how closely it matches the question, finds it highly relevant precisely because it addresses the topic asked about, and passes it to the model as authority. A status field applied at entry is what keeps the withdrawn memo out of the results, which is why status belongs in the curation standard and not in the discretion of whoever files the document.

The second example is an answer the department has already given. A business unit asks whether a vendor contract needs a data processing addendum, and a lawyer answers by email in two paragraphs. Six months later a second business unit asks the same question. Where the first answer was kept as knowledge, with its author, its date, and its status, the assistant grounds on it, cites it, and the department can show which source produced what the business was told. That ability to show the source is what provenance means in operational terms, and it matters more than the drafting time the stored answer saved.

## The frameworks that define the function have not caught up

None of those criteria appear in the texts that define the function. The CLOC Core 12 describes knowledge management as implementing "processes and standards to collect, structure, and organize knowledge to save time and improve outcomes." The ACC Legal Operations Maturity Model 2.0 defines it as "The process of capturing, distributing, and effectively using both structured and tacit knowledge assets, from work products (such as legal memos) to understanding of an issue due to prior experience." Both are accurate accounts of the discipline as it was practiced when they were written, and neither text is recent.

Neither knowledge management text mentions AI. The ACC model does mention AI in other functions, including eDiscovery, intellectual property, metrics and analytics, and technology, which makes the silence in the knowledge management function more noticeable than it would be in a document that leaves the subject alone. Read against the four mechanisms, both definitions assume a human reader. Saving time and improving outcomes describes a lawyer who finds a precedent and does not draft from scratch, and distributing describes moving material to people who will judge it when it arrives.

The research behind this article found no framework or association text that describes what the function becomes when the first reader of the department's knowledge is a system that applies what it retrieves without judgment. That description is what this article supplies, and neither framework should be read as having offered it. Both bodies have defined the function carefully for the practice their members ran, and the definitions have simply been overtaken by a change in who reads the output.

AI is reaching CLOC's program by a different route. On August 19, 2026 the organization announced an AI Intensive, "created and led by legal operations practitioners." A first in-person workshop was announced for October 2026 in New York for about 40 participants, covering "real-time prompting exercises, workflow and agent design, and implementation planning." The phrase "knowledge management" does not appear anywhere in that release, and the Core 12 text is unchanged. Training of that kind addresses what a team does with AI tools, and the question a curator has to answer is what the department's knowledge has to become before those tools can be accurate.

## Legal operations already holds the responsibility

The question a department reaches at this point is who owns that work, and in most departments the answer is already on paper. The CLOC 2025 figure quoted at the outset places knowledge management inside the legal operations team's responsibilities in 79% of the 186 organizations surveyed, ahead of nine other services on the same list of 15. Nothing in this article proposes moving the responsibility. What has changed is what holding it requires, and the distance between the two is where most departments now sit.

Law firms meet the same problem with more people. A large firm carries a knowledge department, professional support lawyers who do nothing else, and in many cases a chief knowledge officer. The ILTA 2026 Technology Survey of 508 responding firms reports that many of them expect to add headcount across several functions, with knowledge management named among them. That survey covers law firms only, and the in-house comparison is not close. The median legal operations function in the CLOC 2025 report was 5 full-time equivalents, a figure unchanged since 2023, and the same team carries knowledge management alongside the 11 other services that at least half of the departments surveyed placed within legal operations.

The ACC maturity model lists "At least one full-time dedicated KM professional" among the 13 markers of its advanced stage, which places a single dedicated person at the top of the scale rather than in the middle of it. The research behind this article found no in-house knowledge management staffing or budget benchmark at all, so the size of the gap between what the work needs and what departments staff cannot be stated with a number.

Mary O'Carroll, speaking to Law360 Pulse in June 2026 about the new roles departments are creating around AI, observed that "With these roles that are new and not well understood, it's even more challenging to get that headcount and get it approved." A director who intends to ask for the position should expect to build the case from the department's own numbers rather than from a published benchmark.

The case for legal operations as the curator is ours to make, because the research behind this article found no statement by any industry body assigning the role to anyone. What CLOC and Harbor do say is adjacent. Both frame the legal operations role in AI as governance and operating model. CLOC's 2026 State of the Industry Report, drawn from 135 law departments and released in March 2026, reports that 85% of departments "have dedicated AI oversight or resources." Oversight of a system that answers from the department's own material has to include that material, because it is the part of the system the department controls. Three observations carry the rest of the case.

First, legal operations already runs the systems in which the new sources of knowledge are created. Intake, matter management, e-billing, outside counsel management, and the technology stack sit with the function in most departments, and the intake answers, matter metadata, billing narratives, and outside counsel work product described earlier all arrive through them. Capturing the position taken in a negotiation or the reason an invoice line was reduced means changing the moment at which the work is recorded, and legal operations owns those moments already.

Second, where the role is not assigned, it fragments across functions. Harbor's brief on ILTACON 2026 reports "a recurring frustration raised in roundtable discussions" over "fragmented ownership of AI security and governance decisions across IT, Risk, the General Counsel's office, and knowledge management." The consultancy concludes that "Governance needs a clear owner and a defined, visible conversation." Those were law firm roundtables, and the research found no equivalent in-house data, so the observation is an analogy rather than a measurement. The in-house version of the list is recognizable enough: IT owns the tenant, the general counsel's office owns privilege, procurement owns the vendor contract, and the corpus is owned by nobody in particular.

Third, curation is operational work of a kind the function already performs. It consists of naming an owner, writing standards, running review cycles, retiring material, setting permissions, and reporting on what the program produced, which is the same shape as outside counsel management and spend governance. Lawyers remain the authors of the department's knowledge and the judges of its quality, because deciding whether a position is still the department's position is a legal judgment and not an operational one. Legal operations owns the system that collects the material, qualifies it, keeps it current, and measures whether it is used.

Seen as operational work, the program is a loop, and it keeps running after the deployment it was built for. The sources of knowledge feed a curation stage that assigns an owner, applies the standards, reviews and retires material, and sets permissions. Curation feeds the four mechanisms that ground, instruct, exemplify, and evaluate, and those mechanisms produce AI output. A lawyer's correction of that output returns to the sources, so the corpus improves with use (Exhibit 2). The loop is also where the measures sit. A department can count the questions its assistant answered from the department's own material, the share of those answers a reviewer corrected, and the age of the items retrieval returns most often. All three report on the state of the corpus, and each one points at a specific item a curator can fix.

One in-house arrangement of this kind is on the public record. At the CLOC Global Institute in May 2026, a session on fragmented legal information was led by legal operations and knowledge staff whom the agenda lists at JPMorganChase and Guggenheim Investments. Its speakers included a knowledge management librarian and a managing director whose remit joins eDiscovery, legal operations, and knowledge management. The session description states that "legal operations struggle with fragmented information and missed collaboration opportunities." A single conference session is not evidence of a trend, and the composition of the panel shows the two functions being staffed and presented together in departments large enough to do it.

We would name the owner in legal operations and give that owner four things: the standards that say what enters the corpus and in what shape, the review and retirement cycle, the permissions rules, and the measures that report whether the program is working. The role stops there in this article. [The New Mandate for Legal Operations](/why-spaarke/managing-legal-operations) sets out why the curation of the department's knowledge is settling on legal operations, and this section supplies the argument for what that curation now has to produce.

The case is continuous with an older one, because [Legal Ops Is Not IT for Lawyers](/why-spaarke/legal-ops-is-not-it-for-lawyers) already held that strategic legal operations builds institutional memory, and that a function occupied with administering tools is doing something else. The operating model this implies, the roles it needs, and the case for CLOC and ACC to revise their definitions are larger subjects than one article can settle, and they belong in a piece of their own.

## Curation includes deciding what AI must not see

Curation decides what enters the corpus, and the harder half of that decision is what stays out. Each of the exposures below has a documented record behind it, and each ends in a rule that a curation standard can state in a line. The rulings are reported here as facts, and the piece offers no advice on privilege, which is a matter for each department and its own counsel.

Privilege is the exposure that moved first. In United States v. Heppner, in the Southern District of New York, Judge Rakoff filed a written memorandum on February 17, 2026 (ECF No. 27). It held that a defendant's exchanges with a consumer generative AI tool were protected by neither the attorney-client privilege nor the work product doctrine. The facts concern an individual using a consumer product rather than an enterprise deployment operating at counsel's direction, so the holding does not settle the enterprise question. Its operational consequence is easier to state than its legal reach. Which tool produced a record, and under whose terms, is part of what a department has to know about that record, and a corpus assembled without the field cannot answer the question later.

Professionals state the confidentiality requirement themselves. In the Thomson Reuters 2026 Future of Professionals Report, as summarized on the company's legal blog in July 2026, 96% of respondents said that AI tools must carry safeguards for confidential data. Respondents were describing what they demand rather than what their organizations have built, so the figure measures expectation. The expectation converts into a rule about entry: material that carries a confidentiality obligation to a third party belongs in the corpus only where the retrieval layer can honor the obligation as reliably as the people who signed it.

Ethical walls and need-to-know access are where the technical detail matters most, and they are the direct consequence of the chunking step described earlier. The OWASP Top 10 for Large Language Model Applications 2025, released in November 2024, includes vector and embedding weaknesses among the ten. It warns that "Inadequate or misaligned access controls can lead to unauthorized access to embeddings containing sensitive information." It prescribes "permission-aware vector and embedding stores" together with "strict logical and access partitioning of datasets in the vector database," so that one group's material cannot surface in another group's answers.

OWASP is a standards body rather than a supplier, and researchers have demonstrated the weakness it names. A 2024 preprint from the University of Texas at Austin describes how content a user should not see can be drawn out of a retrieval system through the caching it performs.

Enterprise indexes are built to prevent that failure. Microsoft's documentation for its semantic index (page dated April 2026 and updated in August 2026) states that indexing continues to honor "the user identity-based access boundary so that the grounding process only accesses content that the current user is authorized to access." Items brought in through a Graph connector carry an access control list, and the connector documentation (page dated November 2024 and updated in August 2025) notes that "The accessType value deny takes precedence over grant." Groups that do not exist in the directory, such as ethical-wall teams, are replicated as external groups so that the deny travels with the content.

Microsoft's security guidance for Copilot (page dated August 2026) also warns that "Overshared or poorly governed content can affect Copilot results and increase risk." The rule reads the same from either side, because an index enforces only the permissions it is given, so a library that was shared with the whole company becomes a source that any employee's question can reach.

The operational lesson is neutral between architectures. Any pipeline that copies documents into a separate store has to rebuild the permission trimming the source system performed, and OWASP treats the failure to do so as a top-ten weakness. Jenni Tellyn of 3Kites put the management consequence plainly to the consultancy Tiger Eye in December 2025, observing that where access is tightened "you have to work even harder at your knowledge management."

Where the department's knowledge physically sits therefore bears on privilege and confidentiality as much as on convenience, a question [AI Without Giving Away the Keys](/why-spaarke/ai-without-giving-away-the-keys) takes up in detail. Spaarke provides the Legal Operations Intelligence platform in both modes, as the system of record for legal work and as ontology architecture over the systems a department already runs. Whichever mode a department chooses, the permission model has to be settled before any content is indexed, because a corpus inherits only the boundaries its source systems were able to express.

Stale content needs no new evidence, because the superseded indemnity memo is the whole case. Every item carries a status and a review date, and retrieval filters on status rather than leaving that judgment to the reader. The reader may be a system with no way to notice that a position is five years old and withdrawn.

Provenance follows from the intake-answer example, which works only because the answer was stored with its author, its date, and its status. An item that cannot be traced to a person and a date does not enter the corpus, whatever its content. An answer the department cannot trace is an answer it cannot defend to a regulator, to a court, or to the business unit that acted on it.

Retention now reaches material that no one drafted. Mayer Brown's client alert of June 2026 on AI notetakers describes how the transcripts they produce create records that "may be accessed by individuals who were not present" and widen the scope of legal holds and the cost of discovery. The sequence is easy to picture. A notetaker records a matter meeting at which strategy is discussed, the transcript enters the corpus, and it becomes retrievable by everyone with access to the matter. It is then discoverable on the same terms as any other business record. Retention and disposition therefore apply at the point of entry, because a corpus is a records population and the department's schedule governs it as it governs any other.

Unregulated tools undo every rule above. The iManage Knowledge Work Benchmark Report 2026 is vendor research from a document management provider, published in February 2026 and fielded in September and October 2025 among 3,185 business and technology decision-makers in 26 countries across legal, accounting and tax, financial services, and asset management. In it, nearly one third of organizations reported an incident involving unregulated AI tools that had an effect on policy.

The figure comes from a supplier with an interest in the conclusion, and the mechanism behind it needs no survey. A lawyer who cannot get a usable answer from the governed system pastes the document into an ungoverned one, and the material leaves the boundary the department's rules describe. Curation quality is therefore a security control as well as an accuracy measure, because a corpus that is not good enough to use will be worked around.

## Knowledge management supplies the context, and the ontology gives it structure

Those rules describe a program, and the program is one part of a larger capability. This series has taken the subject of [What Is Legal Operations Intelligence?](/why-spaarke/what-is-legal-operations-intelligence) in four parts. Knowledge management supplies the source material, which is the context an AI system needs in order to answer in this department's terms rather than in general ones. The other three give that material structure, measure what can be counted, and hold the capability together.

[The Legal Operations Intelligence Ontology](/why-spaarke/legal-operations-ontology) is the entity and action model that provides the structure, and the relationship between the two functions is a division of labor rather than a hierarchy. Knowledge management decides what the department knows and what condition that knowledge is in. The ontology decides what each record is about and what can be done from there.

[A Business Intelligence Approach to Legal Operations](/why-spaarke/business-intelligence-for-legal-operations) covers the deterministic dimension, the measures and facts computed from the records themselves and never generated by a model. The two dimensions answer different questions, and the distinction is worth keeping in view. What an employment matter cost last year is a computation over records, and what the department's position on an indemnity cap has been is a retrieval over curated knowledge. A department that confuses them will either ask a model to do arithmetic or ask a report to explain a negotiation.

[How to Build the Legal Operations Intelligence Platform](/why-spaarke/building-the-legal-operations-intelligence-platform) covers the capability that brings the three together across process, people, and technology, with technology the part of the capability that a department builds and operates. In the vocabulary this library has used since [The Legal IQ Stack: Data, Memory, Inference](/why-spaarke/the-iq-stack), the department's curated knowledge is the Memory layer, which Spaarke calls operational memory. The term names what the department has learned, held in a form its systems can act on.

## A department can start with one recurring deliverable

The program described here is large enough to stall if a department attempts all of it at once. The narrower start is an inventory of the knowledge sources the department already has, in the list given earlier, with the name of whoever controls each one today. The inventory usually returns what the ILTACON 2025 roundtable described, with much of the material sitting in OneDrive, SharePoint, Teams, and personal folders, and that result is itself the argument for naming an owner.

Harbor's advice to law firms after ILTACON 2026 applies in-house without modification. The consultancy recommends that a firm "govern only the data behind a specific, recurring deliverable, such as a report produced regularly or a request fielded constantly, and expand from there." Scope set that way can be tested inside a quarter, and it yields a corpus small enough for one person to curate and an evaluation set small enough for a lawyer to approve.

The NDA thread followed through this article is one such deliverable, and its four assets are already named. The corpus is the approved template and the prior negotiated agreements, each with its status and, where it was recorded, its outcome. The playbook states the standard positions, the acceptable ranges, and the escalation triggers. The examples are three of the department's strongest negotiated agreements. The evaluation set is a dozen questions the business has asked, each with an answer a lawyer approved. The answer to a common intake question, such as whether a vendor contract needs a data processing addendum, works the same way and takes even less material to assemble.

A legal operations director who wants to begin in the next quarter has a sequence available. Name the owner, inventory the sources and who controls each one, choose the single deliverable, and build the corpus, the playbook, the examples, and the small evaluation set for it. Write the review and retirement rules in the same pass rather than afterwards, because retrofitting a status field onto a corpus already in use costs more than setting it at entry. Those rules are also what make the second deliverable cheaper to build than the first.

What grows in the meantime is the cost of starting later. The next assistant the department deploys will ground on whatever repository it is pointed at. An ungoverned repository answers confidently from superseded positions and from passages the person asking was never entitled to see, because the system applies what it retrieves without the judgment the lawyer who filed it would have applied. Errors of that kind reach decisions faster than the old ones did, and they arrive with a citation attached.
