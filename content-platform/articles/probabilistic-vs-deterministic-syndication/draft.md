Generative AI is probabilistic. That isn't a defect.
It's the property that determines where AI belongs in legal work — and where it doesn't.

Take billing as the example. A rule engine that rejects invoices over a rate cap is deterministic — same input, same output, every time. An AI system that identifies "potentially non-compliant billing patterns" is probabilistic — it surfaces candidates from patterns, not from a rulebook. Both useful. Different in kind. Both should sit in your stack — but not in the same place.

The same split runs through legal work — rules and math on one side, judgement and pattern-recognition on the other. Most of the operational stack already does both. Most of the conversation about AI in 2026 still treats them as one thing.

Agent systems make this distinction load-bearing.

Inside an agent system, the first probabilistic step doesn't stay one step. A small uncertainty in the initial relevance call compounds across every downstream action — unless deterministic guardrails or explicit human gates sit at the consequential points. The single-step error rate looks tolerable in isolation. The end-to-end error rate does not.

Good legal AI tools surface the probabilistic layer rather than hiding it. Grounded citations. Where-inference-enters markers. Human gates at the points that touch the operational record. The reader who knows which kind of computation they're looking at can act on the output responsibly.
