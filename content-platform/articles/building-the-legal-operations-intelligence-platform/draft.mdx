Most large legal departments have already taken the governance step with AI. CLOC's 2026 State of the Industry report, released on March 2, 2026, draws on 135 departments with median revenue of US$13 billion. It found that 85% have a dedicated resource or committee for AI oversight and that 80% name technology strategy as a legal operations focus area. The department a reader recognizes bought its tools, seated that committee, and has been learning what they are good for since.

What that department has learned sits with the analysts who ran the pilots and in the email threads that record what they tried, out of the next tool's reach. Axiom's 2026 Legal AI Survey, fielded in March 2026, reached 528 in-house legal leaders, 77% of them at companies above US$1 billion in revenue. It found that 7% had scaled AI beyond pilots and that 83% "cannot measure whether their AI spending is working" (Axiom articles page, June 29, 2026). Axiom is an alternative legal services provider and an interested party. The figures place departments early on the learning curve, and measurement is next. Chris Frickland, Axiom's VP of AI Solutions, puts the difficulty around the tool: "The layer around it is where value either shows up or it doesn't."

A legal operations intelligence platform is the department's own capability, across process, people, and technology, to consolidate information from every source and bring each decision, with the context it needs, to the person who has the authority to make it. A department builds that capability by starting from legal's role in the company and from the decisions it makes, and it adds software and AI models once that foundation is in place. The platform is what allows the experience a department gains with AI to accumulate in its processes, its roles, and its connected information rather than resetting with every tool it adds. [What Is Legal Operations Intelligence?](/why-spaarke/what-is-legal-operations-intelligence) defines the category.

The word "platform" carries two meanings in this field, and both are in use. In this article a platform is the department's capabilities across process, people, and technology, and technology is the part of those capabilities a department can log into, budget for, and renew. The word also names the technology a provider supplies, and the two readings sit together without needing to be reconciled. [The AI Readiness Gap in Legal Departments](/why-spaarke/the-ai-readiness-gap) argued that the tool is rarely what holds a department back. This article says what the department has to build instead, and in what order.

## Legal's role in the company sets what the platform must do

That order starts outside the department, with what the company needs from legal. Legal supplies two kinds of input to the business. The strategic input is risk appetite, market entry, M&A, regulatory posture, and governance, and it reaches the board and the executive committee. The tactical input is the contract that has to be signed this quarter, the dispute that has to be defended, the employment question a manager asks on Tuesday, and the advice a business unit needs this week. A platform designed without a clear view of both optimizes the department for itself, which produces better internal reporting and no change in what the business receives.

Legal's position in the corporate structure has strengthened. In the ACC 2026 Chief Legal Officers Survey, published in January 2026 with 1,049 participants across 43 countries, a record 84% of chief legal officers said they report to the chief executive, and 79% said they almost always attend board meetings. The same survey found that 74% provide proactive strategic counsel. Read together, the figures describe a function that sits close to the decisions it advises on. The survey states the change plainly: "legal expertise is now integrated into business planning during the inception of projects rather than at the point of crisis."

What legal contributes from that position is harder to see from outside it. The Thomson Reuters Institute's 2026 State of the Corporate Law Department, published on March 24, 2026, is built on more than 2,300 interviews with corporate general counsel. It reports that 86% of general counsel see legal as a significant contributor to business objectives, while 17% of other C-suite executives agree. The report does not say how many executives outside legal it spoke to, so the second figure should be read as a direction rather than as a precise measure. Even at that resolution the distance between the two figures matters. Legal's seat is secure while what it contributes remains hard for the rest of the company to see, and that is the condition the platform has to change (Exhibit 1).

Finance registers the gap first, because legal's spend appears in the budget while the reasoning behind the spend does not, a point [Breaking the Silo Between Legal, Finance, and the Business](/why-spaarke/breaking-the-silo) develops. A platform that makes legal's decisions and their outcomes legible to the business therefore has to carry the reasoning alongside the numbers. Risk is the clearest case, because it is the strategic input the business asks for most often and the one least likely to be recorded anywhere a business partner can read.

## The platform is organized around decisions, and data is one ingredient

Legibility of that kind follows from how the department organizes the platform. A legal operations intelligence platform consolidates information from all sources and provides the relevant context in order to better understand a situation, assess the options, and act on it. Consolidation is the half most departments recognize, because they have attempted it with reporting projects. Context is the half that decides whether the result is useful, and it is what a department that has gathered its records and still cannot answer a question about one matter is missing.

For a legal department the sources are known and countable: the matter and spend systems, the document management system, contract repositories, email and other communications, finance and business systems, outside counsel submissions, and the department's own rules and models. None of them was built to be read alongside the others.

The department organizes the platform around the set of decisions it makes. [The New Mandate for Legal Operations](/why-spaarke/managing-legal-operations) sets out the working scope of the function, which covers its role in technology, knowledge management, resource allocation, and outside counsel management. Risk belongs in that list for the purpose of this argument, because the strategic input described above is a stream of risk decisions and the department is judged on how it handles them. Each of those areas is a place where someone decides something, and the platform is judged by whether decisions in those areas become easier to make well and easier to carry out.

Data is one ingredient of a decision. The others are the reasoning applied to it, whether that reasoning is a rule, a calculation, or a model; the action that carries the decision out; and the controls that keep the action within policy. A reporting environment supplies the first ingredient well and leaves the other three to whoever is reading the report.

The wider analytics field has arrived at the same position. Gartner's data and analytics trends, published on June 16, 2026 under Carlie Idoine and read here through a reprint, predict that explicitly modeled business decisions will be five times more trusted and 80% faster than ungoverned decisions by 2029. Gartner has observed nothing here; it is projecting three years out. The unit it models is the decision, and that is the unit this argument takes as well.

## Context from every system and from the organization's knowledge sets the platform apart

A decision needs more than the records that describe its subject. What sets a legal operations intelligence platform apart is that it combines data and information with context. Consolidation on its own produces a larger pile of records, and context is what makes those records mean something to the person who has to act.

Context here has three parts. One is the relationships among the records, such as the matter behind an invoice, the engagement terms behind a rate, and the person responsible for each. Another is the organization's own knowledge: its policies, its playbooks, its precedents, and its history. The last is the record of what the department decided in situations like the one in front of it, which is the part that almost no department can retrieve today.

Context reaches the platform from two directions, and a platform needs both. Integration across the systems the department runs brings the related records into one view, so that a matter is seen with its documents, its invoices, its communications, and its people. Augmentation with organizational knowledge supplies what no system of record captures, because the policy that governs a decision and the playbook that shapes it are not fields in a matter record.

Analysts outside legal state the same requirement. Forrester analysts Boris Evelson, Indranil Bandyopadhyay, Charlie Dai, and Noel Yuhanna wrote on June 2, 2026 that semantic layers, long used to keep business intelligence consistent, now also give agents "the governed context needed to turn natural language into accurate queries and actions". They recommend the semantic layer as the right starting point for most organizations. That recommendation is an analyst judgment, and it names at enterprise scale the same dependency a legal department meets inside its own walls.

Both halves of the context requirement carry enough weight to need their own treatment. Organizational knowledge as the context that AI needs is the subject of the fifth article in this series. The model that holds the context, and that makes the relationships among records available to a query, is the subject of the third.

## A single system of record sees one dimension of a matter

One system cannot supply that context, and the reason is structural. Every system of record a department runs, whether it manages matters, contracts, documents, or invoices, is bounded by its own data model. It holds what its fields were designed to hold. Where no field exists for a piece of information, the system cannot store it, cannot report on it, and cannot make it available to anything reading from it.

The consequence is that each system sees one dimension of the same matter. The e-billing platform knows what was billed and against which rate, and it holds nothing about why the work was necessary. The matter system carries the budget and the assigned lawyer but not the concessions in the engagement letter. The document management system holds that letter and cannot say which invoices it governs. What the department knows about a matter that never fit a field lives where it has always lived, in email, in documents, and in people.

This is an observation about data models and not about vendors, and it holds for any single system, including one sold as a legal operations platform. A department that replaces four systems with one has changed which data model bounds what it can see, and it remains bounded by a data model. The practical problem sits one step further on, because the information a single decision needs is spread across several of these systems at once.

## Matters, invoices, documents, and communications sit in separate systems

Consider a mid-size legal function with about 200 open matters, a panel of firms, an e-billing platform, a matter system, and a document management system. Nothing in that inventory is unusual and nothing in it is wrong. The systems were bought at different times, by different sponsors, to solve different problems, and each does its own job well. What nobody bought was the connection among them, so the department reconciles them by hand, and an analyst opens three applications and a spreadsheet to answer a question about one matter.

Departments report the consequence more readily than they report the cause. The 2025 EY Law General Counsel Study, released on April 9, 2025, surveyed 1,000 general counsel and chief legal officers at companies above US$1 billion in annual revenue across 21 countries. Of those respondents, 52% named disorganized data as a barrier to change, 44% named disconnected legal and business platforms, and 41% named limited access to accurate data. EY Law sells legal services and is an interested party in that finding. The study records what general counsel said was in their way. It counts no systems and measures no reconciliation effort, and the research behind this article found no neutral count of systems per legal department, so we offer none here.

Set beside the 75% of the same respondents who said they were developing or refining their legal technology or data strategies, the figures suggest that departments widely recognize the problem and are still designing their response. What a department fixes first is therefore the live question, and the answer depends on what it wants the platform to do once the information is in one place.

## The loop from insight to action closes inside one governed environment

Consolidation alone will give a department better reports, and where better reports are the goal, stopping there is enough. The test that separates an intelligence platform from a reporting environment is whether the loop from insight to action to recorded outcome closes inside one governed environment, and consolidation for reporting alone does not pass it.

Take a finding that both environments produce equally well, such as a panel firm whose fees on an open matter have passed the budget agreed when the matter opened. In a reporting environment someone reads the finding in a report and then acts somewhere else: in an email to the relationship partner, in an adjustment keyed into the e-billing platform, or in a note to the matter owner. The action happens outside the environment that produced the finding, so nothing connects the two. When the next report shows the matter back inside its budget, the department cannot say what produced the change, and the analyst who made the call has the only account of it.

In an intelligence platform the same finding arrives with the means to act on it. The person takes the action in the environment that surfaced the finding, and the platform records who decided what, on which information, and under which rule. The platform then writes the result, an approved invoice or a reassigned matter, to the system that owns that record. The department ends the quarter holding the finding, the decision, the reasoning, and the outcome together, which is the material that any later analysis of the firm or the matter type depends on.

Closing the loop says nothing about which systems a department keeps. Connect to a system of record already in place where it holds data the department trusts and somebody maintains it, and that system remains the source of truth for its data. Supply system of record capabilities where no purchased system owns the entity at all, which is the usual position for the request, the disposition, the obligation, and the action record. Most departments end up doing both, and that mixture is what a working platform looks like.

Business intelligence keeps its place inside the platform. Deterministic computation, such as a budget variance or a realization rate, has to produce the same answer every time it is asked, and that work is the platform's deterministic dimension. The fourth article in this series develops the point.

## The platform knows who may decide and who may approve

Bringing a decision to the right person requires the platform to know who that person is. Structure, roles, responsibilities, and staff assignments are information like any other, and modeling them is what allows a finding to reach the person who holds the authority to act on it.

The specifics are ordinary, and the department already holds every one of them somewhere. It knows who the responsible attorney on a matter is and who the business client is. It knows who may approve an invoice above a threshold or a settlement within an authority limit, which firm is engaged and which of its timekeepers may bill, and who covers a matter while the responsible attorney is on leave. Most departments keep that information in a policy document, a delegation-of-authority matrix, and the memory of an assistant, none of which the platform can read.

Assignment and delegation are themselves governed actions, with their own rules, validations, and notifications. Reassigning a matter is one defined transaction that changes the record, notifies the outgoing and the incoming owner, and checks that the person submitting it holds the authority to do so. Treating it as one transaction is what makes the change reportable a year later. The same change keyed in as a set of field edits, in whichever screen is nearest, alters the data and leaves nothing to report on.

Permissions, ethical walls, and privilege controls travel with the information rather than sitting on the application that displays it, and they apply in the same way to a person and to an AI agent acting for that person. We treat that parity as a design principle. An agent that can read a matter its user could not read is a breach whichever component performed the read, and an agent that can submit an action its user could not submit is the same breach one step further on.

Two families of control do the work, and a legal technology leader will recognize both. Roles granted to groups, usually supplied by the identity provider the company already runs, determine what a person may open. Labels that travel with each unit of information, wherever it is derived or copied, determine what may be done with it once opened. The platform keeps permission to act separate from permission to see, which matters in a department where a paralegal may need to read every invoice on a matter and approve none of them.

## Process determines what the department can record

With the decisions identified and the organization modeled, the platform can be described in its three components. The industry writes them as process, people, and technology, and this article keeps that order, which is the field's convention and implies no ranking. Process comes first here because it determines what there is to record.

Process is how work enters the department, how it is triaged and assigned, how it moves, how it closes, and what gets recorded on the way. In platform terms a process is a sequence of decisions and governed actions, each carrying its own rules, validations, approvals, notifications, and record. Where intake is undefined, the department cannot report request volumes or turnaround times, because no record of the request was ever created.

Intake triage shows the shape. A request arrives by email or through a form; the platform records it, classifies the matter type, and assigns it according to role and coverage; the assignment is a governed action that notifies the assignee and the requester. Every department already runs some version of that sequence. What changes is that request volume and turnaround time become reportable, because every step left a record behind it.

Gartner expects intake to be where AI lands first in legal departments. The forecast appeared in a press release issued in May 2026 under Weston Wicks, read here through reprints in SMBtech on May 27, 2026 and in Lawyers Weekly Australia on June 9, 2026. The firm predicts that by 2029, 60% of legal departments will run intake systems that use AI to capture every request and answer half of them without a person involved. The forecast rests on the same precondition as the manual version, because a system cannot classify or answer a request that the department has never defined.

## People own the data, the process, and the outcomes

A process that nobody owns decays into a description of what used to happen. The people component is a question of roles, ownership, and skills: who owns the data, who owns the process, and who is accountable for the outcomes the process produces. The legal team, legal operations, IT, finance, and outside counsel all work inside the platform, and each needs a defined relationship to it.

Ownership often falls to someone who is already doing another job. The Thomson Reuters Institute's 2025 Legal Department Operations Index, published in September 2025, drew 128 US responses collected in July 2025. Among them, 45% of respondents were general counsel who also run legal operations, and 59% said they aim to improve collaboration between legal and business units. The first figure describes who will be asked to own the platform in a large share of departments. The second describes what those respondents said they want from the work.

Role design has not kept pace with the tools. Deloitte UK's The AI Imperative, published on July 9, 2026 from a survey of 121 senior legal leaders conducted in April and May 2026, found that 84% of respondents had not redesigned roles around AI. The survey does not ask whether departments have assigned process ownership, so it supports no claim about how many of them have. What it does show is that most of these departments have put new tools in front of people whose responsibilities were written before the tools existed.

The roles a platform needs are appearing anyway, ahead of the titles. Law360 Pulse reported on June 8, 2026, in an article by Anna Scott Farrell, that in-house legal engineer roles are emerging inside legal operations teams while titles, pay, and headcount approval lag the work being done. Deloitte's US Chief Legal Officer Program describes the same direction in its 2026 Predictions for Chief Legal Officers, a document that carries a 2026 copyright and no publication date. It expects roles not traditionally found in corporate legal departments, among them project managers, data scientists, and technology specialists, to become more common, and it says that collaboration between the chief legal officer and the CIO becomes essential.

Ownership of the platform belongs inside the department, a case that [Legal Ops Is Not IT for Lawyers](/why-spaarke/legal-ops-is-not-it-for-lawyers) makes at length. The data, the process, and the outcomes each need a named owner in legal, with IT accountable for the environment they run in and finance accountable for the numbers that cross into its ledger. In our experience the ownership question is the one most often left open at the start of a build, and a build that starts before the department settles it tends to stall at the first action that needs a named approver.

## Technology is the part of the platform a department can point to

An owner needs something to own. Technology is the third component and the one a department can point to: the systems of record that hold its data, the layer that connects them, and the surfaces its people work in. It is where legal operations intelligence acquires a budget line, a login screen, and a renewal date, and it is the part of the platform that a provider can supply.

The three parts do different work and fail in different ways. The systems of record hold what the department has already committed to storing. The connecting layer decides whether a matter can be seen with its invoices, its documents, and its communications at once. The surfaces decide whether any of that reaches the person with the decision in front of them. A queue shows the exceptions worth looking at, a matter page answers the questions a legal professional puts to a matter, and an operating view gives the general counsel and the CFO the same numbers to read. [The UX That Legal IQ Requires](/why-spaarke/the-ux-that-legal-iq-requires) develops the third of these, which departments underestimate more often than the other two.

Three further subjects sit inside the technology component, and this article places them without developing them. The ontology is the foundation the component rests on. Business intelligence is the platform's deterministic dimension. Knowledge management supplies the context that AI needs and that no system of record captures. Each carries its own article in this series.

## Software enforces a rule only after someone has decided it

The technology carries out the process definitions and the ownership described above, and it cannot stand in for either. Software can enforce an approval rule only after someone has decided who approves what, route an exception only after someone has defined what counts as an exception, and report a turnaround time only after someone has decided when the clock starts. Departments that buy first and define later pay for the definitions anyway, in configuration work and change requests, at the point where the cost is hardest to see.

What technology supplies is scale, and consolidation, governed action, and the decision record are all department-scale work. One analyst can reconcile one matter's invoices, budget, and engagement terms by hand in an afternoon. Nobody does that for 200 matters every month, which is why those three remain technology problems in a department that has defined its processes well.

Where the department already works decides where the platform has to meet it. Legal professionals spend their days in email, in documents, and in Teams, and the money side of the work happens in the e-billing platform. A platform that asks people to leave those places in order to record a decision will capture fewer decisions than one that reaches into them, whatever its data model looks like.

Harbor, which advises legal departments on operations and technology and has an interest in the answer, reached the same conclusion when it released its 2026 Legal Department Maturity Index survey on May 11, 2026. Kevin Clem's reading of the result was that the technology is in place and the operating model has not caught up with it, which matches what Axiom's respondents said about measurement.

The provider's part in this is narrow enough to state plainly. Spaarke provides the technology that instantiates a department's platform in either mode, with system of record capabilities where the department lacks them and an ontology architecture over the systems it keeps. What no provider supplies is the decision inventory, the process definitions, and the assignment of ownership that make any of the technology worth installing.

## Define the platform by writing each decision as one sentence

Writing those definitions down is the first piece of platform work a department can do without buying anything. Start with what the general counsel, the CFO, and the business unit head need to know and decide, and work back to the decisions the department makes on their behalf. Then write each decision as a single sentence that names the user, the working surface, the decision itself, the inputs it needs, and the action it produces.

The sentences are duller than they sound, which is what makes them useful. A first attempt usually produces something close to this: the head of litigation reviews matters approaching their budget and decides whether to raise the budget, change the staffing, or move the matter, using current spend, the firm's forecast, and the exposure estimate, and the outcome updates the matter record and notifies the firm. Everything the platform owes that decision is now visible in one line: three inputs from at least two systems, a surface that shows matters against budget, two governed actions, and a notification.

Three questions follow each sentence. What is the ultimate decision it serves, meaning the one a business leader would recognize as theirs? What intermediate decisions have to be made before it, and by whom? And which of those decisions does a named user own, as distinct from being consulted on? Departments find at this stage that some of their decisions have no owner and that others have three, and that finding is worth more than the sentences that produced it.

A dozen sentences of this kind do more than list requirements. They name the measures the department will need and the entities and relationships the platform has to hold, both of which later articles in this series take up. They also expose the data each process has to capture, which is often where a department learns that its intake form has been collecting the wrong five fields for four years.

Put the test to your own department this way. Take the ten decisions your general counsel would name as the ones that matter most, and for each ask who makes it, where they make it, what they open in order to make it, and where the outcome is written. The decisions you cannot answer in that form are the ones to write down first, because they are the decisions the department is making somewhere nobody can see.

## An invoice exception, run twice, shows what the platform changes

The example that follows is illustrative rather than a case study, and it uses the department described earlier: about 200 open matters, a panel of firms, an e-billing platform, a matter system, and a document management system. Written in the form above, the decision reads as follows. A legal operations analyst reviews an invoice exception queue and decides whether to approve, reduce, or return an invoice, using the engagement terms, the matter budget, and the firm's billing history, and the outcome updates the invoice record in the e-billing system.

### Today the decision is made in an email

The e-billing platform flags a line on a litigation invoice because the partner rate exceeds the cap in the outside counsel guidelines. The analyst opens the matter in the matter system to see the budget and how much of it the firm has consumed. She opens the engagement letter in the document management system, because the guidelines allow an uplift for one named partner and she has to check whether this is that partner. She opens a spreadsheet she maintains herself, which holds what this firm has billed on similar matters over two years. Four applications, one of them her own creation, are open before she has applied any judgment.

The judgment takes a few minutes. She writes to the billing partner, explains that the rate exceeds the cap and that the engagement letter's exception covers a different timekeeper, and proposes a reduction. The firm agrees. She keys the reduction into the e-billing platform, which records an adjusted amount and an adjustment code. It records none of the reasoning behind them: the budget position that made her press the point, the clause she checked, the firm's pattern on comparable matters, and the exchange that settled it. All of that sits in her mailbox. When a different analyst meets the same firm and the same clause next quarter, the work starts again at the guidelines.

### On the platform the decision is recorded against the invoice, the matter, and the firm

The same exception arrives in a queue with its context attached: the engagement terms that govern the rate, including the named-partner exception; the matter's budget position as of that morning; and what this firm has billed on matters of the same type. The analyst approves, reduces, or returns the invoice in the queue itself, and the reduction she enters is a defined action with a reason code and a note rather than an edit to a field.

The platform then records how the analyst reached the decision. The rule that flagged the line and the threshold it applied, the information the analyst was shown, her decision and her reasoning, and the date are written against three entities at once: the invoice, the matter, and the firm. The approved amount is written back to the e-billing platform, which remains the system of record for invoices and for the payment that follows from them.

The visible change is that the analyst opens one surface instead of four, and that saving is real but modest, since the judgment took longer than the searching. The change worth building for is that a judgment which used to exist only in a mailbox has become a record the department owns, attached to the three things it concerns.

Four quarters of those records answer questions that no department commissions a report for today: how often this firm claims the rate exception and how often the claim is upheld, what happens to its invoices after review compared with the rest of the panel, and whether it bills differently on matters that are running over budget. The platform captured the answers as analysts made each decision, and together they are the firm's report card at the next panel review (Exhibit 2).

## Build one decision at a time, and add AI last

One decision is also the right size for the first build. A use case, in the sense that matters here, is a time-bound effort by a dedicated team to support one decision. Integrating a source system does not qualify, and neither does building a dashboard, because neither ends with someone deciding something differently from the way they decided it before.

The order of work inside that effort follows the argument of this article. Model the business reality in the department's own language, using the terms its lawyers and analysts already use, rather than mirroring the tables of the systems the data comes from. Connect to the systems already in place where they serve the department well, and supply system of record capabilities where it has none. Define the governed actions, including the write to the system of record, because an action that stops short of completing the transaction leaves the loop open. Build the surfaces the decision is made in. Add AI last.

Validation has a cheap form that departments underuse. Collect the questions a general counsel or a practice leader puts to the team over and over, the ones that currently set off a two-day scramble. Then put those same questions to the new surface, to a recent joiner, and to the AI the department will eventually ask to answer them. How long each takes to produce a defensible answer is a design signal, and a question that takes longer on the platform than it took by telephone points at something the model has left out.

Then expand. The second decision costs less than the first, because the entities, the connections, and the governance built for one are largely what the next one needs, and the third costs less again. Intake to matter to spend is the usual spine, since each stage feeds the next and what the department captures at intake determines what it can answer about spend. A first build needs no program behind it. A working session of days, on real data, with the people who own the decision in the room, produces a model worth arguing about, which is more than most requirements documents achieve.

The modeling step is what analysts outside legal mean when they talk about building an ontology, and the people who have done it are candid about the cost. Barry Briggs, an analyst at Directions on Microsoft and formerly the chief technology officer of Microsoft's own IT organization, wrote on December 2, 2025 that "Creating an ontology, in Fabric or elsewhere, is a major effort and requires on-going maintenance, so there's no getting out of the hard work." Juan Sequeda's counsel for work of that kind, written on June 10, 2026, is to "start small and think big", which in a legal department means one decision modeled properly in preference to a department-wide data program that reaches nobody's queue. The stages in [From Reactive to Predictive](/why-spaarke/loi-maturity-model) describe where a department stands before and after such a build, and this argument proposes no new staircase.

## Every decision becomes data the department can learn from

The record left behind by the invoice example is the general case. Every governed action writes down what was decided, by whom, when, on which information, and under which rule, and those five facts are what turn a decision into data. Two things follow from having them. The department holds a traceable history for audit, for a regulator, and for the handoff when the person who made the decision moves on. It also holds material to learn from, because the department can compare outcomes with the decisions that produced them.

Most departments lose that material as a matter of routine, and no survey in our library measures how much of it. A decision made in an email lasts as long as the mailbox and is retrievable by whoever remembers the thread. A decision taken in a meeting survives in the minutes if someone wrote them. [Institutional Knowledge Is Walking Out the Door](/why-spaarke/institutional-knowledge) sets out what that costs when a lawyer leaves, and the same loss happens more slowly when nobody leaves at all, because the reasoning behind a decision is already unavailable to the next person who faces the same question.

The field has begun to ask for the record. At CLOC Global Institute 2026 in Chicago, which drew nearly 2,400 attendees in May by CLOC's own count, CLOC's Oyango Snell described what had changed: "The AI conversation has matured. Teams are now sharing what has worked, what broke, and how they are governing it." (Legal IT Insider, Toby Weston, May 15, 2026.) The Thomson Reuters Institute's 2026 report on corporate law departments finds that "very few are collecting success metrics around AI's implementation or linking its use to business revenue". Answering the measurement question Axiom's respondents could not requires a record of decisions to measure against, and running the work produces that record.

## AI works through the same actions and permissions as people

AI comes last in the build order because the earlier steps produce what makes it useful. [The Legal IQ Stack: Data, Memory, Inference](/why-spaarke/the-iq-stack) sets out the arrangement, in which inference reads over the department's data and over its operational memory, meaning the accumulated record of what the department has done and decided. A model with access to neither answers from general knowledge, which is available to the other side as well.

Some of the work should never reach a model at all. Calculations, rule evaluation, and routing on defined criteria have to return the same answer every time they run, and [Legal AI Is Not Deterministic](/why-spaarke/probabilistic-vs-deterministic) explains why a generative model does not. The budget variance, the rate cap test, and the assignment rule belong on the deterministic side of the platform, and keeping them there is what makes the results of the probabilistic side checkable.

AI earns its place on the work that has resisted rules: classifying an inbound request, extracting obligations and dates from an agreement, summarizing a matter for the person picking it up, drafting a first response, and increasingly carrying out steps of a process under supervision. Each of those is a place where a person currently reads something and types something, and where the reading is routine and the typing is not the decision.

An agent doing any of that work submits the same governed action a person would submit, with the same validations and approval rules, under the permissions described above, and its submission appears in the decision record with the agent named. The department widens what an agent may do on its own as the record shows where it has been reliable, starting from a pattern in which the agent proposes and a person confirms. That staging is the department's own operating choice, and the phrase we use for it is AI-directed, human-controlled.

The model layer itself is arriving through several doors at once. Anthropic launched Claude for Legal on May 12, 2026 with "20+ new MCP connectors" and 12 plugins. Thomson Reuters announced a connection between CoCounsel Legal and Claude the same day and presented it as live. iManage released its MCP Server two days later and described it as now available. Microsoft published a Message Center notice on June 12, 2026, "Legal agent available in Frontier worldwide", making a legal agent for Microsoft 365 Copilot available to tenants enrolled in its Frontier program. Legal IT Insider, reporting from CLOC Global Institute 2026 on May 15, 2026, observed that "The race to become the operating system for legal departments is underway."

A department should therefore expect to change its model, its assistant, or its connector during the life of its platform, and probably more than once. What survives each change is the part the department built. The entity model, the governed actions, and the decision record belong to no provider, and they are what the department points the next model at. In a press release of May 11, 2026, Gartner predicted that by 2027 organizations that prioritize semantics in their AI-ready data will raise agentic AI accuracy by up to 80% and cut costs by up to 60%, and it advised establishing a context layer as a core component of the data and analytics infrastructure. Gartner has measured nothing here either, and its advice still lands on the same durable part, the context layer the department owns.

## An ontology of the department's entities and actions is the foundation

Each part of this argument rests on the same underlying model, one that holds the department's entities and the actions it may take on them. The name for that model is an ontology, and it is the foundation of a legal operations intelligence platform.

The ontology does two jobs. The first is insight across the systems of record, in more than one dimension at a time. A matter in the model is the matter together with its documents, its invoices, its communications, its obligations, and the people responsible for each, because the model carries the relationships among those records and no single system that stores them can. At this level the model answers the limit set out earlier, that each system sees one dimension of a matter.

The second job reaches past insight to the running of the platform. The model carries the actions the department may take on each entity, the inquiries it can put to the model, the policies that govern those actions, the typed outputs that actions and inquiries produce, and the governance around all of it: who may act, under which rule, and what the action leaves behind. Every governed action, decision record, and permission described in this article is defined there rather than in the code of whichever application happens to display it.

A department cannot buy a model of that kind ready-made, and it cannot derive one from the schemas of the systems it already runs. Building the model takes a close understanding of how the department works and of who does what. That dependency is why process and ownership come before technology, and why the build starts at the decision and works back to the data it needs. The third article in this series develops the ontology: what it contains, how it binds to the systems where the data sits, and what it takes to keep it current.

A department that accepts the argument has one decision in front of it, smaller than any platform strategy, and that is which operational decision to model first. The candidates are the places where the department's judgment is exercised most often and recorded least well, and the invoice exception used above is only one of them, alongside matter intake, outside counsel selection, budget approval, and settlement authority. Postponing that choice costs nothing that shows up in a budget line, and the decisions keep being made in email, each defensible on the day it was taken and out of reach of the person who meets the same question next quarter. The bill arrives when the general counsel asks what the department has learned from two years of AI, and the only place that learning lives is with the people who happen to still be there.
