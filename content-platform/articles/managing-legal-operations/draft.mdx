In the Thomson Reuters Institute's 2025 Legal Department Operations Index, drawn from a July 2025 survey of 128 US respondents, 82% reported at least one dedicated legal operations role. The report found that "Legal operations work is expanding beyond its origins as primarily a cost-control function to include a focus on systems, processes, and technology."

The CLOC and ACC frameworks set out that scope, and the reader of this piece runs it. Three things around the function are now changing at once. The legal department faces more demand on flat resources, AI in daily work, and a changed view of what it needs in people and roles. The function's place in the business has moved. Legal and risk are treated as critical business considerations, the general counsel contributes to strategy and to decisions, and the business takes a direct part in its own legal risk. What companies send to outside counsel, and what they expect back, is changing as well. In its 2026 Legal Department Maturity Index, released on May 11, 2026, the consultancy Harbor found AI implemented or under exploration at 98% of departments and concluded that "Technology is in place. The operating model has not kept up."

Managing legal operations effectively now means directing the changes under way in the legal department, in the legal function's place in the business, and in the outside counsel relationship. Each of those changes reaches legal operations as a decision about how legal work gets done, and every such decision depends on information the department can act on. [Legal Ops Is Not IT for Lawyers](/why-spaarke/legal-ops-is-not-it-for-lawyers) separated administrative legal operations from strategic legal operations, and what is widening now is the strategic remit itself.

Six drivers push those changes in the same direction. The largest is a new view of the resource requirements of legal and legal operations, driven by AI. Demand is outrunning budget and headcount, and legal, and risk in particular, is recognized as a critical consideration of the business. The general counsel contributes to business strategy, direction, and decision making. New delivery options arrive from alternative providers, AI-native firms, and the frontier model providers, and the business can now act on its own legal risk through the front door and self-service.

## Six drivers push the remit in the same direction

Each of the three changes has causes that can be named, and six drivers account for most of the movement.

A new view of what a legal department needs in people and roles is the largest of the six, and AI is what produced it. ACC published the key findings of its 2026 Chief Legal Officers Survey in January 2026, drawn from 1,049 participants in 43 countries. In it, 63% of chief legal officers said they expect headcount to remain stable, a finding the association published under the heading "AI Adoption Targets Role Evolution Over Headcount Reduction".

The same survey describes what moves inside that stable headcount. Technology and AI proficiency is the main area that 47% say their CEO wants them to develop, and the priority placed on technological fluency for department lawyers rose 17 points to 34% in a single year. These are statements of expectation rather than records of hiring, so they show what chief legal officers plan and not what their departments did. What they plan is to hold the size of the department and change the work its people do. ACC's own summary of the position is that chief legal officers are mandated to lead their departments' adoption of AI while chronic budget and resource constraints hold them back.

The consultancy evidence points the same way. Harbor's finding that AI is implemented or under exploration at 98% of departments sits beside its conclusion that the operating model has not kept up. Kevin Clem of Harbor names talent alongside technology, workflows, and governance as the things a department now has to align. Hiring has begun to show it. Law360 Pulse reported on June 8, 2026 that legal engineers are appearing inside legal operations teams, which is what a change in roles rather than a change in headcount looks like on an organization chart.

Demand is the second driver, and it is outrunning the budget and the headcount that would absorb it. CLOC's 2026 State of the Industry Report, released on March 2, 2026, draws on the 2025 Harbor Law Department Survey of 135 law departments with a median revenue of $13 billion. It reports workload growth driven by regulatory compliance at 63% of departments and by cybersecurity at 58%. Expectations for resources moved the other way over the same year: 37% expect outside counsel spend to increase, against 58% a year earlier, and 32% expect attorney headcount to increase, against 42%.

Oyango Snell, CLOC's President and CEO, put the two findings together: "Demand is accelerating in areas like regulatory compliance and cybersecurity, yet budget and staffing growth are not keeping pace." Closing the distance between those two lines is what a legal operations director is asked to do, and in most departments adding people is no longer among the available methods.

The third driver is the standing of legal, and of risk in particular, inside the business. The Thomson Reuters Institute's 2026 State of the Corporate Law Department, published on March 24, 2026, draws on more than 2,300 interviews with corporate general counsel. It found that 86% of general counsel see legal as a significant contributor to business objectives, while 17% of the C-suite respondents agreed. The two figures come from different populations, and the C-suite sample size is not disclosed, so the pair measures a difference in perception and not a difference in contribution. The gap still matters, because the executives who do not see the contribution are the ones who approve the department's budget and its headcount.

The fourth driver is the general counsel's own position. In the ACC survey, a record 84% of chief legal officers report directly to the CEO, 79% say they "almost always" attend board meetings, and 74% provide proactive strategic counsel rather than answering questions as they arrive. A general counsel who sits in those rooms is committed to work that is not legal advice in the narrow sense. The department has to run without them for more of the week than it used to.

The fifth driver is the number of places a department can now send a piece of work. Anthropic released Claude for the legal industry on May 12, 2026, describing it as "20+ new MCP connectors" (Model Context Protocol) and "12 new plugins tailored to specific legal work and practice areas". Its first legal plugins had arrived on January 30, 2026 (LawSites, February 3, 2026). AI-native law firms and alternative legal services providers (ALSPs) offer fixed-price work on routine agreements, a development the International Bar Association covered on December 11, 2025 and Artificial Lawyer examined on March 31, 2026.

The alternative provider market was $28.5 billion as of fiscal 2023, with 18% compound annual growth, according to a report published on January 28, 2025 by the Thomson Reuters Institute with Georgetown Law and Oxford Saïd. Those numbers describe supply. What a department does with the supply is a sourcing decision, and it is a different question from whether the options exist.

The sixth driver is the business's new ability to act on its own legal risk, through a legal front door that takes in requests and through self-service for routine agreements. No neutral adoption statistic exists for front doors, intake portals, or non-disclosure agreement self-service in 2025 or 2026. Adoption is therefore visible and unmeasured, and no number belongs in a sentence about it. What can be said is that departments are using these routes, which changes who performs part of the legal work.

## The frameworks describe the function, and practice has moved past their texts

Taken together, the six drivers move the boundary of the function: more of the work arrives somewhere other than a lawyer's desk, and someone has to decide where. The profession's published frameworks were written before that movement began.

The CLOC Core 12, the ACC Legal Operations Maturity Model 2.0, and the LegalOps.com Legal Operations Management reference model are the shared vocabulary of the field, and they remain useful for exactly that. They also record the practice as it stood before most of the changes above. The Core 12 was last substantively revised on April 7, 2020. The ACC model dates from 2020 and sets 14 functions at three stages of maturity. The LegalOps.com model of 15 practice areas was unveiled at the inaugural Running Legal Like a Business conference in October 2023, as LawVision recorded on October 30, 2023. Its per-area content is available to members, and nothing in this article rests on it.

Four gaps between the CLOC and ACC texts and current practice measure how far the function has moved. First, neither text treats request intake or the legal front door as a function of legal operations, and ACC alone names intake and triage, only as maturity markers inside two other functions. Second, the term self-service appears in neither text. The concept appears once in the ACC model, as an Advanced-stage marker under Contract Management, which describes playbooks that "allow extensive contract creation/execution with no legal department involvement". A CLOC-published blog post of March 10, 2020 described "on-demand, self-service legal solutions" for internal customers, "driven by playbooks, AI and legal bots". That language did not carry into the Core 12 text revised on April 7, 2020.

Third, both texts define knowledge management without reference to AI. Fourth, AI appears once in the Core 12 function texts and four times in the ACC model, always at the Advanced stage and never under knowledge management. The four gaps run in one direction. The texts describe a department that receives legal work and performs it, and they say little about a department that decides where each request should go.

The frameworks are doing what a framework does. Each records the state of a practice at the time it was written, so the four gaps measure how quickly the practice moved after 2020 rather than any defect in the texts.

LegalOps.com reaches the same ground from the outcome rather than from the function list. On its account, legal operations management is the oversight of how legal services are delivered across a department. That oversight exists so that delivery meets the quality, cost, and risk thresholds the enterprise requires, with competitive advantage for the business as the objective. The two approaches describe the same work, one by listing functions and one by naming the outcome, and a department can use either without choosing between them. [From Reactive to Predictive](/why-spaarke/loi-maturity-model) maps the frameworks against the maturity question in detail, and this article does not walk them again.

## The business needs more legal judgment at more decision points

What the business asks of the function has changed further than the function's own scope, and it has changed in two directions at the same time. The business wants legal judgment earlier and at more points. ACC found that "legal expertise is now integrated into business planning during the inception of projects rather than at the point of crisis". The breadth of what chief legal officers oversee has grown with that involvement. ACC reports 64% overseeing compliance and 62% overseeing the corporate secretary function, with remits that extend into ethics, privacy, and cybersecurity.

When internal capacity runs out, departments buy more of it. Under a heading that calls external resources a pressure valve, ACC reports outside counsel use for regulatory surges at 48% and consultants at 27%. The workload growth that CLOC measures comes from the same risk domains, which is why the second driver and the third are one story told from two ends.

The business also does not yet see the contribution it receives. Thomson Reuters calls the distance between the 86% of general counsel and the 17% of C-suite respondents a visibility gap. Among those C-suite respondents, 42% said that legal contributes "a little" or "not at all". The report's advice to general counsel is to "institute metrics for success that translate to the rest of the business". It also tells them to position efficiency metrics "in the context of larger business goals, rather than in the language of the legal department". Both instructions describe legal operations work: choosing the metrics, producing them on a schedule, and stating them in the business's own terms.

Supplying that evidence is harder than the instruction makes it sound, because the underlying data is rarely in a state to support it. EY Law's 2025 General Counsel Study, published on April 9, 2025, surveyed 1,000 general counsel and chief legal officers at companies with revenue above $1 billion. In it, 52% reported disorganized data and 44% reported disconnected legal and business platforms. A department in that position can usually answer a question about one matter and cannot answer the same question about two hundred of them. Harbor's index gives the shape of what closes the gap: technology is in place at almost every department, and the next phase of maturity depends on aligning technology, workflows, talent, and governance into a single operating model.

The business's expectation about how legal addresses its issues has moved with everything else. It expects legal requirements to be handled inside its own processes and at the point where it decides, where legal was historically a separate step handled directly by the department. Meeting that expectation or declining it is itself a design decision, and declining it tends to send the work around the department rather than through it.

Effective management of the function begins here, with a sizing question. The department has to decide how much legal capability and decision support the business needs, and at which of its decision points. It then has to decide how each part is delivered, whether by an in-house lawyer, by the business itself under guardrails, by an automated or AI-assisted workflow, or by an outside provider. The answer differs by company and by risk domain. The question belongs to legal operations because no one else in the department is positioned to ask it across every practice group at once. [Breaking the Silo Between Legal, Finance, and the Business](/why-spaarke/breaking-the-silo) takes up the reporting side of the same problem.

## The business is becoming a participant in its own legal risk

One of those four delivery channels is newer than the others, and it changes the relationship more than the rest. The business is moving from being a recipient of legal service to being a participant in it. The visible forms are a legal front door that takes every request through one route, self-service for non-disclosure agreements, and self-service for other steps in the contract lifecycle that used to wait for a lawyer.

Legal operations serves two constituencies, the legal team inside the department and the business outside it, and the second relationship is the one now changing. ACC's finding that legal expertise enters business planning at the inception of projects is the condition that makes participation workable. A business unit that has had legal involvement early enough to understand its risk is in a position to handle part of that risk itself. Harbor's conclusion that the operating model has not kept up with the technology explains where the design work sits. The guardrails, the thresholds, and the escalation paths around that participation are the department's to build, and building them means deciding in advance which positions the business may accept, at what contract value a request returns to a lawyer, and who approves a change to either.

The markers of adoption are indirect. The ACC maturity model places intake, triage, and playbook-driven contract execution at its intermediate and advanced stages. On the published program for CLOC Global Institute 2026 in Chicago, the sessions on intake and self-service were largely vendor sessions. Checkbox AI presented on the legal front door and Legora on business self-service, both on Wednesday, May 13. One in-house workshop, run by HPE on Tuesday, May 12, covered building a request intake tool with Power Apps and SharePoint.

Weston Wicks of Gartner has predicted that by 2029 about half of contract reviews will be delegated to self-service systems that escalate one review in 10 for human attention. He also predicts that 60% of legal departments will run intake systems that use AI to capture every request and answer half of them without a person. Both figures are forecasts rather than measurements, and no survey base was given with them, so neither can be checked against an adoption figure that does not exist.

The purpose of the guardrails, the thresholds, the playbooks, the automated and AI-assisted workflows, and the escalation paths is to take legal out of the bottleneck wherever technology can manage the risk. That is what legal operations means by legal as an enabler of the business. A business unit that can complete a standard vendor agreement inside its own process does not wait in the department's queue for it, and the department keeps its review capacity for the agreements that carry the exposure.

The department's role in the work it hands over is to make sure that the business understands what it is agreeing to and what the exposure is if the counterparty does not perform, and to stay available for the cases that exceed the playbook. Working those cases with the business, rather than taking them back, is the partnership the arrangement depends on. A self-service path that records which position the business took, and why, leaves the department able to answer for the agreement afterwards, which is how the handover keeps the risk managed rather than merely moved. The decision about what the business may complete on its own therefore sits with legal operations rather than with the business unit that wants the speed.

Deciding which answers a system may give without a lawyer is the difficult part of that design, because a generative model returns a different answer to the same question on a second run. [Legal AI Is Not Deterministic](/why-spaarke/probabilistic-vs-deterministic) sets out why that behavior matters for any workflow expected to produce the same result twice. The practical consequence is that self-service works first on the agreements where a playbook already fixes the acceptable positions.

Three decisions and one habit follow from that. The department decides which work the business may complete on its own, within which risk thresholds, and with what escalation path when a request crosses one of them. The habit is to treat the front door as the department's best source of demand data. A request that arrives through a queue is counted, categorized, and timed, while a request that arrives as an email to the lawyer who knows the business unit is none of those things.

## A strategic general counsel depends on a well-run function

The same evidence problem appears one level up, in what the general counsel is now expected to do and to prove. ACC describes the move from head lawyer to business executive as having reached a structural peak, and the reporting and board figures above put the general counsel in the rooms where the business decides. Two further findings say what the position costs. The CEO's main development ask of chief legal officers is technology and AI proficiency, as the first driver showed. Budget and resource constraints are the top barrier, named by 35% of them. A general counsel is therefore asked to lead the department's use of technology, from a seat that keeps them in board meetings and strategy discussions, with the resources the department already had.

Legal operations is how that arithmetic resolves. It runs the department so that the general counsel's time goes to the business rather than to the department's own administration, and it produces the evidence, in business terms, that the contribution is real. The second half is the harder one, because the visibility gap does not close through effort. Thomson Reuters records technology rising as a strategic priority for general counsel from 14% to 28% over a year, with 86% of those technology mentions referring to AI. The same report finds that very few departments are collecting success metrics around AI's implementation or linking its use to business revenue.

The direction of the general counsel's own attention has moved as well. In the Thomson Reuters Institute's 2025 Legal Department Operations Index, 47% of general counsel said they are more focused on improving legal service than on reducing its cost. Only 7% put cost reduction first, and a further group weighs the two equally. A function managed for cost alone is being managed against its principal's stated priority. The same index describes the profession as growing beyond its cost-control origin, and most department budgets are still written as though it had not.

Jeff Franke set out four paradigms for the general counsel in an ABA Journal column of July 24, 2023: the risk manager, the business partner, the strategic and trusted adviser, and the creator of competitive advantage. Of the last he said that "the ultimate paradigm is the general counsel who is helping to create a competitive advantage for the business". He paired each paradigm with a legal operations role, running from tactical, cost-focused work at one end to supporting and driving strategy at the other. The column predates most of the changes described here, and the pairing has held, because a general counsel cannot occupy a paradigm that the operations function does not support.

Legal operations sits inside the department, and its mandate runs across every practice group, which is what allows it to ask the sizing question of the whole function rather than of one team at a time. Reporting lines differ too much between companies to be worth prescribing.

## Companies are changing what they send out and what they expect back

The sizing question does not stop at the edge of the department. Once a department has decided how much legal capability the business needs and where each part of it should come from, the largest of the external choices is what still goes to a law firm.

Most of the evidence says that less of it does. Harbor's 2025 Law Department Survey, released on December 8, 2025, drew on the same 135 departments as the CLOC report. It found 76% of departments using alternative fee arrangements, against 70% a year earlier, and 61% that had completed or were implementing convergence on a smaller set of firms, against 50%. Another 65% reported deliberate efforts to keep work in-house. These are department-reported practices rather than audited spend, so they show where departments are pushing and not yet how far the money has moved.

Departments say the same about their plans. ACC and Everlaw surveyed 657 in-house professionals in 30 countries for a report released on October 14, 2025, with Everlaw as a vendor co-sponsor. In it, 64% expected to rely less on outside counsel, and the work they named for insourcing was drafting (78%), contract management (71%), and research (62%). Axiom's survey of 516 general counsel, released on February 17, 2026, found more than 80% planning to move law firm work to internal teams or alternative providers within two years. Axiom is an alternative provider and an interested party in that answer, which is a reason to read the direction of its finding rather than its size.

What departments expect from their firms has changed alongside what they send. In the same ACC and Everlaw survey, 59% "report no GenAI savings from their law firms yet" and 24% are satisfied with their firms' adoption. Few departments have asked for the savings directly. The 18th annual Law Department Operations Survey, run by the Blickstein Group with FTI Consulting among 68 mostly North American companies, was reported by Legal.io on December 19, 2025. Fewer than one in five of those departments had pressed their firms to show what their use of AI had saved. The dissatisfaction is therefore largely unspoken, and a firm that has never been asked the question has nothing to answer.

Three findings cut against that direction of travel, and a department that plans on the first set alone will misread its own next three years. The Thomson Reuters Institute's 2025 Legal Department Operations Index observes that "For most of the past decade, regardless of whether in-house legal departments or outside law firms are being surveyed, AFAs are used in about 20% of legal matters consistently." That is a long-run observation rather than a single year's data point, and it describes a practice announced more often than it has spread. In the 2026 Thomson Reuters report, 36% of general counsel expected to increase outside counsel spend against 20% who planned to decrease it. ACC's pressure valve finding, the 48% turning to outside counsel for regulatory surges, records a department reaching for a firm at the moment its own capacity runs out.

The supply side widens the choice at the same time. The alternative provider market described under the fifth driver, at $28.5 billion as of fiscal 2023, is the established part of it, and Anthropic's legal release under the same driver adds a frontier model provider to the same list of options. The newer part is a set of firms built around AI from the start, and their figures are their own claims. The International Bar Association covered them on December 11, 2025 and Artificial Lawyer on March 31, 2026. NormAI, the parent company of Norm Law, reports total funding above $140 million, and Blackstone invested $50 million in Norm Law in November 2025. Crosby reports a median contract turnaround of 58 minutes. Garfield.Law was approved by the UK Solicitors Regulation Authority on May 6, 2025, and Eudia Counsel operates under Arizona's alternative business structure program. General Legal prices a standard contract review at $500 and a non-disclosure agreement at $250, which is the part of the model a department can compare directly against its own cost per document.

The first driver is what makes the sourcing question live again. A department that plans on stable headcount and on AI-assisted work has to decide once more what it sends out, and last year's panel list is a record of an earlier decision rather than a reason for the next one. The market that results is not zero-sum. In the same year that departments plan to insource drafting, they buy more regulatory surge capacity from the same firms, because the two movements answer different questions.

The outside relationship is managed in three parts. The department makes a deliberate sourcing decision for each type of work, rather than letting history make it by default. It states what it wants from its firms' use of AI in terms a firm can act on, such as the categories of work where efficiency is expected and how the saving is shared. It also holds the data to conduct that conversation, which means knowing what it spent by matter type, what it received, and how long the work took. [The $20B Blind Spot](/why-spaarke/the-20b-blind-spot) describes what a department gives up when it can answer the first of those questions and not the second.

## Knowledge curation is settling on legal operations

Every one of those channels runs on the same material. A playbook tells a business unit which terms it may accept, a precedent tells an AI-assisted workflow what an acceptable clause looks like, and matter history tells a panel firm what the company has agreed to before. AI output is only as good as that material, and departments have not settled who keeps it. Four things are pushing the job toward legal operations, starting with the definitions the profession already has.

Both published frameworks describe knowledge management as a practice for people. CLOC's Core 12 page on the function, modified on May 6, 2025, defines it as the processes and standards that collect, structure, and organize knowledge, with taxonomies, automation, and a culture of sharing in the desired state. The ACC model defines it as capturing, distributing, and using structured and tacit knowledge assets. Neither text mentions AI, as the four gaps above recorded. A department that follows either definition will produce a library organized for a person who knows what to look for, which is a different design from one a retrieval system can work through.

Harbor's finding supplies the second reason. Technology is in place at 98% of departments while the operating model around it, including its workflows and its governance, has not caught up. Source material is part of that operating model. Which playbook is current, who approves a change to it, and how a superseded precedent is retired are operating questions rather than drafting questions, and they land with the function that owns the operating model.

Hiring shows the third. Law360 Pulse reported on June 8, 2026 that departments are creating legal engineer roles, and it quoted Elly Meenan, founder of The Legal Ops Job Board: "From what I see, the legal engineer is already in-house. We just haven't seen the title, salary shift yet." Mary O'Carroll described the difficulty of getting such a position approved: "With these roles that are new and not well understood, it's even more challenging to get that headcount and get it approved." The work these roles do, which is preparing the department's material so that a system can act on it, is being staffed before it has been named.

Practitioners have already put the two disciplines in one room. At CLOC Global Institute 2026, the practitioner-led breakout on knowledge management was run jointly by legal operations and knowledge management staff from JPMorganChase and Guggenheim, including an in-house knowledge management librarian.

AI governance is arriving at the same desk. In the CLOC and Harbor data, 85% of departments have a dedicated resource or a committee overseeing AI. Oyango Snell described how the conversation at the institute had changed, in remarks Legal IT Insider reported on May 15, 2026: "The AI conversation has matured. Teams are now sharing what has worked, what broke, and how they are governing it." Governance and curation are the same job seen from two ends, because a control on what a system may do is worth little while the material the system reads is out of date. [The AI Readiness Gap in Legal Departments](/why-spaarke/the-ai-readiness-gap) sets out what a department needs in place before an AI assistant earns its place in the workflow.

One decision repeats across the department's material: which version is authoritative, who keeps it current, and how it is structured so that a person and a retrieval system can both find it. The cost of leaving that decision unmade is an old problem, and [Institutional Knowledge Is Walking Out the Door](/why-spaarke/institutional-knowledge) describes the form it used to take. What has changed is that an unanswered question about authority now produces a confident answer from a model, where it once produced silence from a filing system.

## Legal operations is where the department decides how legal work gets done

Curation, sourcing, self-service, and the sizing question are separate decisions with one thing in common. Each is a decision about the operating model, and each arrives at the same desk. Harbor puts the next phase of departmental maturity in those terms, in Kevin Clem's words: "The next phase of maturity will be defined by how well organizations align technology, workflows, talent, and governance into a cohesive operating model." Three of those four are what legal operations already does, and the fourth, talent, is the one the first driver moved.

The working scope of the function can be stated in four areas, and each of them looks different now than it did when the frameworks were written. None of the scenes below names a company, and any figure in them is illustrative rather than a finding.

Under technology, the work has moved from buying systems to deciding what the systems are allowed to decide. A property and casualty insurer with a legal team of roughly 200 replaced a shared mailbox and four departmental intake forms with a single request route. The immediate gain was administrative, in that requests stopped arriving twice. The durable gain was that the department could report, for the first time, how many requests each business unit sent, which kinds they were, and how long each kind took to close. Writing the routing rules was the hard part, because a mailbox never required anyone to state which requests need a lawyer.

Knowledge management has moved upstream of the answer. A security software company rewrote its non-disclosure agreement playbook as a set of positions with a stated fallback for each, then pointed both its self-service tool and its AI assistant at that one file. When the company later changed its position on mutual indemnity, it changed it once, and the change carried to every channel that answers the question. A library of past agreements organized by folder does not behave that way, because each copy of the old position stays where it is.

Under resource allocation, the question has become who or what performs each piece of work. A food delivery company operating across several countries sorted its agreement types by the risk each one carried. Standard vendor terms went to a business self-service path inside fixed guardrails, a middle tier went to an AI-assisted draft with a lawyer's review before signature, and the agreements carrying regulatory exposure stayed with the department's lawyers from the first draft. The department measured itself on the share of agreements that never required a lawyer's time, and the guardrails took longer to write than the tooling took to configure.

The fourth area, outside counsel management, is now a sourcing choice with a date on it rather than a relationship maintained by default. An online marketplace grouped a year of completed matters by type and cost, and found that a category of routine regulatory filings consumed a disproportionate share of one firm's billing. It moved that category to an alternative provider on a fixed fee, keeping an escalation path back to the firm for anything contested. The analysis that made the move possible took three months, because the matter records and the invoices had to be reconciled by hand before they agreed with each other.

The four areas describe one system. A request enters the department through the front door and leaves through one of five channels: business self-service inside guardrails, an automated or AI-assisted workflow, an in-house lawyer, outside counsel, or an alternative provider. Legal operations owns the rules that route it, the risk thresholds that decide when a request moves to a different channel, and the data that returns from each one (Exhibit 1).

Read against that picture, the six drivers stop being separate forces. The new view of what the department needs in people and roles changes which channels a department can staff. Demand against flat resources decides how much of the volume has to leave the lawyer's desk. Legal as a critical business consideration and the general counsel's strategic role together decide which requests deserve judgment and which deserve a rule. New delivery options add channels to the right of the diagram, and the business's ability to act on its own risk adds one to the left. Each driver ends at the same routing table.

## Readiness before and after the event is the practical test

A routing table says how the department handles what arrives. It says little about what the department can do when something arrives that nobody routed, and that second question is the one that separates management from administration. Administration keeps the department running from one week to the next: invoices are processed, matters are opened, and reports go out on schedule. Management does that and also keeps the department ready for events that nobody scheduled.

Readiness takes two forms, and a department can be strong in one and weak in the other. The reactive test is whether the department can respond to the subpoena, the budget overrun, the regulator's letter, or the sudden departure using what it already knows. Each of those events arrives with a short clock, and each is answered out of records that either exist in usable form or do not. A department reconstructing three years of a vendor relationship from email while a litigation hold runs is paying, at the worst available moment, for a record-keeping decision it made years earlier.

The proactive test is whether the department sees the spend trend, the capacity gap, or the recurring risk before any of them becomes an event. CLOC states that expectation on its own page describing the function: "A proactive legal ops team can anticipate and plan for challenges before they arrive." The two forms of readiness sit on either side of the same moment, and both draw on information the department already holds (Exhibit 2).

Applied to the changes above, the test produces four questions you can put to your own department. Can you see your demand at the front door, by business unit and request type, for the last 12 months? Can your general counsel answer a board question about legal risk with evidence rather than with an account of how the department reads the situation? Can you show what the department received for its outside counsel spend, by matter type, against what it expected to receive? Can a person or an AI system in your department find the authoritative answer to a recurring question without asking the lawyer who wrote it?

Closing the distance is usually a question of whether anyone has decided which records are authoritative and who keeps them current, because the systems that would hold those records are in most cases already bought and installed. [From Reactive to Predictive](/why-spaarke/loi-maturity-model) places the same movement against the maturity models the field already uses, and the language carries over here: a department that can answer only after the event is operating at the reactive end of its own curve.

## Every requirement depends on information the department can act on

The department has to see four things before it can make any of the decisions above: its demand at the front door, its spend, its capacity, and its recurring risks, with the last of them seen early enough to act on. A department that cannot see those four cannot direct the changes it is now expected to direct. The work will still be processed and the matters will still close, and the general counsel will still make the strategic claim in front of a board that has no evidence for it. The same information is what allows the department to tell the business, with evidence, where the risk sits and where the business may proceed on its own. That evidence is the difference between a guardrail the business trusts and a rule the business routes around.

That information does not assemble itself.
