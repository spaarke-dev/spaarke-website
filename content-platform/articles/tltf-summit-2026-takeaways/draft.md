I spent three days at The LegalTech Fund Summit in Scottsdale, and I left convinced that real change is under way in both the practice and the business of law. These are my own takeaways, a snapshot of my reactions rather than a report on any one session or speaker.

The content was excellent. I attended more than a dozen sessions, and every one was substantive, well prepared, and well presented. For a legal technology provider, the Summit is a strong place to connect, learn, and keep up with the market. It doesn't replace time with customers, but it was well worth the trip.

Seven things stayed with me.

• Judged by attendance, the field is in good health. By my estimate more than 1,000 people attended, and hundreds of legal technology companies were represented, from startups to growth stage. The room leaned toward law firms more than corporate legal departments, although many products serve both.

• TLTF's Pathways initiative framed the program with "The Future of the Business of Law 2040," a well-produced and thought-provoking book handed out to attendees. Whether you see the change it describes as an existential threat or a generational opportunity, it ran through the whole program.

• AI-native offerings remain the main focus, and that now covers technology-enabled legal services as well as software and tools.

• Alternative business structures, and management services organizations in particular, were the hot topic. I sat in on almost every ABS and MSO session, and each one was standing room only.

• Hearing the frontier model companies describe where their legal offerings are heading should lead many teams to reconsider how their technology stack is architected, deployed, managed, and used. We are doing exactly that at Spaarke. I didn't see Microsoft or Google.

• Investment in legal remains historically active, but it is maturing and feels less frantic than in 2025 and early 2026, much like the wider market for AI-driven business software. Many people said deals are taking much longer. Several late-stage private equity holdings should come to market, but many were bought at peak valuations, and the gap between bid and ask is forcing a reset of expectations.

• The long-predicted end of the billable hour came up again and again. Clients and new AI-native law firms are pressing for better answers, and the question is bound up with the MSO discussion.

If you were in Scottsdale, what did you bring home?
