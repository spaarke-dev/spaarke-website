The quarterly report that reaches the general counsel already shows outside counsel spend by firm and by matter type, and the e-billing platform produced it without anyone commissioning a project. The Thomson Reuters Institute records 62% of departments saying they routinely report total spend by law firm and 50% saying they routinely report it by matter type (2025 Legal Department Operations Index, produced with Buying Legal Council; 128 legal department professionals and general counsel in the United States, surveyed in July 2025 and published that September). In the same survey, 60% of departments have e-billing or spend management tools in place.

The questions put to the department have moved past what that report covers. In the 2026 ACC Chief Legal Officers Survey (n=1,049 chief legal officers across 20 industries and 43 countries, January 2026), 84% of CLOs report to the chief executive and 79% almost always attend board meetings. Because the function sits that close to the business, chief executives and boards ask it about service, risk, and value. Some departments have begun to wire those same measures to automated processes, and the vendor releases of May 2026 describe products built for that arrangement. The spend report answers none of those questions on its own.

Business intelligence (BI) is the deterministic dimension of legal operations intelligence, a capability co-equal with AI within the platform, because legal decision making and legal operations require exact, repeatable outputs that a probabilistic system cannot supply. Rising expectations now require it to measure service, risk, and value alongside cost, to answer on demand, and to feed the processes that AI orchestrates. A department that relies on AI for more of its work needs one layer in which the same question always returns the same number, and most departments already run that layer for spend. [Legal AI Is Not Deterministic](/why-spaarke/probabilistic-vs-deterministic) drew the line between computing an answer and predicting one, and BI is the layer of the platform where the computing belongs.

## Spend analytics is mature, and service measures are the next stage

The spend report exists because the department standardized the data behind it. Invoices arrive in a defined format, matter types are coded against an agreed list, and the e-billing platform applies the same calculation to every firm, so the figures can be produced each quarter without an analyst assembling them by hand. That is business intelligence, and in most departments it is established practice. ACC and Everlaw found that 83% of teams track outside counsel spend, in a survey of 284 chief legal officers, general counsel, and legal operations professionals worldwide, published in November 2025. The same survey found that 28% track time to resolution, 12% track outside counsel performance, and 9% track impact on business outcomes.

Thomson Reuters finds the same shape from a different sample. In its 2025 Legal Department Operations Index, service, cycle time, outcomes, and the value of technology were each routinely reported by fewer than one in five departments, against the 62% who routinely report total spend by law firm. The report describes departments as "tracking metrics related nearly exclusively to cost and to a lesser extent, efficiency", and it records service metrics as "captured by less than 20% of respondent legal departments". Those figures count what departments report routinely, so they say nothing about the one-off analysis a department runs when the general counsel asks a particular question. What they do show is which measures departments have standardized to the point where producing them costs nothing.

Stated priorities have already moved ahead of the measures. In the same Thomson Reuters survey, 47% of general counsel said they are more focused on service enhancement than on cost reduction, and 7% said they prioritize cost reduction. Where leadership has made that shift and the reporting has not, the questions that get answered are still the cost questions, because cost is what the data answers without preparation.

Dedicated tooling has spread less far than the practice itself. Thomson Reuters reports that 37% of departments have a dedicated legal BI tool, with 16% rating it valuable and 21% rating it underutilized, from the same 128 United States respondents. The figure counts dedicated legal BI products, and it excludes the reporting a department does inside its e-billing system or on the enterprise BI platform the company already licenses. Read with that scope attached, 37% shows how far one product category has spread, and it leaves open how much reporting the other departments produce with the systems they already own.

The field has begun to recognize the work in its own awards. The RLLB 2025 awards of LegalOps.com included a "Data-Driven Legal Department of the Year" category, which recognized three departments: PayPal, Phillips 66, and Total Quality Logistics (The Edge Room, September 2025). The published account of the judging says that "The strongest entries demonstrated business outcomes backed by data." An award measures nothing about the field as a whole. What the category and that sentence show is what the top rank is now judged on. An entry has to carry evidence of business outcomes, and the number of reports a department publishes is not evidence of that kind.

ACC has published a definition of what the advanced stage of this function looks like. In its Legal Operations Maturity Model 2.0 (2020), which calls the function Metrics & Analytics, department leaders at the advanced stage "directly access dashboards to filter data and answer questions", supported by a central data source and predictive analytics. That is ACC's definition of advanced, and the survey figures above indicate that most departments have not reached it. Current expectations reach further than the definition does, toward answers given on demand in plain language and toward measures that feed automated processes, and the sections that follow take each of those in turn.

## Business intelligence turns the department's operational data into decisions

The field and the analysts who cover the category define business intelligence in nearly the same terms. ACC defines Metrics & Analytics as "The system to collect, organize and use data to inform decision making and performance management". Forrester's Boris Evelson places BI inside what he calls the "data-to-decisions process", and wrote in April 2025 that "GenAI is not the end of BI". Both definitions describe a chain that ends in a judgment somebody makes, and BI in a legal department is best understood the same way, as the practice of turning the department's operational data into decisions.

The inputs are records the department already produces: matters, invoices and their line items, budgets, requests, contracts, time, outcomes, and outside counsel engagements. The outputs are measures, trends, exceptions, forecasts, and the questions a person can now ask and have answered. Dashboards deliver some of those outputs, and so do a monthly report, an alert, an answer typed into a chat pane, and a threshold that a workflow reads before it routes a request. Which surface carries the output matters less than whether the number behind it means the same thing wherever it appears.

## All three frameworks for legal operations name business intelligence as a function

A definition of that kind earns its place when it names something a department can own, staff, and fund. The three frameworks the field uses all do that. Each names business intelligence as a function of the department, with the software as one of its instruments.

The [legal operations intelligence platform](/why-spaarke/building-the-legal-operations-intelligence-platform), as the second article in this series defines it, is the department's capabilities across process, people, and technology, with technology as the tangible instantiation of the other two. BI has all three elements in it. Someone owns the definitions, someone asks the questions, tooling delivers the answers, and the answers change what the department does. Buying the tooling and settling none of the ownership leaves a department with one element of three, which is the usual reason a reporting project produces charts that nobody uses. ([What Is Legal Operations Intelligence?](/why-spaarke/what-is-legal-operations-intelligence) carries the category definition the series works from.)

CLOC lists Business Intelligence among its Core 12 functions, under the tagline "Make better decisions through data", in a framework last revised in April 2020. ACC's Maturity Model 2.0 calls the same function Metrics & Analytics. The LegalOps.com reference model, unveiled at the first RLLB conference in October 2023, lists "Performance Analysis" among its 15 practice areas, as the published diagram stood in August 2026; the definitions behind it are available to members. Three organizations built those frameworks for different purposes, and each of them treats the measurement of the department's own work as a standing function, with an owner who is accountable for it between reports.

Practice has moved on since the framework text was written, and the field's own programming shows where. Jeff Franke of LegalOps.com names "data and metrics" among the subjects its programming covers, alongside prompt engineering and agentic AI (CCBJ, August 2025). CLOC's Compass maturity tool, launched in May 2026, assesses Business Intelligence as one of its twelve modules, which puts the function on the same footing as financial management and vendor management when a department scores its own maturity. Compass is in beta for CLOC members and has published no aggregate findings, so it tells us what CLOC has chosen to measure rather than how departments score.

## A function that reports to the CEO needs answers on demand

Naming the function settles what it is called. What has changed is what the business asks the function to produce, and the change follows from where the legal team now sits. In its 2026 survey of chief legal officers, ACC describes legal expertise as "integrated into business planning during the inception of projects rather than at the point of crisis". The business now puts its questions to legal before the facts have settled, and it wants the answer in the meeting, while the following week's report arrives after the decision.

Three requirements follow from that position. The measures have to cover more ground than cost, which means service, risk, and value. They have to be usable without an analyst in the loop, so the answers arrive in plain language inside the tools people already work in. The answer also has to arrive on demand, produced and shared without days of manual preparation. [The UX That Legal IQ Requires](/why-spaarke/the-ux-that-legal-iq-requires) makes the case that an answer a reader can drill into, pivot, and act on is a different product from an answer that only prints.

Two kinds of evidence show that vendors are taking the third requirement seriously. The first comes from the legal market. In May 2026, around the CLOC Global Institute, legal vendors announced natural-language questions over spend, rate, and matter data. Wolters Kluwer's release of 5 May 2026 describes natural-language interrogation of invoices, and Legal.io's release of 11 May 2026 quotes its chief executive, Pieter Gunst: "In-house teams don't need another dashboard. They need answers." These are announcements and they carry no evidence of results, so we read them as evidence of what vendors believe their buyers now want and as nothing more than that.

The second kind of evidence comes from the general BI platforms, where the same interface arrived first. Databricks made AI/BI Genie generally available on 12 June 2025, Microsoft made Fabric data agents generally available on 18 March 2026, and Salesforce made Tableau Agent conversational analytics generally available on 5 May 2026.

A question asked in plain language draws its answer from governed measures that someone defined in advance, so the figure a person gets by typing a question is the figure the dashboard would have shown. These interfaces are, on our reading, a new way into the same definitions, and the department still has to agree what a matter, a request, and a cycle time mean before either the dashboard or the answer is worth quoting.

## The readers of legal's numbers now sit outside legal

The case for answering on demand rests on who is asking. A general counsel who reports to the chief executive and attends board meetings carries the department's numbers into rooms where nobody from legal operations is present, and those numbers have to survive the questions asked there without a follow-up email.

The readers outside legal do not yet see what the department contributes. In the Thomson Reuters Institute's 2026 State of the Corporate Law Department, published in March 2026 and drawn from more than 2,300 interviews with general counsel, 86% of general counsel said their department contributes significantly to organizational objectives, while 17% of C-suite respondents agreed. Thomson Reuters calls the difference a visibility gap. The two figures come from different samples and the C-suite sample is not stated, so the pair is not a like-for-like comparison. What it does report is that the executives legal answers to describe legal's contribution in very different terms from the ones legal uses, and a contribution that legal cannot show in the business's own numbers reads to them as an assertion.

Thomson Reuters advises general counsel to "institute metrics for success that translate to the rest of the business", and it suggests presenting legal spend as a percentage of revenue. The advice concerns translation, because the same underlying measure expressed as a share of revenue arrives in a denominator that finance already uses in every other budget conversation. [Breaking the Silo Between Legal, Finance, and the Business](/why-spaarke/breaking-the-silo) sets out the organizational form of the same problem.

The list of readers has grown past the chief executive and the board. Finance and procurement have scrutinized legal spend for a decade, and that scrutiny remains the most durable demand on the department's numbers. In the departments we work with, IT increasingly co-funds legal technology, most often at the larger end of the market, and co-funding gives the technology leader a claim on how the department models, secures, and reports its data. Both points are our own observation, drawn from the deployments we see, and neither is a survey finding.

The business units that submit requests are readers too, and their question is the simplest of the set, which is where a request stands and when it will come back. The other readers put harder versions of the same question to the same records: finance asks what a matter cost, the board asks what the exposure is, and planning asks what to expect next year. The audit committee belongs on the list as well, and we know of no published statistic on how much of its reporting draws on legal's operational data.

The chief legal officer also has more kinds of information to account for than the role once carried. ACC's 2026 survey finds that 64% of CLOs oversee compliance and 62% oversee the corporate secretary function. Those responsibilities sit inside the CLO's remit, so they widen what the office has to report on without adding a new reader, and the department answers each of them from the same operational records.

In an illustrative case, the head of a business unit asks whether the company should keep negotiating a liability cap that its counterparties resist, or accept the standard term and price the risk instead. The department answers from its own records. Over three years, 140 contracts carried the negotiated cap and 90 carried the standard term; the disputes that reached litigation split six to five between the two populations; and the negotiated cap added a median of 11 days to the cycle. The figures are illustrative, and the shape of the exchange is what matters. A business leader asked a commercial question, legal answered with evidence from its own operation, and the leader decided in the meeting. When legal cannot answer at that speed, the next commercial question goes somewhere else, and the matters that would have taught the department what to say never reach its records.

## A governed measure returns the same number every time

A number that leaves the department has to hold still. When the general counsel quotes a cycle time to the board and finance quotes a different one from the same records a week later, the discussion stops being about the operation and becomes about the data. [Legal AI Is Not Deterministic](/why-spaarke/probabilistic-vs-deterministic) drew the distinction this rests on: deterministic software computes the same answer from the same inputs on every run, generative AI predicts, and the most reliable patterns pair probabilistic surfacing with deterministic enforcement on the same record.

A BI measure is deterministic in that sense. It is defined once, governed by someone who owns the definition, computed the same way for every reader, and traceable to the records behind it. The governed measure calculates spend against budget, cycle time, and matter counts from the records, and when an assistant or an agent needs one of those numbers it calls the measure rather than producing a figure with a language model.

That property is why we treat BI as a capability co-equal with AI within the platform. Legal decision making turns on exact outputs. A settlement authority, a budget approval, a privilege call, and a regulatory deadline each have a right answer that does not vary between runs, and the same holds for spend against budget, cycle time, matter counts, and rate compliance. A probabilistic system cannot supply an output of that kind, and it does the work that tolerates variation well: classification, extraction, summarization, and drafting. The platform therefore carries both capabilities and ranks neither above the other. This is our position rather than a finding from the research, and it rests on the list of decisions above.

Support for the same architecture comes from outside the legal market. Forrester's Boris Evelson wrote in July 2026 that organizations should "treat semantic layers and context graphs not as analytics features but as enterprise infrastructure for data, analytics, and AI". The semantic layer is the enterprise BI term for the place where governed definitions live, and Forrester's advice is that the layer matters more as agents begin to consume it. Measuring AI has become part of the same job. In Axiom's 2026 In-House Legal AI Report (n=528 in-house legal leaders in six countries, fielded in March 2026 by InsightDynamo and published by Axiom, an alternative legal services provider), 83% of respondents said they cannot measure whether their AI spending is working. The figure reports what respondents said about their own measurement, so it describes a reporting gap and not a verdict on the spending itself. Nobody can state the return on AI spending without first stating the measure, which is the same governance problem one layer up.

Three caveats keep the claim honest. The first is vocabulary. "Deterministic" is the language of BI vendors rather than of the analysts who cover them. dbt Labs writes of systems that "handle the actual query generation deterministically", and Google Cloud listed "Deterministic advanced calculations" among the properties of Looker's semantic layer in May 2025. ThoughtSpot said in March 2026 that "This deterministic approach relies on our patented search tokens, not text-to-SQL powered by LLMs". Forrester uses the word in neither of the two posts we checked, from April 2025 and June 2026, and writes instead of governed context and consistency. The substance is shared even where the word is not.

The second caveat is that natural-language layers differ in how strictly they keep to governed measures. Some of them only call a measure that already exists. Microsoft's documentation for the standalone Copilot experience in Power BI, which is in preview while the report agent Copilot pane is generally available, says that it "uses the measures and other data fields in your semantic model (or even creates new DAX calculations)" (Microsoft Learn, page dated 6 July 2026 and revised 27 August 2026; Copilot overview page dated 24 August 2026 and revised 31 August 2026). A layer that can write a new calculation on request answers a different kind of question from a governed measure, and a department that cares about repeatability has to know which mode it is working in.

The third caveat is that the accuracy gap behind the argument is moving. dbt Labs ran its own benchmark in April 2026 on 11 questions with 20 runs each, and on a well-modeled project raw text-to-SQL scored 84.1% and 90.0% for two models, while the same questions answered through the semantic layer scored 100.0% and 98.2%. By the same vendor's measurement, text-to-SQL accuracy on the full question set rose from 32.7% in 2023 to 64.5% in 2026, so the gap has narrowed and will keep moving.

The durable part of that finding is the failure mode the authors describe: failure through the semantic layer appears as an error message, while failure with text-to-SQL appears as a plausible wrong answer. A legal department can act on an error message, and it cannot act on a number that is wrong and looks right, which is also the number that an audit trail will later have to explain.

The same principle is beginning to appear in legal products. Brightflag's page for its MCP connector, which shows an update date of 18 August 2026, says that answers "come from your actual Brightflag data, not estimates, not model-generated approximations". It is a vendor page with no usage figures attached, and it is the clearest statement we have found in the legal market that an AI workspace should call the governed number.

## Measures and actions share one entity model

A department can govern a measure only over something it has modeled. Cycle time on a request means nothing until the department has agreed what a request is, which events start and stop its clock, and where the record of it lives. The same holds for every measure named in the previous section. Each definition sits on top of an entity, and the entity has to exist in the model before anyone can write the definition down and apply it the same way twice.

The [entity and action model](/why-spaarke/legal-operations-ontology) that the third article in this series sets out supplies exactly that: matters, requests, invoices, firms, and contracts, together with the actions taken on them and the outcomes those actions produce. BI measures are defined over those same entities. When the measure for cycle time counts requests as the model defines them, "request" carries one meaning in the quarterly report, in the routing rule a workflow applies, and in the answer an assistant returns when somebody asks how long intake is taking. Define the measures separately from the operational records and an analyst spends the quarter reconciling two populations of requests, work that produces no decision.

The two structures do different jobs, and neither replaces the other. Business intelligence measures, compares, and surfaces the exception: the matter running over budget, the unit whose requests take twice as long as anyone else's, the firm whose effective rate moved between one year and the next. The entity and action model is where someone acts on that exception, and where the action taken and the outcome it produced are recorded against the same matter or request. Those records become the next period's inputs, so the measure that surfaced the exception also reports whether the response worked. Collapsing the two produces a warehouse nobody acts in and a workflow nobody can report on.

The enterprise data field has arrived at the same arrangement from its own direction. Microsoft's documentation for Fabric IQ states that "Semantic models and ontologies work together", and says that ontologies can be generated or aligned directly from semantic models so that terminology and KPIs stay consistent across reports, agents, and applications (Microsoft Learn, page dated 8 July 2026 and revised 31 August 2026; the ontology capability is in preview). The Apache Ossie specification, which entered the Apache incubator on 22 June 2026, describes itself as "an open specification for both semantic layer and ontology", and its core specification is published as a development draft rather than as a ratified standard. Forrester calls the semantic layer "the right starting point" for organizations that are not yet ready to build a knowledge graph (June 2026), and in August 2026 defined a context layer that "combines business semantics and governance of semantic layers with the ontological modeling of knowledge graphs". None of that was written about legal departments. The convergence still bears on a legal department's choice, because it shows semantic layers and ontologies being built to sit together, which means a department that builds one is not foreclosing the other.

The loop closes on itself. The records the department produces are the inputs, and the governed measures are computed from them. A measure surfaces something that calls for a decision, the decision produces an action, and the action and its outcome are recorded against the same entities, which makes them the inputs the next period measures. The measure feeds two consumers on the way round, a person who reads it and decides and an automated process that acts on it, and both arrive at the same action (Exhibit 1).

## A measure now has two consumers

The second of those consumers is the recent arrival. A person reads a measure, interprets it, and decides. An automated process uses the same measure as a trigger, a threshold, or a routing rule, and it acts without waiting for anyone to open the report. Both draw on one definition, and the second draws on it far more often than the first.

The legal market announced the pattern in May 2026, around the CLOC Global Institute. Wolters Kluwer's release of 5 May 2026 describes an Invoice Review Agent that "identifies non-compliant invoice line items and automatically implements adjustments", with the adjustments described as fully auditable and reversible. Checkbox's release of 11 May 2026 describes agent actions that create a populated matter and start the right workflow from an incoming request. Neither release reports a result. Both describe a class of product in which a rule that used to sit in a reviewer's head is written down, applied to every invoice or every request, and recorded.

One of the same announcements shows the measure and the automation feeding each other. Checkbox described matter status set from message activity, so that reporting separates "time spent by legal from time spent waiting on the business". The automation maintains the status, and the measure computed from that status answers a question the department could previously answer only by asking the lawyer who handled the file. Once the split exists, legal can argue about its cycle time with evidence and route on the same number.

The general BI platforms have carried the same pattern for longer, and their documentation dates it. Power BI data alerts can trigger Power Automate flows (Microsoft Learn, page dated 1 December 2025), and Fabric Activator rules fire on report visuals as well as on data streams (page dated 17 April 2026). Google released Looker's Agentic Workflows in preview on 29 July 2026, for Looker 26.08 and later: "When a metric crosses your defined threshold, the background agent does more than send a basic notification. It can automatically run a Key Driver Analysis (KDA) across the underlying data model", with the result delivered to Slack or to email. Forrester describes the general case in July 2026, agents that "can deliver alerts when thresholds are crossed, anomalies emerge, trends shift, or opportunities arise".

Gartner forecasts that by 2029, 60% of legal departments will use intake systems that use AI to capture every request and to answer one half of them without human intervention (Gartner, reported by Lawyers Weekly on 9 June 2026 and by SMBtech on 27 May 2026). That figure is a forecast, and we quote it here for the direction it describes rather than for its precision. An intake system that answers half the requests it receives is deciding which half, and it decides on a measure.

"Legal AI Is Not Deterministic" described the pattern that holds up under that load: probabilistic surfacing paired with deterministic enforcement on the same record. An agent can read an invoice and propose an adjustment, and the rule that decides whether the adjustment falls within the department's billing guidelines is a governed calculation applied the same way every time. While a measure only filled a chart, a disagreement about its definition cost a meeting. Once the measure triggers an action, a definition that two systems compute differently produces a wrong action, and the action stands on the record.

## Risk appears in the operational data before it appears as an event

The measures worth wiring to an action are usually the ones that carry risk, and risk shows up in the department's own records well before it shows up as a claim, a regulator's letter, or a write-off. ACC's 2026 survey of chief legal officers found that 74% provide proactive strategic counsel. Counsel of that kind depends on a view of the department's own matters, by type and by business unit, that someone can produce every quarter without commissioning a project to build it.

[The New Mandate for Legal Operations](/why-spaarke/managing-legal-operations) set out the proactive and the reactive halves of what a legal operations function is asked to do, and business intelligence is how the department does the proactive half. [From Reactive to Predictive](/why-spaarke/loi-maturity-model) puts the same progression in maturity terms. The patterns a quarterly view exposes are ordinary ones: the matter type that keeps recurring, the business unit that generates disputes out of proportion to its size, the contract terms that keep appearing in the matters that reached litigation, and the budget that starts drifting in month three.

An illustrative department with 200 open matters runs the view each quarter. One business unit produces two fifths of the employment disputes with one sixth of the company's headcount. Twelve matters show budget drift of more than 20% by month three. Two contract clauses appear in most of the disputes that reached litigation. None of those three findings required a new system to produce, and each of them required that the department code its matters consistently and attribute them to a business unit.

The response is four actions: a review with the unit's leadership, a reserve adjustment on the twelve drifting matters, an intake rule that routes that unit's requests for early legal review, and a change to the contract template. Each action is recorded against the same entities the measures were computed over, so the following quarter's view reports whether the concentration moved, whether drift fell, and whether the clauses stopped appearing in litigated matters. No published statistic counts the departments that run a view of this kind; we describe it from the deployments we see.

## Three decisions the department now makes on a measure

Each of these three decisions was once settled on the recollection of whoever had handled the most matters, and each is now settled on a number the department can produce on request. The figures in the three examples are illustrative.

### Outside counsel spend is priced on evidence

This is the established case, and the one most departments have already built. Spend analysis by firm, matter type, and phase answers which work costs what, and allocation analysis extends the question to which work belongs inside the department, which belongs at a firm, and which timekeeper level should carry it. Rate and staffing analysis tests whether the rates a firm quoted are the rates it billed, while budget-to-actual discipline catches the matters that are drifting while there is still time to intervene. Alternative fee arrangements priced from the department's own history of comparable matters turn a negotiating instinct into an evidence question. [The $20B Blind Spot: Why Legal Spend Is Still a Black Box](/why-spaarke/the-20b-blind-spot) sets out what the absence of that visibility costs a department.

Thomson Reuters records a split in who does the analysis: in its 2025 index, 63% of legal operations respondents said they use data analytics to identify cost savings, against 29% of general counsel respondents. The split says the analysis is a legal operations practice before it is a general counsel practice, so a measure that only the operations team can produce will not reach the panel conversations the general counsel has. As an illustrative case, a department compares three years of wage and hour defense work across its panel. Two firms handle most of it. The first bills a median of US$68,000 per matter with 40% of its hours at partner level; the second bills US$91,000 with 22% at partner level and resolves in two thirds of the time. The comparison names no winner, and it gives the department one question to put to both firms and a basis for pricing a fixed fee on the next ten matters of that type.

### Intake is where service data originates

Intake is the department's front door, and it is where service data begins. Request volume by business unit and by type, time to first response, cycle time divided between the time legal held the request and the time it sat with the business, the share resolved through self-service, and the route each request took are all recorded at intake or nowhere.

It is also the least measured area. Fewer than one in five departments routinely report cycle time in the Thomson Reuters 2025 index, and 28% of teams track time to resolution in the ACC and Everlaw survey. One reason may be structural. No association framework treats intake as a function of its own, and in ACC's maturity model intake and triage appear only as maturity markers inside Internal Resources Management and Technology Management. A practice that no framework names is a practice that nobody has been made accountable for measuring.

In an illustrative composite of the departments we see, a team instruments its front door for two quarters. Of 640 requests in a quarter, 38% are non-disclosure agreements and vendor contracts below a US$50,000 threshold, and their median cycle time is six days, four of which are spent waiting for the requester to answer a question. Two business units account for 44% of the volume. Once those numbers exist, the department can publish a self-service path for the lowest-risk band, set a routing rule that sends the two heaviest units' requests to a named reviewer, and report the cycle time split so that it can discuss the four waiting days with the business.

That last step is where intake measures and automation meet most directly. A routing rule is a measure with an action attached to it, and the Gartner forecast quoted above describes a market in which that arrangement becomes ordinary. The definition of the threshold then matters as much as the value of the threshold, because a request routed on a cycle time that two systems compute differently is a request routed at random.

### Request and matter data carries the case for headcount

Companies are asking legal departments to absorb the workload without adding people. Gartner, reported by Lawyers Weekly on 28 July 2026, found that 56% of legal departments anticipate a headcount freeze or reduction in 2026; the sample is not published. CLOC's 2026 State of the Industry release of March 2026, drawn from the 2025 Harbor Law Department Survey of 135 law departments, reports that 32% of departments expect lawyer headcount to increase, down from 42% a year earlier.

Under that constraint, the department builds its resource case from request and matter data or it does not build one at all. As an illustrative case, a department of fourteen lawyers records 2,400 requests in a year, up 18% on the prior year, with the commercial contracts group absorbing 60% of the growth while its median cycle time rises from five days to nine. The same records show that 30% of that group's volume falls below the value threshold at which a lawyer changes the outcome. The case that follows asks for one paralegal, a self-service path for the low band, and a date by which both are in place, and it carries the measures that will report next year whether the answer was the right one.

## Business intelligence needs consistent definitions, connected data, and a place to act

The three examples share a set of preconditions, and those preconditions are where most departments stall. Business intelligence stands on consistent definitions of a matter, a request, and an outcome; on data that reaches the measure without somebody assembling it from separate systems each time a reader asks for it; and on a place to act on what the measure shows.

ACC's maturity model names the first two as the hurdles departments hit. At the early stage it describes "Uneven integrity/cleanliness of data". At the intermediate stage it describes metrics "generated through manual synthesis of data from disparate systems", which is the stage at which a department can answer any question once and no question repeatedly. ACC's account of the Everlaw survey puts figures against the same barriers: lack of time or resources at 57% of teams, data scattered across systems at 50%, limited tools for automation at 43%, and difficulty aligning metrics with business priorities at 29% (ACC Corporate Counsel Now, July 2026).

Tooling is rarely the binding constraint. Figure 16 of the Thomson Reuters 2025 index reports that 21% of departments have a dedicated legal BI tool they rate as underutilized and 16% have one they rate as valuable. That comparison comes from the figure alone, and the inference is ours rather than Thomson Reuters': more of the dedicated tools already bought are going unused than are earning their place, which is what happens when a tool arrives ahead of the definitions and the data it needs. [The AI Readiness Gap in Legal Departments](/why-spaarke/the-ai-readiness-gap) treats data readiness as the shared precondition for business intelligence and for AI, because both fail in the same way when the records underneath them disagree with each other.

The other two preconditions are the subjects of the two articles before this one. [How to Build the Legal Operations Intelligence Platform](/why-spaarke/building-the-legal-operations-intelligence-platform) covers how a department assembles the capability across process, people, and technology. [The Legal Operations Intelligence Ontology](/why-spaarke/legal-operations-ontology) covers the entity and action model the measures are defined over and the place where the resulting action is recorded. Where those records physically sit is a separate question from whether anyone governs the measures over them. Spaarke provides both system of record capabilities and ontology architecture over the systems a department already runs, so business intelligence measures can be defined over records that Spaarke holds and over records that a third-party system holds.

The fifth article in this series takes up knowledge management, which supplies AI with the context it needs in the same way that business intelligence supplies it with the exact numbers. The two rest on the same foundation seen from two directions, and a department that has settled its definitions for one has done much of the work for the other.

The instruction that follows for a legal operations director is narrower than the argument. Govern the measure that is about to trigger an action, and govern it first. In most departments that measure sits at intake, because intake is the least measured area and the place where measurement and automation meet soonest. Write down what a request is, when its clock starts and stops, which system holds the record of it, and who owns the definition when the business asks for it to change. Then wire the rule.

A department that takes those steps in the other order finds out later and in public. An intake rule routes on a cycle time that two systems compute differently and escalates or holds a matter on that number, and six months on the action stands recorded against a figure that nobody can reproduce when somebody asks why it was taken. The platform holds the capability and the entity model gives the measure something to stand on; a department settles both before the first rule fires, or it settles them afterwards, in an audit.
