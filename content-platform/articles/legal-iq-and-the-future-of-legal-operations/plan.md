## Opener — paragraphs 1–2 (~180 words)

**Claim**: Every modern operational function has an intelligence metric. Legal does not.

**Evidence / move**:
- Lead with the comparison directly. Marketing has attribution and CAC/LTV. Sales has pipeline coverage and velocity. Finance has working-capital cycles, days-sales-outstanding, gross-margin trends. Each is measurable, improvable, and read at the board level.
- Then the contrast: legal has volume of matters, headcount, and a partner's gut.
- Land the gap without naming "Legal IQ" in paragraph 1. Let paragraph 2 introduce the term as the answer to the gap the reader has just recognized.

**Voice reference**: cadence of `the-20b-blind-spot` opener (specific observation → contrast → category claim).

## §1 — What we mean by Legal IQ (~250 words)

**Claim**: Legal IQ is the measurable, improvable property of a legal department that arises when its work, data, and inference run together.

**Structure**:
- One-sentence definition the reader can repeat.
- The clarifier: Legal IQ is not the IQ of any individual lawyer. It is the IQ of how the department operates. Two indistinguishable General Counsel can run departments at very different Legal IQ.
- Distinguish from related terms:
  - **Legal Operations Intelligence** (the category) — Legal IQ is the property; LOI is the operating model that produces it. Cite `what-is-legal-operations-intelligence` once, link.
  - **Productivity** — Productivity is a per-task efficiency. Legal IQ is a per-function operational property.
  - **Maturity** — Maturity is the journey; Legal IQ is the property at any point on the journey.
- Close the section: every metric the reader knows is the result of years of organizing data, capturing context, and applying inference to it. That sequence is what raises Legal IQ.

## §2 — Where Legal IQ shows up (~280 words)

**Claim**: A legal department running at high Legal IQ is recognizable in three concrete operational properties — visibility, decision quality, and agility — none of which requires explaining to a CFO what AI is doing.

**Structure**:
- **Visibility**: Spend, performance, status, and outcomes are continuous, not quarterly. The CFO conversation shifts from "why did legal spend so much?" to forecast, justify, optimize. (Cross-reference `the-20b-blind-spot` argument.)
- **Decision quality**: Counsel selection, settlement strategy, vendor mix, and panel-firm allocation become comparisons against the department's own matter history rather than gut. Rationale is preserved, not just decisions.
- **Agility**: Cycle time on matters drops because intake is structured, handoffs preserve context, and routine work routes itself. The function compounds capacity rather than burning headcount on operational friction.

**Close section**: each of these is operational, not aspirational. The reader can audit their own department against the three.

## §3 — How you raise it (~280 words)

**Claim**: Legal IQ rises in a specific order — data first, then memory, then inference. Skipping ahead produces plausible bad output that gets embedded into decisions before anyone notices. (Echoes `the-ai-readiness-gap` argument; cite, do not restate.)

**Structure**:
- **Data**: Unified, structured, complete. Matter records, document records, billing records, communication records — all carrying the same matter ID. Without this, the rest is ungrounded.
- **Memory**: Context, rationale, prior decisions retained in machine-readable form. Why a vendor was chosen, which OCG terms were hard-won, what worked in similar matters. (Cross-reference `institutional-knowledge`.)
- **Inference**: AI applied to the grounded, contextualized record. AI-directed, human-controlled. Foundry IQ for grounding and operational memory; Microsoft Agent Framework for orchestration and execution; Power Platform + M365 as the user-experience layer — name them once, link to architecture pieces.

**Close section**: cite `the-iq-stack` as the long-form architecture; this piece's job is the why-of-the-sequence, not the architectural detail. One paragraph on the practical implication: the return on AI investment in legal is gated by the data/memory work that precedes it.

## §4 — Why now (~250 words)

**Claim**: Three forces converge in 2026 that make Legal IQ a near-term operational concern, not a five-year vision.

**Structure**:
- **AI grounded in operational data is now operationally usable.** Generic AI gives industry ranges; grounded AI gives department-specific answers. The departments that have organized their operational data become AI-ready; everyone else stays at the productivity-tool tier.
- **In-house legal has scaled.** US in-house counsel population grew 87% from 2008 to 2024 (ACC, citing US BLS, 2025). Functions of that scale acquire intelligence metrics. They have to.
- **Vendor consolidation has filtered the market.** Buyers in 2026 are asking architectural questions — where the AI runs, what grounds it, what it costs — rather than feature questions. The buying conversation already aligns with a Legal IQ frame.

**Close section**: a department that organizes around legal IQ in 2026 compounds an advantage. A department that waits accumulates technical debt and falls behind, the way mid-2010s sales organizations that delayed CRM adoption fell behind.

## §5 — A board-level metric, eventually (~180 words)

**Claim**: Legal IQ becomes a board-level metric. Not this quarter, but on a five-year horizon — and the departments that organize around it now will be the ones with credible numbers when the board asks.

**Structure**:
- One paragraph: project the trajectory. CFOs and CEOs already ask about marketing attribution, sales pipeline coverage, finance working capital. Legal joins that list when there is a credible metric to ask about. The metric is Legal IQ, or its successor by another name; the structural property is what matters.
- One paragraph: the immediate practical move. Audit the department against the three operational properties in §2. Each is a known-deficit lever. None of this requires a five-year transformation.
